The history behind the picture

Valentine-Black: An Island in Monochrome

A street runs away between the shopfronts. Telephone poles climb into a pale sky. Along the bottom, a few carefully formed capitals tell us where we are: Water Street, Summerside.

Card 2038 has a lovely restraint. Its monochrome picture, white border and small hand-lettered-looking caption let the street do the talking. Gather its companions and that same feeling carries us out along the coast, across bridges and into the Island’s fields.

The main album follows both the look of 2038 and its printed Carter & Co. back. Dark lettering sits on pale foregrounds; several companions use white lettering where the photograph is darker. Each view appears once, with other copies linked below. The Valentine number is included for collecting, while the subjects determine the order.

The Valentine–Carter album

A local name on an international postcard

The reverse of 2038 supplies a useful piece of the story: “Published by Valentine-Black Co., Ltd., for Carter & Co., Ltd.” It also says the card was printed in Great Britain. Between “POST” and “CARD” sit the familiar twin globes of Valentine & Sons, promising a name known throughout the world.

There is a satisfying journey inside that little imprint. An Island subject, a local firm’s name, a Canadian publisher and British production all meet in one inexpensive souvenir. Carter’s role is printed on the object itself, giving this group a firmer connection than resemblance alone.

Valentine’s Canadian business had become independent of the Scottish parent in 1909. In 1926 it divided into the Toronto-based Valentine Black and the Winnipeg-based Valentine Edy. The older twin-globe identity continued to appear on cards bearing the successor company’s name. The Toronto Postcard Club’s history is a useful guide through those changes. Valentine’s Canadian history

The Carter group was in circulation by 1929: the Summerside memorial copy 0005 was sent that August, and the golf-links card 4351 also travelled in August 1929. The small 28-1 code is another feature to watch when comparing editions. Its precise meaning can await a printer’s record; the postmarks already give us a secure foothold in the late 1920s.

An Island seen at an unhurried pace

The variety is part of the appeal. Water Street gives us commercial life, with shop signs and a long perspective down the road. At Stanley Bridge, a figure and a vehicle occupy a much quieter crossing. The court house at Georgetown and the hospitals in Charlottetown and Summerside stand among the scenery as buildings a community might wish to show the world.

Elsewhere the pictures breathe. Boats rest at Tracadie. Trees lean above the water at Midgell River and Thompson Mills. The Marshfield potato field has the lovely geometry of planted rows, framed by two trees; the farm near Charlottetown offers a different rhythm, with the gathered crop spread in small heaps across the foreground. These are photographs worth lingering over before reading their numbers.

The coast receives several treatments. At East Point, small figures stand against the open water. Canoe Cove brings people down among the rocks. Bay View puts the eroded shore close to the camera, while Cavendish opens into a long sweep of beach. The subdued printing makes texture unusually prominent: stone, timber, foliage, sand and the folds of the land.

Six close companions

The collection also holds the six consecutive views numbered 113,595–113,600. Their monochrome fronts and capital-letter captions make them natural neighbours. Their backs carry the Valentine-Black Toronto imprint and a 27-2 code, forming a distinct little group alongside the Carter album. Here too, the pictures are arranged alphabetically.

These six move between the capital and the shore: the provincial buildings, St. Dunstan’s, Heartz Memorial Hall, the harbour’s sails and two dramatic rock formations. The small figure beneath the Kildare cliff gives the stone its scale. Beside the larger Carter album, they help us see how a publisher could give quite different subjects a recognisable family appearance.

The photographic authorship remains an inviting subject for another chapter. For this album, the printed publisher and Carter imprints give us the firm connections. The captions supply the places, the numbers help reunite scattered copies, and the photographs provide the pleasure.
