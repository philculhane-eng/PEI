The history behind the picture

Silver at the Old Homestead

“Will arrived last night for Xmas & a visit afterward.” On a postcard addressed to Miss Patie Inman at Central Bedeque, Bertha had news of a visitor and an invitation: come down while he is here. The Tryon postmark is Christmas Day, December 25, 1907.

On the other side, the trees are in leaf. An unpaved approach leads towards a light-coloured house, with a small figure in a hat standing before it. The gable end faces the camera. Mature trees frame the building, and a fence runs away through the grounds. Across the lower part of the photograph, a silver-coloured inscription gives the scene its name:

THE OLD HOMESTEAD
W. W. LORD
TRYON, PRINCE EDWARD ISLAND

The lettering is what first caught my attention. This is a real photo postcard, with the title and its rectangular border applied over the photographic image. In my own collecting, I have never encountered another Prince Edward Island real photo postcard with this silver-stamped overlay. Someone took particular care over the way this family home was presented.

The likely building is the Lord brick house illustrated in Remember Yesterday: A History of North Tryon. Its photograph, credited to the Evelyn Lord Collection, shows the house beside the water. The two views appear to have been taken from positions roughly a quarter-turn apart: the postcard looks towards a gable end, while the history photograph shows the broad roof slope and an adjoining wing. That different angle helps explain why they do not immediately look alike. The pale wall in the postcard may be painted brick. The history’s photograph, p. 211.

The history supplies a later chapter in the building’s life. Charles Humphrey Lord and his wife, Mary Hall MacQuarrie, moved from Bedeque to North Tryon on October 2, 1912, purchasing Robert Lord’s house on the east side of the Tryon River. Elsewhere, it identifies Robert Forrest Lord as a resident of the Lord Brick House and an operator of Gouldrup’s Grist Mill.

In 1906, Past and Present of Prince Edward Island described Robert F. Lord as the owner of 340 acres of improved farmland and the operator of a grist and saw mill. The Lords were a prosperous farming and milling family. Robert’s biography, printed pp. 698–699.

The family details point to Bertha Leard, who married Richard Lathley “Rich” Lord in 1889. “Will” was likely her father-in-law, William Warren Lord (1833–1928), the Tryon farmer also known as Warren. He was seventy-four that Christmas. Bertha was telling Patie that he had arrived for a holiday visit and hoping she would join them. The Lord family account, printed p. 207.

Dear Patie, How are you and when are you coming down. Will arrived last night for Xmas & a visit afterward. Wish you could come down while he is here. Hope you will have a Merry Xmas and Happy New Year! Bertha

Patie was almost certainly Patience Inman, the Bedeque schoolteacher. The 1901 census records her as the unmarried daughter of Nelson and Martha Inman, living with her parents in Lot 26. Born on September 11, 1872, she was twenty-eight at the census and thirty-five when Bertha sent this Christmas invitation. Bertha was thirty-seven. Inman was Patience’s birth surname. Patience’s 1901 census record.

The Guardian places her in Central Bedeque before the postcard was sent. An October 1904 wedding report names “Miss Patience Inman, Central Bedeque” as a cousin attending the bride. In March 1910, it reports her return to Bedeque after a five-week visit in Charlottetown. The Guardian, October 3, 1904, p. 5; March 15, 1910, p. 7.

Patience was still part of the Bedeque community in 1932. That May, the Guardian reported that Miss Helen Clark of Summerside had spent Sunday in Centreville Bedeque visiting her cousin, Miss Patience Inman. Twenty-five years after Bertha’s invitation, the local news was still recording visits among Patience’s family and friends. The Guardian, May 28, 1932, p. 6.

Earlier in 1907, another Bertha in the Lord family had died. Anna Bertha Dobson Lord was Robert Forrest Lord’s wife, distinct from Rich’s wife, Bertha Leard. The Guardian reported her death on June 25, saying that she had died the previous morning. About two and a half weeks earlier she had fallen and broken her leg. According to the newspaper, pleurisy and heart complications followed. She had seemed to be recovering before suddenly becoming worse. She left three sons and four daughters. The Guardian, June 25, 1907, p. 8.

A fuller obituary, reprinted from the Wesleyan in August, remembered her as a woman of judgment, intellect and wide reading. It described her importance to her family and her work alongside her husband in the adult Bible class. A daughter was with her when she died. The Guardian, August 29, 1907, p. 5.

The postcard may have been made as a memorial keepsake after Anna Bertha’s death. A photograph of the old family home, finished with silver lettering, would have been a fitting remembrance to share with relatives. Her death in June and the card’s Christmas posting fall six months apart. The silver finish could also have marked a family occasion or simply expressed pride in the homestead.

Seventeen years later, William Warren Lord celebrated his ninety-first birthday at Rich and Bertha’s home. Children, grandchildren and great-grandchildren gathered beneath trees beside the river where Warren had fished as a boy. The Guardian, August 2, 1924, p. 3.
