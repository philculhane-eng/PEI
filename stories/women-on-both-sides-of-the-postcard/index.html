The history behind the picture

Women on Both Sides of the Postcard

Florence feeds the chickens. Agatha explains the shipping season. Effie is having a marvellous holiday. Turn an Island postcard over, and the women begin to speak.

Look through enough Island postcards and a familiar procession comes into view: fishermen beside their boats, farmers with horses, soldiers in uniform, gentlemen assembled for some important occasion. Then a woman catches your eye. She is standing in an orchard, sitting at a desk, or looking straight at the camera from beneath an extraordinary hat. Sometimes she has been there all along, small enough in the picture that we have passed her by.

There is another place to look. Across the backs, and sometimes around the edges of the pictures themselves, women describe the Island in their own words. They explain the harvest, arrange journeys, send family news, answer offers of work and tease the people they love. A postcard album becomes a much fuller account of Island life when we read it from both sides.

A face, and sometimes a name

The two women photographed by A. E. Lyon and the pair who visited Cook Studio have survived with remarkable clarity. We can study their clothes, their expressions and the ways they sit together. Both cards identify a Charlottetown studio. The women’s own names are still a question. It is an odd little imbalance: the business that made the portrait is easier to recover than the people who made it worth taking.

Alice Maude Kinch gave posterity a tremendous helping hand. Across her portrait she wrote, “Yours truly, Alice Maude Kinch.” Those words let us begin following a life through records, places and family connections. There is something especially pleasing about meeting her first in her own handwriting. She chose how to introduce herself.

Portrait postcards could travel between people who already knew every face. A name was unnecessary to the recipient. A century later, that small omission can be the largest obstacle in the picture. Alice’s generous signature makes her feel wonderfully close.

The work inside the view

Florence’s chicken-feeding photograph is full of movement: birds crowd the foreground while she stands with the feed container beside a gate. The handwritten “Flos.” brings a familiar name into a scene of everyday work at Stone Cottage. Elsewhere in the family correspondence, Florence is planning travel, organising wedding arrangements and keeping Basil supplied with news. The portrait of her life grows through the combination.

In Louson’s The Apple Harvest, women appear among the trees, ladder, wagon and horses. The title promises a harvest scene, and the photograph gives us a gathering of people within it. Clothing and equipment share the frame; work and a moment posed for the camera sit together naturally.

The woman behind the telephone is one of the collection’s most intriguing people. Papers spread across her desk, and an Intercolonial Railway map hangs on the wall. She looks directly towards the camera. Her name, occupation and the room’s location remain to be established. For now, the photograph gives us a woman occupying the centre of a space filled with the tools of communication.

Other kinds of work are easier to miss because the camera stayed outside. The Island fisheries history preserved by Library and Archives Canada describes girls extracting tail and claw meat at the tables of nineteenth-century lobster canneries. That shore work belonged to the same industry as the boats so often celebrated on postcards. A factory exterior gives us a building; the history helps us imagine the labour that filled it. Agatha knows what the picture means

Montague’s wharf is crowded with horses, wagons and vessels on card 4598. The station stands in the background. It is a splendid picture of a busy waterfront, made more valuable by the woman who sent it to Gladys Dodge in New Hampshire.

“This is a picture of what takes place here every fall. Farmers loading vessels.”

Agatha supplies the rhythm of the place. The activity belongs to an annual round, something familiar enough for her to explain in two short sentences. She then turns to the cold weather and sends her love. The card reached Alton in December 1906.

Her message is a small piece of interpretation written while that waterfront was still a working part of daily life. We can spend hours identifying buildings and counting wagons. Agatha tells us what the scene meant to someone who knew it.

“It is fine to travel”

Effie’s enthusiasm runs right around her view of Dalvay. Writing to Fannie in Massachusetts in August 1901, she announces that they are in Charlottetown and have had a lovely trip. It is market day, and they are going to “buy out the market.” Then comes a sentence that needs very little help from a historian:

“It is fine to travel.”

The picture and the message take us to two places at once. Dalvay is the view she chose to send; Charlottetown is where she says she is spending the day. Her delight lies in the journey, the shopping and the wish that Fannie could share it. Effie’s Dalvay postcard has its own story.

Twenty-one years later another holiday voice reaches us from Summerside. Annie sends a Queen Hotel postcard to Miss Echo Morton in Wenatchee, Washington:

“Am over in Summerside P.E.I. Am having a lovely time. Will write when I come home. Annie.”

There is a lovely economy to that promise. The postcard can carry the essentials while the holiday is still happening. The fuller account will follow. Even the busiest correspondent sometimes deserves a day off.

The three women in this cyanotype seem a fitting company for those holiday messages. Their hats and long skirts belong to another era; the pleasure of sitting beside the water is entirely familiar. A photograph preserves the occasion even when the names and conversation have slipped away.

The news that mattered at home

A postcard could fit a surprising amount of care into a very small space. Blanche sent the photograph of Constance and a horse from Souris East in June 1909. The horse is enormous beside the little girl. The back answers the question a family member might ask first:

“Constance is quite well, and as happy as can be. We are taking good care of her.”

Picture and message work together beautifully. We get to see Constance, while Blanche tells the recipient how she is doing. The photograph has become part of caring for a child across a distance.

The Alberton greeting sent to Mrs. R. S. Whidden carries a more sombre bundle of news. Mrs. Hooper was buried on Wednesday; Aunt Martha is very poorly; love is sent to everyone. Underneath comes another piece of business: “I will accept that work.” The funeral, illness, affection and answer about work all fit on one side of a souvenir card. The writer expected Mrs. Whidden to understand the people and circumstances immediately. We arrive much later, trying to reassemble the circle of acquaintances. Read the fuller story of the Whidden card.

The Haslam–Colclough cards let us follow a family for much longer. Florence’s May 1914 message to Basil is densely packed with wedding preparations and travel advice. He should book to Breadalbane because the road to Emerald is in poor condition. Clara, Florence’s sister, thanks him elsewhere for a “letter containing snaps.” Their exchanges preserve the practical arrangements through which a family stayed close: invitations, trains, roads, parcels and photographs. Florence, Clara and Basil’s family story brings those cards together.

Whose names reach the caption?

The group portrait on card 3279 was taken at Pictou in July 1923. Chief John Sark gives it a direct Island connection. Four women stand prominently in the group, their clothing and presence central to the photograph, while the printed caption gathers them into “their wives and children.”

The Nova Scotia Museum’s record helps restore individual names: it identifies Mrs. John Sark, Agnes Francis, Louisa Julian and Elizabeth Paul among the women, and Martha Francis among the children. Even that fuller record preserves one woman under her husband’s name. Reading the postcard alongside the museum description lets more of the people come into focus. The museum’s record of this photograph

A name can be a remarkably small addition to a caption, and a remarkably large addition to a person’s place in history. The same care that helps us find Alice in a family record also helps us look more attentively at a group whose members were presented to postcard buyers collectively.

When the news became war

On the back of a quiet Bonshaw river view, Annie writes to her sisters about men whose journey to Petawawa has been interrupted. Her message is headed “Sunday eve”; the Charlottetown postmark is 3 August 1914. She says the men were turned back and arrived home “on account of war.” It is a glimpse of uncertainty reaching a household before Britain’s declaration on 4 August.

Her short account sits beside the much more public photographs of the Island artillerymen leaving Charlottetown. The waterfront photograph shows a departure; Annie’s message records news moving through a family.

Women’s wartime service also appears in a building’s name. The Rena McLean Memorial Hospital commemorated the Island nurse who joined the Canadian Army Medical Corps in 1914 and died when the hospital ship Llandovery Castle was torpedoed on 27 June 1918. All fourteen nursing sisters aboard were lost. Her name on a postcard of the hospital carries a working life in nursing into the Island’s remembrance landscape. Rena McLean and the hospital

At Summerside, two women stand beside the memorial and its wreaths. Their personal connections to the names remain unknown. In Charlottetown, a woman’s part in the unveiling is recorded: Rena’s mother, Matilda McLean, unveiled the Soldiers’ Monument on 16 July 1925. The public history of remembrance includes women’s service, grief and participation as well as the figures cast in bronze. The Charlottetown unveiling

There is more Island here

The women on these cards are doing many different things. Florence feeds chickens and makes wedding arrangements. Agatha explains the wharf. Effie wants to share the pleasure of a journey. Blanche reassures a family member. Clara enjoys receiving photographs. Other women sit for portraits, spend time together beside the water or stand beside a memorial.

Their messages also change the way we look at the pictures. A busy harbour becomes an annual harvest ritual. A hotel becomes a holiday in progress. A quiet river scene becomes a place to write about a world beginning to change.

Perhaps that is the best reason to keep turning these postcards over. There may be a familiar name, a joke, some urgent arrangement, or a woman who has been waiting a hundred years for someone to listen.

A note on the messages and the search

Quotations were checked against the postcard scans. Line breaks have been joined, and punctuation lightly regularised for reading. The short extracts preserve the writers’ words. Alice’s portrait is still uncatalogued; its image opens her existing story. Every numbered image opens the full postcard record.

A preliminary random sample of 24 cards with recorded postmarks found nine apparent female-name signatures, three male-name signatures and two joint sign-offs; ten were blank, unsigned, initials-only or too uncertain to classify. Those are exploratory readings of names, including one tentative female-name reading, rather than verified identities or a census of the collection’s writers. The sample gives us a promising reason to keep reading. This story follows the messages and people we can actually show.

The photograph descriptions come from the cards themselves. The linked fisheries history, Nova Scotia Museum record and Veterans Affairs memorial histories supply the wider context. The established Alice, Whidden and Haslam–Colclough stories contain the fuller family research.
