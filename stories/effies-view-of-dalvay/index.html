The history behind the picture

“Here Is a Pretty View for You”: Dalvay in 1901

Dalvay is one of my favourite places on the Island to sit and watch the world go by. A visitor named Effie sent this view to Massachusetts in 1901. She had rather more ambitious plans for her afternoon: “This is market day and we are going to buy out the market.” I’d love to know what Effie actually bought.

Dear Fannie, We are now at Charlottetown and oh, we have had such a lovely trip. This is market day and we are going to buy out the market. You would enjoy this trip so much. It is fine to travel and here is a pretty view for you. Effie.

Effie’s two places are easy to mix up. The picture is of the north shore. She was writing in Charlottetown, where Friday, August 23, 1901, was indeed a market day. The city still had its old wooden market; the stone building in the Wonderland postcards had yet to be built. Effie doesn’t say that she stayed at Dalvay. In 1901 you couldn’t simply have booked a room there.

When Dalvay was somebody’s house

Alexander MacDonald, a Scots-born oil businessman who made his home in Cincinnati, came to the Island on holiday with his family. They stayed at the Acadian Hotel at Tracadie. MacDonald liked the north shore well enough to buy land and build a summer house of his own. Parks Canada dates it to 1896. When Effie posted her card, the house with its great gables had been part of that shoreline for barely five years.

Look beyond the main house. There’s a long, low building to the left and a cluster of light roofs to the right. A private summer place on this scale meant year-round work for somebody. The hotel’s history remembers a caretaker, a gardener, two laundresses and two men to look after the horses and stable, besides the household staff. At the end of the season the family held a dance for local people and hired a violinist. I can’t assign a use to each little building in the print, but I like that the card gives the working parts of the place room in the picture. Dalvay’s account of the household.

There was even another Dalvay. On card 2285, somebody photographed an elaborate pair of gates and wrote “Back Entrance into Dalvay.” An easy enough mistake for an Island collector, except that the note on the other edge identifies Alexander McDonald’s estate in Clifton, and the cancellation is Cincinnati. The family used the name at both homes. When a sender leaves us a building name, an address and a postmark, it pays to read all three.

A postcard too big for the album

This early lithograph is one of the oversized cards, closer to four by six inches than to the roughly three-and-a-half by five that collectors could slip into the albums I’ve come across. I often find examples with an edge trimmed to make them fit. This one survived unclipped. Here that matters for more than condition: trim the top to tidy the edges and the first thing you lose is Effie’s signature. The reverse says “Private Post Card.” Fannie’s address in Lawrence, Massachusetts, takes pride of place on that side; the whole conversation had to fit around the picture.

This is the collection’s earliest Island-postmarked view of Prince Edward Island. The distinction is worth making. Islanders had been sending postal cards for decades. There are even earlier cards with illustrations: this 1898 Charlottetown card advertises the Independent Order of Foresters with a drawing of its building in Toronto. What Effie had to send in 1901 was an Island scene. The great postcard vogue of the early 1900s was still ahead of her. The University of Alberta’s brief history of the postcard.

The previous earliest view was 2505, Victoria Row, mailed in Charlottetown on December 20, 1901. Its sender wished the recipient a happy Christmas and New Year and reported good sleighing and skating. Put those two little messages together and you have two errands in the same town, four months apart: summer shopping to do, and Christmas wishes to get into the mail.

Coming back to the water

I think the view on 4587 looks across Dalvay Lake. The strip of water, the house and the little boats make sense from that side. Pinning down the exact spot from the shore today is another matter. The curved dining room that now looks out over the lake dates to an expansion in 1999–2000. And where the early print shows a group of buildings, the later postcards let us see how differently each photographer framed the same shore.

Once Dalvay became a hotel, the postcard subjects changed with it. On 0756, the caption gives the house two jobs: hotel and park administration offices. 0762 takes us through the door, where a guest sits beside the fire and the staircase rises to the right. The aerial view on 2923 is the one I’d take along to compare shorelines. And on 0769, the water that gave Effie’s picture its foreground has become part of the holiday: there’s a red canoe right in front of the house.

My favourite companion is the 1932 card. Turn it over and the writer, sending it to Belmont, Massachusetts, hopes the people at home are having “as wonderful a vacation as we are enjoying here.” Thirty-one years separate the two Charlottetown postmarks. Two different people chose a view across the same lake, sent it to Massachusetts, and found themselves saying much the same thing. Effie put it best: “You would enjoy this trip so much.”
