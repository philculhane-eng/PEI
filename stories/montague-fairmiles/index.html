The history behind the picture

Two Fairmiles and a Warehouse on the Montague Waterfront

When I first wrote about the card, I began with these words.

This postcard was mailed from Montague in 1947. I had questions...here, I hope, are some answers.

One correction from the card itself. Montague is where the boats were photographed. The legible postmark is Charlottetown, August 25, 1947, at 10 a.m. Exactly where the senders put it into the mail is a different question. They addressed it to Ray in Roslindale, Massachusetts. They tell him they’ve been having fun with the animals. They say nothing about the boats. For those, we have to turn it over.

The nearest boat rescued 78 people from torpedoed merchant ships, and the warehouse behind her shipped Island potatoes to Cuba. The clue to the first story is a white number on a dark hull. The clue to the second is a merchant’s name up on a building. To find both of them in one quiet view of Montague is what made me want to know more.

What seventy-eight people meant aboard Q083

Q083, in the foreground, was a Royal Canadian Navy Fairmile motor launch built at Weymouth, Nova Scotia, and taken into naval service in May 1942. On September 7 of that year the German submarine U-517 attacked convoy QS-33 in the Gulf of St. Lawrence. Three merchant ships went down, the Greek Mount Pindus and Mount Taygetus and the Canadian Oakton. The last of these was carrying coal from Sandusky, Ohio, to Corner Brook, Newfoundland. An attack on Atlantic shipping could reach a cargo that had started on Lake Erie.

When Q083 came into the naval station at Gaspé, she had 78 survivors aboard. A wartime signal recorded the number reaching port and reported three minor injuries treated in hospital. Naval historian Marc Milner identifies the launch and recounts how the survivors went on by rail to Montreal. Go back to the photograph. Follow the hull from its number to the deckhouse and imagine finding room on a boat about 112 feet long for those men in addition to the crew who had picked them up. The little Montague print gives an otherwise enormous number a scale you can see. Marc Milner on the attack on QS-33

Three sailors from Q064

Read the second number and another Weymouth-built launch comes into view. In March 1943 Q064 went to the aid of the crew of a Catalina flying boat that had come down offshore and was burning. The Winnipeg Evening Tribune carried a report on April 1. It names Leading Seaman Norman Kean and Able Seamen Harry Dean and Cyril French. Their commanding officer, Lieutenant Norman Williams, told the reporter that the aircraft came down about a mile offshore while the launch was tied up. His men had her ready to sail within moments. The danger was the fuel burning on the water between the wooden launch and the two unconscious airmen. Kean, Dean and French took the dinghy through it. Williams recalled that one airman was floating within inches of the flames. The sailors brought both men back alive. An ambulance met the boat ashore. Williams later heard that one man would live. The report follows both men as far as the rescue and gives no later word about the other. The clipping has photographs of Williams and of Dean and French together on the bridge of their launch. It is an important distinction in a story about the capabilities of a wooden naval launch. Her own small boat could get men to a place it was dangerous to take the launch herself. The April 1 Tribune clipping, with photographs

A crew-history file assembled by For Posterity’s Sake preserves a less heroic memory of working in a wooden boat. George Crowell recalled Q064 and Q080 lying alongside one another at Rimouski when the wind got up. Their overhanging upper works caught together. In his account Q064 went to Pictou for a temporary canvas patch before going on to Weymouth for repairs. That story comes through a crew recollection. The dated newspaper report is the source for the dinghy rescue. Side by side they give a better sense of the life of a Fairmile than either adventure alone.

How two launches came to Montague

Commander Fraser McKee followed the former naval boats into civilian shipping registers. Lorne Johnston of Montague appears against Q083, renamed Lazy Mariner. Wendell Graham of Montague appears against Q064, renamed Six-Four. The entries put both boats on the Charlottetown register in early 1947 and give a price of $3,000 for each. Six-Four kept her old number in a form a civilian owner could paint on a nameboard. The photograph still lets us read the naval numbers. The owners’ names and the civilian names come from the registers. McKee’s entries for Q064 and Q083

The two boats’ paper trails part company after that. McKee found Six-Four under a Nova Scotia owner in 1948 and recorded her loss off Sydney on December 7, 1952. He gives November 20, 1950, as the date Lazy Mariner’s registration closed. One entry records a foundering, the other an administrative act. Registration in the same port, ownership by two Montague men and a card in the mail by August 1947 make a postwar photograph persuasive. The exact day the camera came down to the wharf is still missing.

The trade behind the sign

At the back right is the warehouse George A. Thompson and William Leith Poole built around 1912, behind their Main Street store. The heritage record describes potatoes, carrots, grain and timber going out to Cuba, with fertilizer, coal, hardware and building supplies coming in. Loading a schooner began some distance from the wharf. For potatoes alone there were farmers, haulers and barrel makers between the field and the ship’s hold.

There’s a witness to one part of that work in the collection. On 4598, horses and wagons stand beside vessels at Montague. In December 1906 Agatha sent the picture to a friend in New Hampshire. Farmers were loading vessels, she explained, and they did it every fall. That card had already travelled to New Hampshire several years before the date assigned to Poole & Thompson’s warehouse. It gives us the trade into which the building fitted. Agatha left the ships’ destinations and the contents of those loads to our curiosity. Agatha’s waterfront card and message

The figures in the heritage record explain how the warehouse could matter to people who never went to sea. A nearby cooperage could make as many as 500 barrels in a day. The business at one point shipped 18,000 barrels of potatoes to Cuba. In 1929 fire destroyed the Main Street store and retail sales moved into the warehouse. By the time our postcard reached Ray, this building had served both the export trade and Islanders who came in to shop. The register also traces its later use as Beck’s furniture business after its purchase in 1990 and identifies it as the last surviving former warehouse on this waterfront. The heritage record for 18 Water Street

A final look along the bank is worthwhile. Another Montague card shows a line of boxcars behind schooner masts. In another, the steamer Enterprise hides much of the station. They make a little waterfront album with this one. By the late 1940s somebody with a camera could find two surplus naval launches tied up in the same town where farmers had brought their autumn loads. The two white hull numbers and those partly hidden letters on the warehouse were just enough to let us read the view again. The station and the working waterfront
