The history behind the picture

The DNA of P.E.I. Postcards, Part One: Pugh’s 524 Series

One photograph could have several lives. This first instalment follows twenty-two numbered Charlottetown views through Pugh’s colour postcards, looking for the photographic images beneath the ink.

Look at the hats in the Empire Day crowd. In one postcard the scene is a photograph in shades of brown: people gathered around a monument, bare branches against the buildings beyond. In the other, the roofs have turned red, the grass green and the sky blue. But the hats have not moved. Neither have the people wearing them.

That is where this investigation begins. A postcard can look quite different from another and still preserve precisely the same moment. Cropping changes its boundaries. Colour changes its mood. A fresh caption changes the words by which we find it. Beneath those alterations, the photograph retains details that allow us to recognize it again.

Those inherited details are the “DNA” of these postcards. Following them takes us beyond identifying a building or a street. We begin to see how a photograph travelled: through a studio, into a publisher’s catalogue, onto another postcard, and sometimes into a book. This series will follow those journeys. The first case is a particularly revealing group of Charlottetown views bearing Pugh’s series number 524.

A photograph, a postcard, a business

A real photo postcard, usually shortened to RPPC, is a photographic print on postcard stock. The Pugh cards shown here reproduce their pictures in printing ink, with colour added. Matching the two forms means we can follow an image across that change of medium. It does not, by itself, tell us which surviving piece of card was made first.

Pugh Manufacturing Co. was based in Toronto. The Toronto Postcard Club’s provisional company history describes a business chiefly engaged in selling and distributing postcards, with printing commissioned from other firms, including printers in Britain and Germany. It finds no evidence that Pugh owned a postcard printing press. The history places its main postcard activity approximately between 1907 and 1912, while acknowledging uncertainty about the beginning. Those are company-level dates, not a securely established issue date for every 524 card. The Toronto Postcard Club’s history of Pugh.

The name on a postcard therefore need not name the person who took the photograph, or even the business that physically printed it. A studio might make a photographic print; a publisher might arrange its reproduction; a separate printer might add the colour and lettering. The cards preserve parts of that process, but rarely a complete account of it.

The twenty-two views we can document

Harry Holman’s illustrated Pugh checklist, dated 3 January 2019, records 524-1 through 524-22. Twenty-one of those numbered views are represented in this collection. The exception is 524-21, Hillsborough Bridge. Its place below is retained because the published checklist documents it, even though there is no copy here to display. Twenty-two is the documented extent used for this article; it is not proof that no further numbers were issued. The STRAITPOST checklist, pages 5–8.

There are seven confirmed matches of the same photographic image: 524-5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 14 and 19. Kent Street, 524-4, has a related RPPC, but it is a different exposure. The remaining spaces record what has not yet been matched. An empty space means “not yet identified,” not “never existed.”

Each row places the photographic postcard on the left and the Pugh colour card on the right. For Kent Street the left-hand image is explicitly labelled as a related view. The lettering that would not quite disappear

On a few cards, the most revealing thing is the caption we were not meant to read. The earlier block letters have been worked over, leaving pale or dark remnants beneath the colour and texture of the printed scene. On Empire Day the photographic counterpart lets us compare the surviving caption directly. On Sunnyside, the title is still partly legible even though its photographic companion is missing.

The Queen Square view in 2633 is especially suggestive. Once the older lettering has been noticed, the new caption no longer looks like the beginning of the card’s history. It looks like a later layer. I suspect that remnants of an earlier caption run through the whole 524 series. That is a working theory: some traces are much easier to resolve than others, several cannot yet be transcribed, and the missing Hillsborough Bridge copy cannot be examined here. The seven confirmed image pairings do not depend on accepting that wider theory.

Even a convincing ghost caption leaves a question about the object used for reproduction. The source could have been an RPPC, another captioned photographic print, or a copy made from one. A shared photograph does not prove that the surviving card on the left was the actual object sent to the printer. Nor does it prove that every photographic edition preceded every colour edition. Prints could be made from the same negative at different times.

Harry Holman had already noticed the problem on Brighton Pond in 2017. His comparison of Pugh and Rumsey cards found the same scene with a difference among the ducks, as well as nearly erased lettering on the Pugh image. He left open whether a duck had been added to one edition or removed from the other. It is a small change with a large implication: the printed view contains editorial decisions as well as photographic information. Ducks and Drakes on Brighton Pond.

That is why it matters to follow particular trees, figures and objects across editions. A newly coloured roof is not evidence that a building was reroofed. A fountain without its spray need not be a fountain that was turned off. Before turning differences between postcards into a history of changes in the town, we need to ask whether the changes happened at a printer’s workbench.

Does the trail lead to Bayer?

Two matches take us beyond visual resemblance. The photographic versions of Market Square, 524-10, and Prince of Wales College, 524-19, carry Bayer imprints. Those are physical marks on surviving cards. They connect those views with the Charlottetown studio of J. A. S. Bayer, whose career is explored in the Bayer story.

It is tempting to extend that connection across the entire numbered series. The views form a coherent Charlottetown group; some photographic counterparts use conspicuous block captions; retouched lettering recurs on the printed cards. A group of images obtained through one studio would explain those relationships. That remains a hypothesis. A publisher’s series could also bring together photographs from several sources, and the matching RPPCs themselves use more than one caption treatment.

The question becomes more interesting when W. S. Louson enters the picture. Carter & Co.’s souvenir album Charlottetown: The Beautiful City of Prince Edward Island states that its photographs were “furnished by W. S. Louson.” Some images in that album also survive on cards bearing explicit Bayer credits. Furnishing a photograph and taking it are not necessarily the same act. The album names the James Bayne Co. of Grand Rapids as its engravers and printers, introducing yet another business into the history of those images. The digitized Carter album.

There is published evidence for a studio connection. Chris Ellis’s 2024 study states that Gauvin, Gentzel & Co. printed Louson’s photographs and made exhibition enlargements and copies. Bayer managed and later owned the Charlottetown studio under that name. This makes a printing relationship plausible, but the statement does not identify the maker of the seven photographs paired here. Nor does it document a transfer of the 524 images to Pugh. Ellis, “William Steele Louson: A Philatelic Perspective,” page 174.

An individual printed credit deserves to be kept alongside that broader evidence. The College photograph on 2532, for example, explicitly says “Photo by Bayer” and was posted on 9 August 1904. It is the College photograph in the Carter album, but not the exposure used for Pugh 524-19. Combining those two different views would create a connection the photographs do not support. The album and the Bayer–Louson relationship deserve their own instalment; here they explain why authorship cannot be settled by a familiar name alone.

A series with room for discoveries

The seven exact matches already establish something substantial: photographs circulating in photographic postcard form also became Pugh colour views. The relationship survives changes to lettering, colour, framing and individual details. The remaining gaps show where that argument has not yet been demonstrated image by image.

There is a collecting hunch that the 524 group originally contained twenty-four views. At present the evidence assembled here reaches twenty-two. Numbers 524-23 and 524-24 should remain questions until cards, a publisher’s list or other reliable evidence establishes them. Even a complete numbered run would not automatically establish one photographer.

A newly found RPPC could close an image gap. An original negative or an early print with reliable documentation could strengthen an attribution. An advertisement, letter or order could explain how pictures moved between studio and publisher. Each would answer a different question, and none needs to do all the work on its own.

For now, these cards let us watch the same Charlottetown moments acquire new lives. The people in the Empire Day crowd remain in place while their clothes gain colour. The fountain loses its spray. An old caption almost vanishes, then reappears under magnification. The postcard records a town, but it also records the work of the people who turned its photographs into things to buy, collect and send.

If you have one of the missing counterparts, or a numbered 524 card beyond this list, please get in touch. A clear front and back, with the publisher’s number visible, would help distinguish a new view from another printing of one already known.
