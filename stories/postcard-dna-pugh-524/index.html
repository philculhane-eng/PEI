The history behind the picture

The DNA of P.E.I. Postcards, Part One: Pugh’s 524 Series

One photograph could have several lives. This first instalment follows twenty-two numbered Charlottetown views through Pugh’s colour postcards, looking for the photographic images beneath the ink.

Look at the hats in the Empire Day crowd. In one postcard the scene is a photograph in shades of brown: people gathered around a monument, bare branches against the buildings beyond. In the other, the roofs have turned red, the grass green and the sky blue. Every hat, and every person wearing one, stays exactly in place.

That is where this investigation begins. A postcard can look quite different from another and still preserve precisely the same moment. Cropping changes its boundaries. Colour changes its mood. A fresh caption changes the words by which we find it. Beneath those alterations, the photograph retains details that allow us to recognize it again.

Those inherited details are the “DNA” of these postcards. Following them takes us beyond identifying a building or a street. We begin to see how a photograph travelled: through a studio, into a publisher’s catalogue, onto another postcard, and sometimes into a book. This series will follow those journeys. The first case is a particularly revealing group of Charlottetown views bearing Pugh’s series number 524.

A photograph, a postcard, a business

A real photo postcard, usually shortened to RPPC, is a photographic print on postcard stock. The Pugh cards shown here reproduce their pictures in printing ink, with colour added. Match the details and we can follow a Charlottetown moment from one medium to the other.

Pugh Manufacturing Co. was based in Toronto. The Toronto Postcard Club’s provisional history describes a business chiefly engaged in selling and distributing postcards, with printing commissioned from firms in Britain, Germany and elsewhere. It places Pugh’s main postcard activity approximately between 1907 and 1912. The dates of the individual 524 editions remain to be established. The Toronto Postcard Club’s history of Pugh.

Making a postcard could bring several businesses together. A studio supplied a photograph; a publisher arranged its reproduction; a printer added colour and lettering. The surviving cards carry traces of that work, sometimes in a tiny credit, sometimes in the picture itself.

The twenty-two views we can document

Harry Holman’s illustrated Pugh checklist, dated 3 January 2019, records 524-1 through 524-22. Twenty-one of those views are represented in this collection. 524-21, Hillsborough Bridge, remains on the collecting list, with a space kept for it below. These twenty-two are the views documented so far. The STRAITPOST checklist, pages 5–8.

There are seven confirmed matches of the same photographic image: 524-5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 14 and 19. Kent Street, 524-4, has a related RPPC showing a different exposure. The open spaces mark searches still in progress, ready for a future discovery.

The lettering beneath the colour

Some of the best clues lie beneath the newer captions. Earlier block letters have been worked over, leaving pale or dark remnants in the printed scene. On Empire Day we can compare the traces directly with the photographic card. On Sunnyside, part of the old title remains legible while its photographic companion awaits discovery.

The Queen Square view in 2633 is especially suggestive. Once the older lettering catches your eye, the new caption becomes another layer in the picture’s history. I suspect such remnants run through the whole 524 series. That is a working theory. Some traces are clear, others resist a full reading, and Hillsborough Bridge awaits examination. The seven image matches above rest on the visible details shared by their pairs.

The object that reached the printer could have been an RPPC, another captioned print, or a copy made from one. A negative could yield fresh prints over many years. Establishing the publication order of the surviving cards will take further dated examples and records.

Harry Holman had already noticed the problem on Brighton Pond in 2017. His comparison of Pugh and Rumsey cards found the same scene with a difference among the ducks, as well as nearly erased lettering on the Pugh image. He left open whether a duck had been added to one edition or removed from the other. It is a small change with a large implication: the printed view contains editorial decisions as well as photographic information. Ducks and Drakes on Brighton Pond.

Follow a roof, a fountain or a patch of garden through several editions and the printer’s hand begins to emerge. A roof acquires a new colour; a fountain loses its spray. Comparing the photographic version helps us recognize the changes made while preparing the postcard.

Does the trail lead to Bayer?

Two matches take us beyond visual resemblance. The photographic versions of Market Square, 524-10, and Prince of Wales College, 524-19, carry Bayer imprints. Those are physical marks on surviving cards. They connect those views with the Charlottetown studio of J. A. S. Bayer, whose career is explored in the Bayer story.

It is tempting to extend that connection across the entire numbered series. The views form a coherent Charlottetown group; some photographic counterparts use conspicuous block captions; retouched lettering recurs on the printed cards. A group of images obtained through one studio would explain those relationships. That remains a hypothesis. A publisher’s series could also bring together photographs from several sources, and the matching RPPCs themselves use more than one caption treatment.

W. S. Louson adds another strand. Carter & Co.’s souvenir album Charlottetown: The Beautiful City of Prince Edward Island says its photographs were “furnished by W. S. Louson.” Some of its images also appear on cards with explicit Bayer credits. The album names the James Bayne Co. of Grand Rapids as engravers and printers. A photographer’s credit, a supplier’s credit and a printer’s name bring several people into the life of these pictures. The digitized Carter album.

Chris Ellis’s 2024 study offers a glimpse inside the studio: Gauvin, Gentzel & Co. printed Louson’s photographs and made exhibition enlargements and copies. Bayer managed and later owned the Charlottetown studio under that name. That working connection is worth following. The authorship of the seven paired photographs and their route to Pugh remain open questions. Ellis, “William Steele Louson: A Philatelic Perspective,” page 174.

Card 2532 brings those names together especially clearly. Its College photograph says “Photo by Bayer,” and this copy was posted on 9 August 1904. The same exposure appears in the Carter album. Pugh 524-19 uses a different College view, giving us a second photograph to trace. Part Two: One Market, Several Names follows the album connection through four editions of a Market Building photograph.

A series with room for discoveries

The seven matches already let us follow photographs into Pugh’s colour views, through changes in lettering, colour, framing and detail. The open spaces make this a working album, with room for the next collecting discovery.

The hunch that this was originally a twenty-four-view set adds an enticing possibility. The documented list currently reaches twenty-two. A card numbered 524-23 or 524-24, or a publisher’s list, could extend it. The question of who took the photographs will grow alongside the search for the cards.

A newly found RPPC could close a gap in the album. An early print or original negative with reliable documentation could give us a photographer’s name. A letter or order might show how pictures travelled from a studio to a publisher. Discoveries can arrive from several directions.

For now, these cards let us watch the same Charlottetown moments acquire new lives. The people in the Empire Day crowd remain in place while their clothes gain colour. The fountain loses its spray. An old caption almost vanishes, then reappears under magnification. The postcard records a town, but it also records the work of the people who turned its photographs into things to buy, collect and send.

If you have one of the missing counterparts, or a numbered 524 card beyond this list, please get in touch. A clear front and back, with the publisher’s number visible, would help distinguish a new view from another printing of one already known.
