The history behind the picture

Spotlight on Montague

On a busy day at Montague, the postcard almost runs out of room. Horses, wagon wheels, sacks, men and schooner rigging crowd the wharf. There are people close enough to look straight back at us and masts rising far beyond them. Carter’s caption, “A Busy Day,” is wonderfully economical. The photograph supplies the rest.

The river gives Montague’s postcards their shape. Streets rise from it, ships come up it, the bridge crosses it and the railway reaches its edge. Farther along, trees and small boats make it a place for an afternoon out. The photographers could turn from a crowded wharf to a quiet riverbank and still be telling the story of the same town.

Looking across the water

William Cumming’s “Main Street” view begins with water and a reflection. A wharf stands at the left; beyond it, the road climbs between buildings. Seen from across the river, the town has a clear entrance and a gathering of roofs above it. Cumming used the opposite bank as a natural viewing platform.

The real-photo view of Main Street’s south side brings us onto the road itself. A horse and carriage occupy the middle distance. On the right, people stand beside the storefronts and a sign advertises the Montague Meat Market. The rutted surface makes the distance between the wooden sidewalk and the road feel surprisingly large. This copy was sent in July 1913, placing the shopfront scene firmly within the busy years represented by the coloured cards.

New ways of coming and going

The first-train postcard is one of the collection’s favourites, and it belongs at the centre of any Montague album. Steam rises around the locomotive, people crowd the platform and the waterfront is close at hand. The branch railway’s public opening was set for Dominion Day in 1906; passengers celebrated with a trip to Georgetown and back. The station’s heritage history records that excursion.

In the companion card the locomotive is absent. We can read MONTAGUE across the roof and see people standing under the deep eaves. The excitement has become a new landmark. These two views show how readily a practical improvement could turn into a souvenir people were proud to send away. The full station story follows both photographs in more detail.

Another improvement received equally close attention. A real-photo postcard of the steel bridge is captioned January 11, 1913. Flags fly above the trusses; people stand on the bridge and its approach; temporary supports remain below. The structure occupies almost the whole width of the picture. It gave photographers an occasion, a strong geometric subject and a new way to show the two sides of town joined together.

Cumming’s other Montague

Cumming’s name links many of the town’s best printed views, but he did not confine himself to streets and public buildings. “A Shady Nook” looks through trees towards the river. A seated figure is tucked into the shade. The narrow upright format draws us into a little opening in the foliage, with the water shining beyond.

At MacDonald’s Mill Stream he takes the higher view again. The stream winds between sloping banks and drops over a small dam. Trees supply a foreground and a frame. The title keeps the mill’s working landscape in the picture even as the composition offers it as somewhere beautiful to look at. That combination is characteristic of the Montague selection: the same water can be useful and inviting.

For a town with so much river traffic, a postcard of the post office feels especially appropriate. Letters, parcels and commercial correspondence had their own destination here. Cumming’s photograph gives the building its full height, with steep rooflines above a strongly arched entrance. It is another sign of the town’s confidence, set among the more fluid views of wharf and river.

An afternoon by the river

The RPPC mailed in August 1915 is a lovely place to finish. A few people sit or stand near the bank, with a broad sweep of water and a wooded shore beyond. They are small in the landscape, and that is the pleasure of it. There is space to stop. The town’s river has carried ships, given the bridge its purpose and drawn the railway to the wharf. Here it gives people somewhere to spend time together.

The collection’s Montague is full of movement, but its photographers also knew where to pause. The town comes into focus through those changes of pace: a loaded wagon on the wharf, a carriage in Main Street, a crowd beside the new train, and a few figures looking across the river.
