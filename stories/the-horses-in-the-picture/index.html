The history behind the picture

The Horses in the Picture

Start looking for horses, and the Island changes. A postcard bought for its railway station has a line of carriages outside. A picture of a milk factory has almost as much to say about the animals at its door. A lighthouse stands above two waiting horses. Even beside an icebound steamer, there they are.

They are so familiar to the photographers that often nobody thought to mention them. The caption names the street, the ship, the splendid building. The horse gets on with being there. Following these overlooked animals through the collection brings together an Island of work, travel, holidays and small personal encounters.

On this North Shore beach, two horse-drawn carriages have come down to the water. The printed caption leaves the precise beach unnamed. It gives us enough, though: the shore was somewhere you could take a drive, with wheels and hooves crossing the sand. The passengers get the outing; the horses supply the movement.

One horse, a city full of passengers

In Charlottetown, one driver was sufficiently recognizable to become the subject of his own souvenir. Card 4638 calls him “Charlottetown’s Popular Carman James Murphy.” People crowd the jaunting car, seated on its outward-facing benches, while the horse stands in profile. This is a little portrait of public life as well as a vehicle.

The companion view names “Murphy’s Irish Jaunting Car.” Look at the seat beside the driver: two dogs have secured their places. The dogs are a delightful discovery, but the horse is the essential participant. The postcard preserves the person whose business it was to drive people about, the unusual arrangement in which they travelled, and the animal on which the whole enterprise depended.

Elsewhere, horses are ordinary street furniture. On Central Street in Summerside, a horse and carriage wait beside a house. On Water Street, vehicles share the space in front of the shops. Outside Charlottetown’s railway station, road transport gathers at the entrance to rail transport. A train could carry you across the province; there was still the journey between the station and the place you actually wanted to be.

These photographs are worth reading from the ground up. Notice the road surface, the height of the carriage steps, the shafts extending either side of an animal, and the space needed to stand or turn a vehicle. They reveal the practical scale of a town arranged around horses. The station postcards acquire a second story when we stop looking only for locomotives.

A Souris wagon card travelled with quite a different joke. Harold asked his correspondent in England, “What do you think of our carriages?” The photograph shows two men on a working cart. On another printing of the same view, Jack told his sister she would recognize the men, naming one as Charlie Paquet. The messages preserve familiar people and Harold’s joke where we first see an old vehicle.

The work between the farm and the ship

At Charlottetown’s condensed milk factory, the delivery system is visible at the door: horses, open wagons and rows of metal milk cans. The factory is an imposing industrial building, but the picture makes its dependence on the surrounding countryside visible. Milk has to reach the door before anything can happen inside.

At Montague, “A Busy Day” is almost an understatement. Horses and loaded carts crowd the waterfront beneath a forest of masts and rigging. This is the landward side of the Island’s shipping story: the short journeys that brought cargo to the vessels. The view does not tell us where each load was going, and it would be a mistake to assign these particular carts to a named voyage. It does show how much work happened before a ship could leave.

The same collection follows horses back into the fields. There are teams ploughing, working among potatoes, and drawing harvesting equipment; hay wagons carry loads that rise above the people beside them. Taken together, these pictures show successive kinds of farm work rather than one generic “rural scene.”

The horse-drawn machine in card 2645 comes with an unusually personal note. Its sender calls it “another poor print of one of my pictures,” identifies Bedeque and the Dunk River in the distance, and signs himself “Papa.” His dissatisfaction with the print is our good fortune: he wrote down what he had photographed. A distant working team becomes part of a family exchange.

At O’Leary, a roadmaking crew lines up with its teams and equipment. Horses were helping make the roads on which they worked—and which would, eventually, carry increasing numbers of motor vehicles.

A loaded wagon or a field implement represents effort. The photographs cannot tell us how a particular animal was treated, but they do let us recognize its labour. Behind the attractive view was a daily obligation to feed, shelter, harness and care for the animals that made so much of the work possible.

“I am having some nice drives”

Work is only part of the tale. The North Shore road view places a carriage in a carefully composed landscape of water, fields, fences and trees. The card advertises the Inter-Colonial and Prince Edward Island Railway, yet its foreground traveller is a horse. Rail travel and a country drive could belong to the same holiday.

At East Point, the lighthouse is the landmark, but the two horse-drawn carriages occupy much of the foreground. We do not have names for the people or proof of the reason for their visit. We can see how they were equipped to travel. The animals make the lighthouse feel less isolated: there was a road journey to this place, as well as a maritime purpose for the tower.

Card 4581 adds a little humour. It carries “Greetings from Rusticoville,” but appears to use a stock landscape rather than a photograph of that community. A place-name greeting does not establish where the pictured scene was photographed. Writing in July 1914, Emmie tells Mae: “I am having some nice drives. Willie and I are in the carriage you see on this card.” She had arrived on the Island late the previous Friday and wanted a reply sent to Rustico, care of John Houston.

Emmie’s line is best read as whimsy: she puts herself and Willie into the ready-made scene. Her message records the pleasure of taking drives and sharing a holiday joke. It does not identify the pictured passengers or establish that the landscape is Rusticoville. That playful use of a postcard is a small story in its own right.

Other photographs give the carriage a place in the family portrait. In front of Senator Yeo’s house at Port Hill, the horse and vehicle share the foreground with two men whose identities the image alone does not establish. At Charlottetown, two people pose in a carriage outside a substantial home. The horse belongs in the picture of the property and the people who used it. The Yeo–Richards story follows the Port Hill view further.

Hooves on the ice

The winter postcards offer a startling change of surface. Beside the Stanley near Pictou, a line of horses and sleighs stands on the ice close to the steamer. At Georgetown, the card captioned “D.G.S. Minto (Winter, 1905)” has another gathering of horses and sleds to the right of the ship. A Summerside view puts horses and people alongside the Stanley amid broken and uneven ice.

The near-Pictou photograph is on the Nova Scotia side of the service. These are scenes of access to ships at particular places, not evidence that the horses made the whole crossing of the Strait. That distinction matters. The iceboat journey between the capes involved a different kind of travel, and the people hauling those boats deserve to remain visible in its history.

On the Island’s rivers, winter could open routes of its own. In recollections preserved by UPEI’s Island Narratives Program, Keith Wheatley remembered travelling by horse and sleigh down the North River, under the bridge and onward to Charlottetown. Spruce bushes marked the way across the ice, giving travellers something to follow when snow obscured their bearings.

Don MacEwen remembered his mother and a neighbour taking a horse and sleigh across the ice to the movies in the 1950s. They paid fifty cents to leave the horse while they went inside. That modest detail brings the distance between past and present down to a very human scale: an evening’s entertainment, with somewhere to leave your transport. Other recollections on the same page describe dangerous ice and horses being lost. Winter mobility depended on judgment as well as endurance. Read the North River recollections.

On parade, at the track, in the photograph

Some horses were meant to be noticed. Mounted men fill the postcard captioned “Prince Edward Island Light Horse.” At Charlottetown’s Exhibition Track, horses and sulkies pass a grandstand crowded with spectators. A much later colour postcard of harness racing at Summerside carries that part of Island horse culture into another generation of images.

The cyanotype below moves closer still: a rider and horse posed together, with a handwritten claim to a first prize. We have the image and the inscription, but no secure identification of either participant or the particular competition. It is enough to see that the horse was worth a portrait of its own.

A long overlap with the motor car

The number of horses in these pictures is more than a photographer’s preference. The 1931 census recorded about 35 horses for every 100 Islanders, down from 38 in 1911. Its commentary explicitly connected the decline to the growth of tractors, trucks and automobiles. That is a ratio of animals to people, not a measure of journeys or of how many households owned a horse. It nevertheless gives useful scale to the scenes: horses remained numerous well into the motor age. 1931 census, Prince Edward Island, table XLVIII.

One visitor found that persistence inconvenient. On 11 August 1939, Frank wrote on a North Shore postcard: “This is a beautiful Island but no so good for my business as most transportation is done with horses.” It was mailed from Summerside the following day.

Frank does not tell us what his business was. His complaint is a traveller’s assessment, not a transport survey. Yet it is a wonderful contemporary voice to set beside the census and the photographs. A horse could be picturesque to one person and a commercial obstacle to another.

The change required more than putting cars on sale. D. Bailey Clark’s research on Island transportation traces the importance of road improvement and snowplowing during the 1940s, 1950s and 1960s in making automobiles the predominant form of transport. Summer roads and dependable winter access were different problems. Remembering the horse-and-sleigh trip to the movies helps explain why the transition was an overlap of ways of travelling, rather than a single farewell to the horse. “Who Wants a Car Anyway?”

A little girl, a very large horse

After the teams, the wagons and the steamers, return to the small girl at Souris. She stands in a white bonnet beside a horse that dwarfs her. There is a little bow in its mane. Someone has taken the trouble to make a photograph of this particular pairing.

The back begins, “What do you think of this?” Blanche reports that Constance is well and happy, and that they are taking good care of her. The card, postmarked Souris East in June 1909, is addressed to Henry Macfarlane at 54 School Street in Charlottetown. Here the name comes from the message, not from a guess about the child’s face.

There is a promising further identification. Constance Ida MacFarlane, born in Charlottetown in 1904, was the daughter of Henry Havelock MacFarlane and grew up at School Street House. The names and address make her a strong candidate for the child. Her later career was in marine botany: she directed the Seaweeds Division of the Nova Scotia Research Foundation from 1949 to 1970. The connection is persuasive, but a probable identification should remain labelled as such. The childhood home’s account and the Nova Scotian Institute of Science biography provide the background.

The horse’s name has not survived on the card. Nor do we know who tied the bow. Those gaps need no invented answer. We have the animal, the child, the photograph and Blanche’s affectionate reassurance. Among all these records of horses earning their keep, someone saved this small encounter too.
