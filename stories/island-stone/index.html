The history behind the picture

Island Stone: From the Quarry to the Postcard

On Main Street in Souris, a stone building stands shoulder to shoulder with its wooden neighbours. Two flights of steps rise from the pavement. Paired windows climb towards a steep gable. Even in a faded photograph, the rough blocks and pale bands give the post office a weight of its own.

Follow that masonry through the collection and a different Island emerges: a farmhouse built from the hill behind it, a bank raised by an Acadian community, churches repaired after fire, and public buildings whose original jobs have changed. Some wear their Island stone in broad red walls. Others combine it with brick or with the lighter sandstone brought across the Strait from Nova Scotia.

The story reaches from the nineteenth-century quarries to the conservation work of 2026. Its most intimate beginning is a house already familiar from the Haslam–Colclough postcards.

1857: Stone Cottage, Springfield

The little tent in the garden, the fence and the trees make Stone Cottage feel like a place where an afternoon is unfolding. Behind them is the squared masonry of the Haslam home. Robert Haslam built the house in 1857 with stone cut from a quarry at the back of the farm. The house and the land belonged together in the most literal way. Carter W. Jeffery’s account traces that local origin and records the building’s eventual loss.

The family photographs and correspondence give these walls another life in Florence Haslam and Basil Colclough’s story. The approach to the property also appears on card 3185; here, on 3214, we can get close enough to read the stonework.

Stone Cottage was demolished in 2011. After visiting the site that August, Jeffery counted six remaining Island sandstone houses, in Hamilton, Hazel Grove, Harrington, Brackley, Clyde River and Lower Montague. That was a count of houses in 2011. The churches and public buildings on these postcards belong to a wider surviving landscape.

1861–1864: a bank for Rustico

The Farmers’ Bank of Rustico sits quietly at the edge of this view, its long stone front much less conspicuous than the church tower. Yet the building carried an ambitious idea. Father Georges-Antoine Belcourt and his parishioners established a bank that could lend to local farmers on manageable terms. The bank operated from 1864 to 1894 and became an important predecessor of the credit-union movement.

The building was constructed in the early 1860s from Island sandstone. Its broad, simple front gives it a solidity quite different from the elaborate stone churches that followed. After banking came years of use as a parish hall; the Farmers’ Bank museum welcomed visitors for another season in 2026. The heritage record connects the surviving building to the community that made it possible.

1878: two stones at the Kirk

At the Kirk of St. James in Charlottetown, the materials reverse a pattern seen elsewhere in this article. The church’s own visitor guide, reproduced by historian Ian Scott, describes walls of Wallace, Nova Scotia freestone, with Island sandstone at the doorways, windows and buttresses. Island stone could supply the accents as well as the body of a building. Read the Kirk’s self-guided tour.

Dedicated in 1878, the church survived an interior fire in 1898. William Critchlow Harris designed the replacement ceiling. Its parish history follows the subsequent alterations, while the 2026 parish notices bring the building into the present: the old stone church still gathers a congregation.

1887: Georgetown’s courthouse

Georgetown’s courthouse looks compact enough to be a house, but its substantial walls had a public purpose. Fire had destroyed the earlier wooden courthouse and valuable records. The replacement, designed by William Critchlow Harris and built in 1887, answered the demand for masonry.

Its exterior brings together red Island sandstone and grey Wallace sandstone. The lighter stone forms the carefully shaped window and entrance arches and the foundation. On the postcard, those smooth openings stand out against the rougher faces of the walls. The heritage description identifies both stones and the way Harris used them.

The building has retained its original calling. In 2026, the Courts of Prince Edward Island still list the Kings County courthouse at 60 Kent Street as the Georgetown location of the Provincial Court.

1887–1888: Montague’s post office

Montague’s post office makes a different contribution to the story. The walls are brick, made locally by Robert Stewart; the foundation stone came from the bank of the Montague River. The arches, steep roof and dormers made an imposing public building on the rise above the bridge. The building’s heritage record preserves the unusually precise connection between its foundation and the river below.

Postal service ended here in 1954. The town bought the property in 1957 for a museum, giving the building a second public life as the Garden of the Gulf Museum.

That second life is still developing. In November 2026, the Town of Three Rivers issued tender documents for an accessible washroom and walkway/ramp project at the museum. The plans show a familiar challenge for an old public building: making it easier for people to use while caring for the fabric that makes it worth keeping.

1888: the small chapel beside St. Peter’s

All Souls’ Chapel rewards a closer look at a postcard easily filed under the larger cathedral beside it. Built in 1888, the chapel has heavy, rough-textured Island sandstone walls. William Critchlow Harris designed it; the Lowe Brothers built it; Robert Harris supplied the paintings inside. The stone changes again at the chancel arch, which is Wallace stone. The heritage record describes that meeting of materials and crafts.

The two cards let us pass through the door. One places the chapel in the streetscape; the other brings us to the altar. A new Hidden Island film about William Critchlow Harris, released by the P.E.I. government in July 2026, returns to the chapel to explore the architect behind these buildings.

1896: St. Paul’s in red and grey

At St. Paul’s, the familiar red wall and pale trim belong to another Harris design. The church opened in 1896, built by H. and S. Lowe. Island sandstone forms the walls; Nova Scotia freestone marks the stringcourses and the window and door surrounds. Colour printing gives the postcard a warm glow, but the contrast between the materials is part of the building itself. The city’s heritage description sets out that distinction.

The tower, steep roofs and irregular outline make a fine picture from the street. Inside, Harris paid close attention to sound as well as appearance. The present-day parish continues to worship in the stone church, carrying its use into 2026.

1901–1902: St. Mary’s above Souris

St. Mary’s draws the eye uphill. Its sandstone came from nearby Chepstow, and the church was completed in 1902 to William Critchlow Harris’s design. Pale Nova Scotia freestone outlines the Gothic openings and provides decorative contrasts higher in the towers.

The tall wooden spire in the early postcards dates the appearance we are seeing. Fire gutted the church in 1929, leaving its sandstone walls standing. Architect John Marshall Hunter rebuilt around them, and the replacement church opened in 1930. The lost spire gave way to a lower copper cap. The heritage record makes it possible to follow that transformation from the postcard church to the one still overlooking Souris.

The parish’s current photographs show the later tower and the continuing life within those older walls. Here survival included a major rebuilding.

1905: the stone post office on Main Street

Back on Main Street, the close view supplies the name for the building at the left of card 3979. Souris’s Post Office and Customs House was built in 1905 from Island sandstone quarried at nearby St. Catherine’s. Its lighter trim is Wallace freestone from Nova Scotia. The rough walls, smoother bands and arches are especially easy to study in these real-photo cards. The heritage record identifies the quarries and describes the building’s changing uses.

The post office occupied the first floor, customs the second, and the caretakers the third. Federal services moved out in 1960. The building became Town Hall, and Souris’s municipal offices remain at 75 Main Street in 2026. Card 2778 preserves another copy of the close view.

The railway’s boulder stations

The stone stations at Kensington and Alberton belong beside this story. Their rounded boulders give them a texture quite unlike the quarried sandstone churches. Kensington’s 1905 station used fieldstone from western Prince County and New Brunswick, together with Wallace stone sills and the lettered entrance arch. Alberton’s 1904–1905 station is described as granite construction. Both were designed by C. B. Chappell. The heritage entries for Kensington and Alberton explain this distinctive pair.

The Island stone inside Province House

Province House takes us from the postcard surface into the building. Its familiar exterior was built with stone from Pictou, Nova Scotia; Island sandstone was used in the inner walls. Modern repairs have brought stone from Wallace to the exterior and from Kellys Cross to the interior.

Reg Porter drew attention to that connection between land and building in his 2022 essay Topography and the Promise of Settlement and Architecture. In discussing the marks people leave on the landscape, he included the extraction of sandstone for Province House’s inner wall. His essay encourages us to look beyond the finished façade to the countryside that supplied it.

Parks Canada’s conservation account supplies the next chapter: suitable Island stone was located at Kellys Cross in 2018, and the above-ground interior sandstone repairs were completed in April 2022. Its 2026 update kept the building closed to public visits for the summer while the larger conservation project approached completion.

Stone Cottage is gone; the Farmers’ Bank and Montague post office have become museums; Souris’s post office serves its town; the churches and Georgetown courthouse continue their original work. At Province House, newly cut Island stone has joined the old. The postcards hold an earlier moment in each building’s life. Put beside the surviving walls and the work still being done to them, they let us follow that life forward.
