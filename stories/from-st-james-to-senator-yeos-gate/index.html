The history behind the picture

From St. James to Senator Yeo’s Gate

The way into this house was through a graveyard. In a postcard view beside St. James Anglican Church at Port Hill, it was the great red monument to Susan S. Yeo Richards that first drew my eye. Then I came upon a view captioned “Senator Yeo’s Residence.” The names belong together. John Yeo, the senator, was Susan’s younger brother. Follow her married name instead and the trail runs to another house, at Bideford. As it happens, somebody put the three family houses on a single atlas plate long before any of these postcards went into the mail.

Fifty pounds for St. James

Susan belonged to the family of the shipbuilder James Yeo and his wife Damaris Sargent. She married Captain William Richards in 1849 and died in December 1901. The family history records her as Susannah and places her burial at St. James. The burial ground gives us a link to her father in his own lifetime. In June 1843 Bishop John Inglis came to consecrate the little mission church at Port Hill and the ground around it. He recorded fifteen confirmations that day. He also put down a sum of money: Mr. Yeo had subscribed fifty pounds toward the building.

The little church, built in 1841, stands on an Island sandstone foundation among the graves. A later St. James was built across the road. That distinction matters when looking for Susan’s monument: the name belongs to a parish with two church buildings, and the old one is remembered as the Shipbuilders’ Church. By the time Susan was buried, nearly sixty years had passed since the bishop wrote down her father’s contribution. Old St. James and the bishop’s account · The Yeo and Richards family record.

Three houses on one sheet

Open the residences plate in Meacham’s 1880 atlas. There’s John Yeo, still styled “Esq.,” at Port Hill. There’s his brother James at Green Park, also Port Hill. And there’s the Hon. William Richards at “Biddeford,” as the atlas spells it. Three houses, three men named as householders. You could read the captions from one end of the sheet to the other without learning the one name that explains why these particular houses belong together: Susan was sister to the first two men and wife to the third.

It’s easy to attach the right surname to the wrong house. The Yeo House preserved at Green Park was built in 1865 for James Yeo Junior, within sight of the shipyard. Its high octagonal cupola is a useful landmark. The house in the postcard is John’s. Above its central porch is a broad gable with an arched window in the middle and a chimney on either side. The atlas engraver had distinguished the brothers’ homes on paper in 1880. The postcard printer did it with a title printed in red.

At the Richards house, the family business reached into the next generation. One of Susan’s sons, also named John, worked with his father in shipbuilding and produce export, and went on to raise cattle and cultivate oysters. Both he and his older brother James William served in the Island legislature. That makes two Johns in the story, and it matters which one is meant: John Richards was Susan’s son; Senator John Yeo was his uncle. The Legislative Assembly’s biography of John Richards.

What the red caption can date

John Yeo was born at Port Hill in 1837. He was Speaker of the Island Assembly at Confederation in 1873. In 1891, when his brother James retired from the House of Commons, John took his place in federal politics. He was appointed to the Senate in 1898 and remained there until his death at Port Hill in 1924. That gives the postcard a useful pair of boundaries: the caption calling him senator belongs to 1898 or later, and the copy mailed from Arlington was already in use in September 1909. The photograph itself could be older than the caption. The Island legislature’s biography of John Yeo.

I’d stop there in trying to name the people. Two men in dark coats and hats stand at the roadside. The porch, clipped lawn, trees and fence are all as much a part of the picture as they are. There’s a pole by the road; the horse and carriage have been brought neatly into the frame. We can credit the view to “M. Griffin, Photo, O’Leary, P.E.I.,” because that is what the printer put on the back. Nobody printed the men’s names. A caption identifying a senator’s residence is a poor reason to turn either man into the senator.

There are three copies of Griffin’s view here. 0567 has no message. 4042 went to Mrs. C. M. Bliss in Cambridge, Massachusetts, ahead of somebody’s visit. On 0749, posted at Port Hill on November 19, 1910, Ella asks Edgar McArthur in Watertown, Massachusetts: “Do you forget all about fourteen and the nice people who live there.” I don’t know what Edgar called “fourteen,” but the question tells us what Ella wanted the picture to do. An atlas had given the property an owner. A postcard gave somebody far from home a nudge to remember the neighbours.

I started with the red monument because it stood out in a cemetery view. I’d go back to it now for another reason. The atlas preserves the houses under the men’s names. The marker preserves the woman whose own family joined the households together. Neither picture tells quite the same Island story without the other.
