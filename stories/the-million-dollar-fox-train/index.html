The history behind the picture

PEI’s Greatest Postcards – #3 – The Million-Dollar Fox Train

A man stands high beside the engine’s headlamp. A boy has found a place among the men in suits. There’s even somebody with a bicycle. I can make out all of them on postcard 3716, but the passengers that made this train worth photographing are another matter. See if you can spot a single fox.

Two things would have drawn me to this card anyway: a Prince Edward Island steam engine and the little “Bayer Photo” credit. It was another J. A. S. Bayer postcard, the one showing the Odd Fellows in a horse-drawn carriage, that got me collecting. The carriage postcard What earns this one a place among my favourites is the trouble somebody took to identify the people. Turn the card sideways and you can read three labels running up from the bottom edge, each pointing to a man.

Charles Dalton stands just ahead of the tender, labelled “President.” Farther along are Dr. Lundie, the veterinarian, and W. B. Prowse, identified on the photograph as manager. A vet seems a telling choice of passenger. These foxes had to arrive alive and well enough to breed. The company named above the engine was moving its breeding stock from Tignish to a ranch at Southport, across the river from Charlottetown.

What was riding behind No. 20?

I have two copies of Bayer’s wider view: 1789 and 3715. From this angle you can read 20 on the nose, the cab and the tender. Behind the tender is freight car 423; farther back you can see rows of coach windows. Ingenium’s railway collection identifies No. 20 as a 4-4-0 built in July 1899. Its record is for a separate photograph taken in Charlottetown on September 26, 1913. That date belongs to the museum’s locomotive photograph. In railway shorthand that’s four leading wheels, four driving wheels and none trailing. For once the number painted on an Island engine is big enough to spare a postcard collector the magnifying glass.

The baggage master, Andy Bagnall, remembered what the photograph couldn’t show. The foxes had been caught the day before and boxed up in the Tignish freight shed. Two men with revolvers guarded them overnight. Bagnall recalled a nine o’clock departure on November 8, with three armed men riding along. He named Dalton, Lundie and Prowse aboard, the same three men labelled on my card. Bagnall described Prowse as secretary-treasurer of the purchasing company. The label on the print says manager. Both accounts put him on the train, and each supplies its own description of his job. Robert Allan Rankin preserves Bagnall’s account.

How much is a pair of foxes worth?

It started with a rather humbler piece of equipment than a steam engine. On Cherry Island near Alberton, Robert Trenholm Oulton buried wire to keep foxes from digging out. For dens, he blocked one end of hollow logs and packed them with seaweed. Oulton and Dalton went into partnership in 1894; Oulton tended the animals and Dalton found buyers for the pelts. A silver fox is a colour form of the red fox. The trick was getting pairs to produce that prized dark coat, litter after litter.

Other Islanders learned how to raise them. The commemorative plaque in Alberton names James Gordon and Robert Tuplin alongside Dalton and Oulton. For years a handful of breeders kept the live animals to themselves. Dalton’s biographer describes Frank Tuplin selling live breeding stock in 1910 at the equivalent of $5,000 a pair. A pelt brought one price at auction. A pair of live foxes could produce a litter to sell the following spring, and another the year after. That helps explain the revolvers. By 1913 the province counted 277 fox ranches with 3,130 foxes between them. Dalton’s biography and the figures from the boom

So where did Bayer’s million dollars come from? In October 1912, a Charlottetown dispatch printed in the St. John’s Free Press reported that a group of businessmen had taken a $600,000 option on Dalton’s ranch, which then held twenty pairs. Dalton later put his own proceeds at $400,000 in cash and $100,000 in shares. Neither figure tells us how many crates went aboard No. 20 or what they were worth. The million dollars is what the postcard says.

Here is a live silver fox from a different ranch. Bayer photographed this fox with its nose to the snow for William Keir Rogers, who started in 1913 with five pairs and eventually acquired parts of both Dalton’s and Oulton’s holdings. By 1925 he had six Island ranches and others as far away as Switzerland. So there’s some history behind the boast under card 2925: “Largest Individual Breeder of Silver Foxes in the World.”

There’s a trap here for anyone looking up the train. In 1926 another “Million Dollar Train” carried 855 live silver foxes from Summerside to the United States. Culture Summerside documents the later shipment. Bayer’s caption gives us a different departure, destination and owner. The exact year of the Tignish trip is harder to pin down. Rankin puts the sale in 1912. The Dictionary of Canadian Biography dates the new Southport operation to 1913. A sale, a train movement and the opening of a ranch could all have different dates. The faint pencil on 1789 reads “Jany 1914.” Whoever added it left us no explanation, and the card has no postmark.

What the company needed after the train arrived

Electric lighting, telephone service to Charlottetown and water under pressure in case of fire all figure in Rankin’s description of the Southport ranch. It is quite a step from Oulton burying wire and making dens out of hollow logs. Move breeding animals on a special train and you have solved the problem of one day. To keep a stock of valuable animals alive and producing young you had to think about the next season and the one after that. The man labelled “Veterinary” on the train begins to look as interesting as the man labelled “President.”

A photograph from another Island ranch brings out just how much the price of an animal could depend on its parents. Bayer took card 3875 for Bay State Fur Farms. A man is crouching with a pail, holding a hand over a fox which has risen on its hind legs to reach him. The typewritten message on the back says the parents of this fox sold for $25,000. That is a claim made about the parents of one particular animal. It tells us a good deal about what somebody considered worth typing on the back of a fox photograph. The Bay State fox and its typewritten pedigree claim

Cumming photographed two foxes among snowy trees for a very different card from Montague. His name is actually printed on 0919, along with “Blue Fox Ranch” and the year 1913. The pair of animals and the trees occupy exactly the same places in the coloured version on 0304. Compare that view with the entirely different location in Bayer’s silver-fox train photograph. At least two photographers were finding subjects in Island ranching, and a purchaser could come away with quite different ideas of what the business looked like. Cumming’s credited view · The coloured version

By 1925 the Dominion Experimental Fox Ranch had been established at Summerside. Its main building and the long rows beside it turn up on another postcard. Breeders wanted reliable information about feeding foxes, controlling parasites and passing fur characteristics from one generation to the next. The federal superintendent published reports on the work. Where Dalton and Oulton had a strong commercial interest in keeping a successful line of breeding animals in a few hands, there was now an institution whose job included making the results of experiments available to other breeders. Summerside’s experimental ranch · The superintendent’s reports

Turn 3716 over and there is one more man to meet. No address, stamp or postmark; just “Yours truly, James Paton” and, underlined with a flourish, “Director.” I can’t pick Paton out of the line-up. Dalton, on the other hand, I can pick out, and so can you. The man standing in front of the engine with his job printed at his feet would become lieutenant governor of Prince Edward Island in 1930. I’d happily collect the train alone. Of the two views, though, I’d keep the one with the names. Paton even made sure we knew which job was his.
