The history behind the picture

The Island’s Second City: Summerside

At the Summerside wharf, the train is almost alongside the steamer. Passengers could hardly ask for a more convenient introduction to the town. On the postcard headed “All Ashore!”, the Empress waits at one side of the broad timber platform while railway carriages occupy the other. The picture has somewhere to take you before you have even turned it over.

Summerside’s postcards have that outward-looking confidence. Tall shops, substantial hotels and a grand post office make frequent appearances. Then the album opens into gardens, shaded streets and small boats on the bay. The place we now know as the Island’s second city was still a town in this postcard era; city status came in 1995. Its importance as a port and service centre for Prince County was already well established. Culture Summerside’s history traces the growth from the shipyards of Green’s Shore to the commercial town.

Water Street puts its best face forward

The brick blocks on Water Street look built to impress. Rounded window heads, decorative rooflines and long stretches of shop windows give the street a scale quite different from the low wooden buildings in many Island village views. One publisher went beyond naming the street: card 1277 advertises the blocks of Major Schurman and J. A. Brace. The owners’ names were part of the attraction.

Look at the angle of the second photograph. The photographer has allowed the buildings on the right to tower over the roadway, while the line of poles and storefronts leads the eye towards the far end. It is a picture of plenty: plenty to buy, plenty of business being done, plenty of town beyond the frame. The unpaved street and the people outside the shops keep that grandeur within everyday reach.

The Clifton House makes a similar promise to a visitor. Its name runs across the facade above the corner entrance, and its several storeys rise well above the neighbouring frontage. In the oval real-photo postcard, a horse and carriage wait outside. A hotel could be a landmark, an advertisement and a small street scene all at once.

A few streets away

Read’s photograph of Central Street changes the pace. Trees lean over the road, a long fence runs along the left, and the utility poles carry their wires away into the distance. The textures of the street, foliage and wooden fence do the work. Lorne Hiram Read opened his Water Street photographic studio in 1909; his pictures let us see Summerside through someone who worked in the town. The provincial archives’ biography of Read places that career behind the signature.

The post office offers another kind of civic pride. The tall roof, dormers and prominent chimney make the building almost a portrait subject. The sender of this copy evidently thought so too: on the back he asks how it compares with “our office,” then signs himself Papa. Summerside was being measured against a familiar place somewhere else.

Louson comes down to the water

William Steele Louson found subjects much smaller than a brick block. In “Catching Smelts,” the fisherman sits close to the edge of the wharf, his line extending over the water. In “Anticipation,” people wait beside a steamer. The upright format brings the figures close while leaving enough ship and harbour to explain where they are. These are pictures about being in Summerside: waiting for an arrival, watching the water, hoping for a bite.

The harbour also gave him room for a more expansive picture. “Return of Lobster Boats” places dark sails against a softly coloured sky. The colouring belongs to the printed postcard; the spacing of the boats and the reflections belongs to the photograph. The result offers a quiet finish to a town album that began with an imminent departure.

Seen together, these cards give Summerside several centres of attention. Publishers liked its large buildings and its connection to the mainland. Read found a town worth recording street by street. Louson found people and pictures at the water’s edge. There is room in this Summerside for the traveller hurrying between train and steamer, and for the person who has the afternoon to sit with a fishing line.
