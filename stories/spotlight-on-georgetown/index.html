The history behind the picture

Spotlight on Georgetown

Georgetown knew how to make an entrance. The railway station on William Cumming’s postcard has a many-sided tower, round windows and a roof that spreads out over the platform. A line of men has assembled beneath it. They make the building feel like a place of welcome, with the town waiting just beyond the tracks.

The local merchant’s name is worth noticing. A. Maclean & Co. helped put Georgetown’s station, residences and waterfront into the postcard trade. These were views customers could send away as an introduction to the place. The surviving cards make their own short tour, beginning with arrival and following the streets down towards the water.

Room to see the town

In the “Sectional View,” houses gather beyond an open foreground of grass, fences and roads. Water fills the background. The photographer has found enough elevation to show how the town is put together while keeping individual houses readable. There is space between things here. Gardens, yards and patches of open ground are as much a part of the scene as the roofs.

The real-photo panorama on card 3281 takes us farther back. A curving railway line draws the eye towards the cluster of buildings at the water. The sender wrote across the sky, describing Georgetown as the Island’s winter port. Posted in April 1906, it offers a contemporary reminder of what that broad stretch of harbour meant to a traveller. Geography and transport are in the same frame.

Rail and water within reach

The two station views reward a comparison. Cumming stood close enough to make the tower and waiting people the subject. Carter’s photographer moved along the tracks, bringing a locomotive and carriages into the picture and catching GEORGETOWN in large letters across the roof. One presents a landmark; the other shows the landmark doing its job.

The same conjunction appears at the wharf on postcard 0986. Rails spread towards two steamers; freight cars and a cloud of steam fill the far end. The ships remain unidentified, but their relationship to the railway is plainly visible. Cargo could come down to the water along the tracks in front of us. The photograph is so full of small details that it has a story of its own. Here it gives the quieter town views their working waterfront.

Another card bears the modest title “The Ferry.” The camera looks down a street towards the landing and the opposite shore. Houses and gardens occupy far more of the photograph than the boat. That is part of its charm: the crossing belongs to the neighbourhood, at the end of a road someone walked every day.

At street level

Water Street’s RPPC gives us the town at human height. A man crosses the rough roadway in front of a large wooden building, while people farther along the street turn the distant part of the picture into a little gathering. The photograph is full of room for everyday movement. It is easy to linger over the storefronts, the fences and the figures at the doors.

Two other buildings show the range of Georgetown’s architectural ambitions. Lieutenant-Governor MacKinnon’s summer residence offers verandas, projecting bays and a pointed tower. The courthouse makes its statement through stonework. Designed by William Critchlow Harris and built in 1887, it combines red Island sandstone with grey Wallace stone. Its arches and banded walls look particularly crisp in the real-photo card mailed in 1924. The courthouse’s heritage record identifies that handsome combination.

A photograph to remember

The most sociable view may be the one labelled “Good Friday 1906.” People stand on a great slab of harbour ice, a flag raised beside one group. The handwritten question “Do you remember this one?” makes the photograph feel like a shared recollection. Someone had been there, or knew someone who had. It brings a burst of occasion into the album without a parade route or a grandstand.

The photographers returned to Georgetown’s station and harbour because they gave the town such recognisable forms. The RPPCs let people step into those forms: on the platform, crossing Water Street, out on the ice. Together they leave an impression of a well-connected harbour town with space to breathe and plenty worth sending home.
