The history behind the picture

Stone Cottage: Florence and Basil’s Life in Postcards

A view of the Dunk River opens a family album: Stone Cottage at Springfield, Florence’s letters, Basil’s calling to the ministry, and the photographs that followed them through their lives.

The Dunk River has a way of turning up in an Island postcard collection until it begins to feel like an old acquaintance. Card 3237 brings us particularly close. Along the bottom runs its handwritten title, “Dunk River (near Emerald) P.E. Island.” Turn it over and the landscape becomes a letter: Florence Haslam, writing from Stone Cottage, to the Rev. B. P. Colclough at Wycliffe College in Toronto.

She wrote on 26 October 1913; the Breadalbane postmark is dated the following day. Their wedding was still ahead of them. The river view belongs to a small, intimate world of Springfield roads, a stone house, gardens and family life preserved in this collection. Basil is the photographer associated with this intimate Island group; his later Ontario postcards carry his name in the photographic credit.

Between those two groups is a story of a young newcomer who meant to farm, a woman with deep Island roots, a changing vocation, and the pictures and messages that travelled with them.

A newcomer at Hazeldean

Basil left a remarkably useful account of his arrival. Writing to the Guardian from Newmarket in 1951, he recalled reaching Prince Edward Island in August 1907, travelling from England by way of Montreal. He remembered earlier years of travel and study in Europe. The Island’s red cultivated soil, hedges and pines had made a lasting impression. More than forty years later, he still wanted to return.

The postcards bring that recollection down to an address. Card 3223, written on 8 October 1907, went to Basil Ireland-Jones at Hazeldean, Springfield, Lot 67. That earlier surname continues through the correspondence to Wycliffe College in 1912. By 1913, Florence was addressing him as Colclough.

Hazeldean belonged to George and Lucy Haslam. Its heritage record identifies William Critchlow Harris as the architect and dates the beginning of construction to 1892. George was Florence’s brother. Stone Cottage, her family home, was a separate Haslam house. Both houses belonged to the family world Basil entered when he came to Springfield.

The house made from the farm

Stone Cottage was an Island house in a particularly literal sense. The Springfield history quoted by architectural historian Carter W. Jeffery says Robert Haslam built it with stone quarried on the farm. Jeffery dates the house to 1857. Hawthorn and spruce hedges helped shape the property around it. After Robert’s death, his widow and daughters remained there, while his son Fred built another house nearby.

Jeffery documented Stone Cottage in photographs and measured drawings in 2007 for the provincial Heritage Division. In August 2011, he recorded its demolition. The postcards now preserve a much earlier chapter of a house whose survival had seemed so substantial.

The 1906 Past and Present of Prince Edward Island lists fourteen children of Robert and Jane (Weeks) Haslam. Among them are Annie Florence, Clara Maud, Ada Alice, Harriet Caroline, Margaret Penelope, George Leslie and Robert Henry Albert. Those full names help us find our way among the Flos, Ada, Carrie, Pen and other familiar names appearing on the cards. The short, familiar names bring a large family into focus, one message at a time.

One photograph is particularly appealing: the card catalogued as 3216, with a woman feeding chickens and the caption “Flos.” The affectionate abbreviation and ordinary activity sit comfortably beside the views of the house. We are looking at a home people used every day.

Florence’s voice

Florence’s messages give the landscapes their human scale. On card 3213, a view titled “Main Road, Springfield,” she writes to Basil at Wycliffe in April 1912. The Breadalbane card, 3207, follows in May. Her letters fit around news from home and church, and close with affection. These are glimpses of a relationship sustained across a considerable distance.

By 6 May 1914, practical wedding arrangements were entering the correspondence. On card 3222 she discusses invitations and announcements and hopes Basil has told his parents the date. There is travel advice, too: he should book to Breadalbane because the road from Emerald was bad. A sandstone landscape carries the sort of instructions that make a journey—and a wedding—work.

The river postcard belongs between those stages. In October 1913, a view near Emerald travelled from Florence to the man she planned to marry. The view carries a little of home to Toronto, accompanied by Florence’s own words.

A Springfield wedding, with cables from India

The wedding report supplies the moment when all these threads meet. Annie Florence Haslam and Basil Philip Colclough married at three o’clock on Wednesday, 24 June 1914, at St. Elizabeth’s Church, Springfield. George Haslam of Hazeldean escorted his sister. Clara Balderston was maid of honour; Lionel Pengelly supported Basil. The reception followed at Stone Cottage.

The report identifies Basil as the eldest son of Rev. P. Ireland Jones of Karachi. Cables brought congratulations from his parents and from Florence’s brother in India. It also says her grandfather, Thomas Haslam, had built the church in 1849 in memory of his wife Elizabeth. The bride’s family history was present in both the church and the reception house.

From farming plans to the ministry

Arch. MacKenzie, who knew the family, later explained that Basil had come to the Island intending to farm. He entered Wycliffe and the ministry instead. By September 1913, the Guardian placed him in church work between Lesser Slave Lake and Peace River Crossing, in the Diocese of Athabasca. The same item announced his intended marriage the following June.

After the wedding, their journey led west. A July 1914 notice records the couple leaving Springfield, with George and Mrs. Haslam and Muriel accompanying them as far as Pointe-du-Chêne. Montreal and the Athabasca diocese lay ahead.

MacKenzie’s later account says pneumonia and pleurisy brought Basil’s western work to an end. By 1916, he was rector at Milton. The family cards follow him there: in October, Ruth wrote to “Uncle Basil” about getting an organ packed and shipped. They needed rope, and help with the lifting. In February 1917, a message to Florence tried to straighten out a parcel containing the wrong footwear. Ministry and household life continued together.

Basil served at Milton from 1916 to April 1919, then moved to Arichat, Nova Scotia. An appointment to Christ Church, Stellarton, followed, beginning in February 1920. The 1920 appointment notice also records his Wycliffe training and a theology qualification from King’s College, Windsor, in October 1919.

Richard Ruggle’s clergy directory extends the trail through Halifax and Ontario and records university study and teaching. It lists Basil as a teacher in Newmarket in 1945. His working life embraced education as well as parish ministry. Newmarket became the home address on his postcards and the setting for a fresh body of photographs.

Clara’s letter, and the photographs travelling home

Florence’s sister Clara Maud Haslam sent Basil the West River view on card 3209. Her pleasure at receiving his “letter containing snaps” takes us straight into the family’s photographic life. Photographs were being enclosed, received and enjoyed, keeping people close across the distance between their homes.

Clara’s card was addressed to the rectory at Milton. The picture she chose carries another photographer’s credit, G. G. Hughes, while her message belongs to the Haslam–Colclough story. A postcard could hold both worlds at once: a landscape worth looking at, and a family conversation worth keeping.

Other messages fill in the household around them. The harbour view below carried Ruth’s request for help packing an organ. The Earl Grey in the ice carried news of a muddled parcel of footwear. Familiar Island scenes travelled with very particular family business.

The photographer signs his work

In Ontario, Basil’s name becomes part of the pictures themselves. Eleven cards in the collection visibly carry a “Photo by B. P. Colclough, Newmarket, Ont.” credit. They range from a brook and bridge on Lorne Avenue to churches, snowy landscapes and Tobermory harbour.

The variety is revealing. Religious buildings belonged among his subjects, alongside water, trees, streets and changing seasons. From the Dunk to a Newmarket brook, water and trees keep drawing the eye. The Ontario views extend the pleasure of the Island group into another landscape.

These eleven credited cards are held in the collection. Open any image to visit its postcard record.

The Island stayed with them

The later correspondence continues into the 1950s. Family news still travelled between the Island and Newmarket; Grace appears among the recipients. Lorne Avenue, where Basil photographed the brook and bridge, was also the street given in his final address.

Their later years had considerable hardship. In January 1952, MacKenzie appealed to Island readers on their behalf. Basil had a heart condition, while Florence had been seriously disabled after a hip injury. He was caring for her himself. The appeal asked for the comfort of letters and for practical support. After years of postcards keeping this family close, correspondence itself had become a form of care.

Basil died at York County Hospital, Newmarket, on 31 May 1955. His obituary remembered his Milton ministry and named Florence, their daughter Grace and their son John, then in India. The death notice gives 33 Lorne Avenue as his home.

Return to the Dunk River card with that life in mind. Its quiet water belongs to Florence’s home country, and its message belongs to their years of anticipation. Around it sit the stone house, the road, the chickens, the church, and the later Ontario photographs. Together they allow us to follow a family through the places they loved—and to hear them speaking to one another.

Sources and the photographs

The story follows the collection’s original fronts and handwritten backs. The Springfield and Dunk River photographs are attributed to Basil through the Haslam–Colclough family group; the eleven Ontario cards shown here visibly credit B. P. Colclough. Commercial postcards sent among the family are identified through their messages. Clara’s signature and her identity as Florence’s sister have been confirmed by the collector.

The chief contemporary sources are Basil’s own recollection, the wedding report, parish notices and the later accounts of the couple. They can be read through Island Newspapers:
