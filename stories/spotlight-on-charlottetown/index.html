The history behind the picture

Spotlight on Charlottetown

On Queen Street, even the roofs have something to sell. SEEDS announces Carter’s business high above the pavement; BOOK STORE and WALL PAPER occupy the wall below. Along the neighbouring frontage are millinery, dry goods and furs. A bicycle leans outside, and a figure crosses the wide, rough street. The photographer has given the shops enough room for us to read them. More than a century later, we can still go window-shopping.

Charlottetown’s early postcards have a generous idea of what makes a city worth showing. Its photographers returned to the public buildings and church spires, but they also watched the market, followed processions and stood beside tennis courts. Flowerbeds receive as much care in some compositions as the architecture behind them. These printed views and real-photo cards take us through the city of the 1900s and early 1910s, from the steamer’s gangway to a shaded place to sit.

Up from the water

The Northumberland fills its postcard almost to the edges. Smoke streams from the funnel; the gangway descends into a foreground crowded with people, horses and wheeled equipment. The camera stands down among the activity on the wharf. It is an excellent introduction to a port city: before any grand building comes into view, there is the business of getting ashore.

Great George Street gives the visitor a long sightline between the waterfront and Queen Square. In the Haszard & Moore view looking south, the road narrows towards the water, with trees on one side and the cathedral’s spires on the other. A bench and small figures occupy the foreground. The photographer has made a broad city street feel like an invitation to walk.

There is a reason so many Charlottetown pictures have such satisfying lines. In his history of the city plan, Reg Porter follows the development of the street grid and its public squares through the surveys of Charles Morris and Thomas Wright. His maps help explain what the postcard cameras inherited: long streets, open spaces and prominent places for civic buildings. The photographers could make a composition simply by choosing where to stand within that arrangement.

A capital with a garden at its feet

Province House is the natural centrepiece. Its columns and pediment give the postcard a firm, orderly shape, while people and flowers bring the foreground to life. Completed in 1847 and the setting for the Charlottetown Conference in 1864, it already carried a considerable history when this copy went through the post in 1908. The national historic site record places it at the centre of Queen Square and at one end of the Great George Street vista.

Turn towards Victoria Row and the city’s commercial ambitions make an equally handsome picture. The buildings offer a succession of different rooflines and window shapes above the trees. The garden and fountain let the camera put something delightful between the viewer and the shops. That combination recurs throughout the collection: substantial buildings softened by planting, a capital that has made room for flowers.

J. A. S. Bayer’s Queen Square Gardens RPPC shifts our attention to the people using that space. A line of benches runs towards the distant buildings, with lamps rising above the paths. You can imagine choosing a seat before you begin identifying the architecture. The printed credit beneath the photograph names Bayer Photo Studio; this is one of the city views that can be connected directly to a local photographic business.

The city gathers

At the market, the camera has plenty of company. Horses, loaded wagons and small groups of people spread across the square in postcard 3794. Some stand facing the photographer; others have business elsewhere. The open ground matters as much as the buildings. It is the space that allows the town and the surrounding country to meet.

William Steele Louson chose a higher vantage point for “Closing Public Schools.” Rows of children fill the space beside the Market Building, their pale clothes forming bright bands across the photograph. The caption announces 1,200 children present. That elevated camera makes the size and arrangement of the gathering visible, while leaving individual figures to reward a closer look.

Bayer came much closer for the Odd Fellows’ gathering in August 1908. In this frame from his sequence, musicians march past while boys walk alongside, near enough for their expressions and clothes to become part of the event. A street used for shopping has become a place to watch a procession. The wider set, including the carriage photograph that began this collection, has its own story. Here the boys give us a particularly cheerful way into a formal occasion.

After working hours

One of the nicest explanations of why anyone bought a city postcard is on the back of Pownal Street. On May 19, 1909, Fred told Jim in Boston that he had been to Carter’s bookstore that day, seen this card and decided to send it. Then he explained his choice: “This is where I am most of the time after working hours.”

That takes us back to the bookshop on Queen Street. The cards on its counter could offer a grand public building or a street someone knew intimately. Fred chose the latter. The photographer’s long view, with the Kirk above the houses and utility poles receding into the distance, had preserved a piece of his ordinary day.

An afternoon to spare

Victoria Park gave photographers a different kind of abundance. Established in 1873, it gained a shore roadway opened in 1897 and extended around to Brighton Road in 1903. The park’s heritage record describes those improvements, which helped make the shoreline such an inviting place for both visitors and cameras.

The pavilion in the card signed Wallace Photo is dressed for an occasion: flags, draped fabric and shields decorate its open sides. A man stands beside the steps, while a small figure at the right has blurred in motion. The photograph preserves both the effort of dressing the place and the people moving through it. Mailed in July 1912, the card has the air of an afternoon someone wanted to remember.

Bayer’s tennis picture finds its interest in movement. Four players occupy a grass court, with the nearest pair turned away from us and the white lines drawing the eye towards the net. He has put the viewer almost on the court. City life includes the pleasure of having somewhere to play, and someone thought that pleasure was worth making into a postcard.

Looking back towards town

The battery offered Bayer another useful camera position. On postcard 0477 the guns form a diagonal across the foreground, with Charlottetown’s roofs and spires stretched beyond them. The buildings we have been walking among become a skyline. It is an economical city portrait: a distinctive foreground, water and open space, then the familiar town.

Clayton Campbell wrote around that picture in 1905, thanking Lois Morrison for her postcard and offering to exchange again. Charlottetown was going out into the world one view at a time. The city in these cards has places to arrive, things to buy, neighbours to meet and afternoons to enjoy. Its photographers found a handsome capital; they also left us the bicycle outside the shop, the boys beside the band and Fred’s familiar street.
