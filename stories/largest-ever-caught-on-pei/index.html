The history behind the picture

PEI’s Greatest Postcards – #8 – Largest Ever Caught on P.E. Island

Fourteen pounds in one hand, twelve in the other. The fisherman holds the lobsters by their tails. Somebody put the weights on the picture down by his knees, just where I’d like to put a scale. A man could send that postcard halfway around the world without writing another word and the recipient would know exactly what to look at. For the name of the man doing the lifting I’d happily give up some of the lettering.

The number on the other side is almost as helpful as the numbers on the front. Pugh Manufacturing of Toronto numbered it 898-25. Another copy in the collection, 3523, has the same number and the same type across the fisherman’s trousers. The man, his moustache, his cap, the lobster claws against his dark clothes and even the lettering sit in the same places. An independent witness to the weights would be a different matter. For now 14 and 12 are what Pugh’s picture tells us. The harbour, the date of the catch and the whereabouts of any larger lobster are all beyond the edge of the card.

There is one date we can recover. My first copy has no postal message. The second one passed through Kensington on July 3, 1911. It was addressed to Central Bedeque. The writer intended to come down on Thursday if it was fine and asked the recipient to be ready. Then comes the line that I suspect anyone who has tried to write a letter on a hot summer evening will understand.

Too hot to write any more. In haste.

That little arrangement between two Island households dates the printed image more securely than any guess based on the man’s clothes. It was available to mail by that Monday in 1911. It also gives us two quite different measures of the postcard’s value. The front made an extravagant promise about the whole Island. The back got word to one person about Thursday.

Where the rest of the catch went

A second picture takes us down to the shore beside the factory buildings. It is printed “Lobster Factory, Prince Edward Island.” Howard’s Cove is the location in my catalogue. The distinction matters if somebody recognizes the buildings and has a named view to compare. The photographer included the water behind the roofs, boats offshore and a tall chimney. This is a better picture of the distance between a landing and a factory than of any individual catch.

The problem for a nineteenth-century Island exporter was what to do with a perishable animal between hauling the trap and finding a distant buyer. Canning changed the distance a catch could travel. The Island advertised lobster to British buyers at the international exhibition in London in 1862. The province’s account of the Basin Head Cannery puts the number of Island canneries at two in 1873, about a hundred in 1883 and 246 in 1900. A fishery a man could enter with a small boat and traps came to depend on a considerable workforce ashore. Somebody had to bring in the catch, cook it, take the meat out and get the tins ready to ship. PEI’s account of the Basin Head Cannery · The Island fishery and its markets

Canning could even enter the description of a place. PECO published a view across the water at Mount Stewart as number 69 and called the village “a noted co-operative canning settlement.” In a picture of houses, trees and open water the choice of caption is almost the only clue to what the publisher wanted a visitor to know about the local economy. Two copies survive here with different colours in the sky. One writer begins “Hello old kid.” The other reached the mail at St. Eleanor’s in June 1959 with news of a safe car trip. The caption tells us how the publisher presented the settlement. The later sender dates the use of that particular copy. Mount Stewart, 0985 · The copy mailed in 1959

The sender of the Howard’s Cove card had another use for a lobster factory. It was the only card on hand, the writer explained, and there was a Monday visit to arrange if weather and health permitted. So I have one visit proposed on the back of a pair of extraordinary lobsters and another on the back of a factory. The man holding up the catch has earned his place at number eight. Turn over his card, though, and even the largest lobster on the Island has to share the billing with Thursday’s plans.
