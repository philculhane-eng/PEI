The history behind the picture

Spotlight on Tignish

There is a wonderful place to begin a visit to postcard Tignish: behind the post-office counter. A man stands beside a desk and scales, with sorting compartments filling the wall behind him. Bundles and papers occupy the work surfaces. On the back of the card, J. A. Brennan has written his name and occupation: Postmaster, Tignish, P.E.I.

Doug Murray’s history of the Dominion Post Office at Tignish, published in the Postal History Society of Canada’s journal in 2008, identifies J. Albert Brennan as the new building’s first postmaster. His term ran from July 15, 1912, to March 25, 1916. That makes the signed card a particularly personal introduction to the early years of the office. The photograph shows the room in use; the handwriting connects it to the person responsible for the service.

The town around the tower

Outside, the new post office was a building to be proud of. Its corner tower and high roof rise above the surrounding frontage. Built in 1911–12 to David Ewart’s design, it gave Tignish an unmistakable public landmark. Murray’s account follows its construction and even the machinery of its four-faced clock. Put the exterior and interior cards together and the grand building becomes a workplace, handling the mail that kept people in touch.

Chapel Street gives the town another organising line. In the colour postcard, houses and commercial buildings run along one side of the road, while people stand on the sidewalk at the right. The church spire rises behind the nearer roofs. This copy was mailed in September 1909, before the new post office joined the skyline.

A community’s landmarks

St. Simon and St. Jude was already a familiar presence by the postcard years. Built in 1859–60 to a design by Patrick Charles Keely, the brick church drew on the labour and materials of the parish. The heritage record credits young parishioners with helping supply the lime, sand and bricks. Its great spire gave photographers a landmark visible well beyond the church grounds.

The convent adds education to that landscape. Opened in 1868, it brought the Sisters of the Congregation of Notre Dame to a school serving girls in the community and boarders from farther away. The congregation’s own archival history follows the school’s work. On the postcard, the rows of windows and the trees at the front make it a quieter companion to the church’s soaring outline.

Faces at the store, families at home

G. A. Shelfoon’s store has a particularly engaging portrait. The camera faces the gabled building squarely. People stand in the doorway and beside the windows; the upper storeys give the business a domestic scale. The printer managed “Tiguish” in the caption, but the shop itself receives careful attention. It is a place with people at the door, ready to meet the viewer.

Reg Porter later included a postcard of Shelfoon’s store in his Tignish Arts Foundation image catalogue, recording Helen Shea as his informant in the summer of 1971. His work gives this kind of shop view a second life: a commercial souvenir became part of the community’s effort to remember its own streets.

The “Pioneer Family, Five Generations” postcard makes people the landmark. Adults stand behind the seated older generation and a child, with lobster traps piled beside them. The publisher’s title celebrates the family’s continuity. The traps place that portrait among the objects of everyday work. This copy was already in the mail in February 1906.

Land, shore and the way out

A “Harvest Scene” puts horses and harvesting machinery into a field, with a church beyond. At Shea’s Beach, a figure stands alone at the water’s edge beneath a low cliff. These two cards were mailed in 1910 and 1911. Together they expand the town album into the farms and shores around it, places from which people came into Tignish and places a visitor might go out to see.

The railway provides the closing view, carrying us into the following decade. In this RPPC, the locomotive faces the camera beside a group on the platform. Reg Porter’s catalogue entry for the same station view dates the original to 1920–30. It is a fitting later glimpse of a town whose postcards had long been travelling outward by mail.

The church and post office gave Tignish impressive silhouettes. The store, family group and postal interior bring the town close. There is particular pleasure in ending where the visit began: with mail being weighed and sorted, and with J. Albert Brennan putting his own workplace on a postcard for someone in Rhode Island.
