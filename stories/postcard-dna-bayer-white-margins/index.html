The history behind the picture

The DNA of P.E.I. Postcards, Part Three: Bayer’s Name in the Margin

A small photograph, a fine black frame, a title in neat lettering, and an expanse of pale card. At the end of the caption, almost easy to overlook: “Photo Bayer.” Put a few of these postcards together and a family begins to emerge.

The search began with eleven cards. Their subjects range from the golfers at Belvedere to Charlottetown’s churches, college, public buildings and waterfront battery. They have a quiet, spacious appearance. Several leave almost as much room for a message as they give to the picture.

Looking closely brings the eleven cards down to nine distinct photographs. The college appears twice. The guns at Fort Edward also appear twice, under different titles. Each of the nine photographs carries a Bayer credit somewhere in this starting group.

Then the names start disappearing. The same Kirk turns up without “Photo Bayer.” So does the Market Building. So do the public buildings and Great George Street. Alongside them sit other views with a very similar appearance, waiting to be compared.

This third instalment of the postcard DNA series follows that trail in three stages: the signed photographs, the unsigned companions, and the lives of the images beyond this group. The galleries run alphabetically by subject, with “A” and “The” set aside. Every postcard opens its full collection record.

1. The signed set

Here is one representative of each photograph. Duplicate copies and alternate titles are linked beneath the pictures. These are printed picture postcards; Bayer’s name is a photographic credit. The business that printed and issued this particular group remains to be identified.

Even within this little group, the photographer’s name travels with different titles. Card 0477 looks across the guns toward Charlottetown and calls the scene “Charlottetown … from Battery Point, Victoria Park.” On 2157, the same photograph becomes “Fort Edward, Victoria Park.” Both give Bayer his credit. A collector searching by title alone could easily count them as different views.

The two “Public Buildings” pictures require the opposite treatment. Their captions are almost identical, but one looks across Queen Square from above, while the other brings us down to the street among pedestrians and bare trees. They preserve two separate moments. The photograph is the unit that holds this enquiry together; titles and catalogue numbers help us find its different appearances.

Room for a message

The pale spaces are part of the pleasure of these cards. They give the picture a border, and they give the sender somewhere to speak. On 4 December 1905, Clayton Campbell sent the Battery Point view to Miss Lois Morrison in Shelburne, Nova Scotia. He thanked her for a postcard and offered to exchange again, then signed his name across the lower margin. The guns are still visible above his handwriting; below them is a little surviving piece of the collecting network that carried Island pictures away.

Another correspondent made a rather less enthusiastic choice. On the signed Kirk card, dated 5 November 1905 and mailed the following day, the writer explained that upright views were scarce. The photograph’s proportions mattered to someone choosing a card for an exchange. More than a century later, that practical detail helps us imagine the selection available at a shop counter.

The unsigned Market Building card brings us closer to everyday use: “This is where I go to market every Tuesday and Friday.” Its writer also mentions the opening of school in September. A building that can look rather formal in an architectural photograph becomes a place in someone’s weekly routine.

The backs add another layer. The signed cards use at least two distinct heading designs: a flowing “Private Postal Card” and a more elaborate “Private Post Card” surrounded by scrollwork. Ink colours vary as well. The group’s resemblance is strongest on the fronts; the reverse designs suggest editions or supplies that deserve further sorting.

2. The unsigned companions

First, photographs we already know

Six of the nine signed photographs have close unsigned companions in a similarly spacious monochrome format. These are the most useful answers to the original hunch. Their relationship rests on the actual image: the same branches, people, vehicles, foreground details and camera position.

The changes extend beyond the missing credit. A title may be shortened, a picture enlarged, a border moved or a reverse reset. The gallery shows those differences alongside the shared photograph. It gives us a firm core of image relationships from which to explore the wider family.

The cathedral and bishop’s residence also survive in unsigned monochrome editions, including 4422 and 3171. Their larger picture and plain, undivided “Private Post Card” backs move them into a different presentation. The golfing scene takes a more colourful route on 0178. Both subjects return in the image histories below.

Then, possible additions with new views

Here are twelve new-view candidates. Each shares several features with the signed group: the fine frame, the small caption, the pale writing space or one of the familiar reverse layouts. Some fit very closely; others stretch the format through an oval picture, a double view or a smaller margin.

These are proposed companions. Their unsigned photographs still need individual attribution. The gallery records the reasons for bringing each into the comparison, along with duplicate copies and other editions that can help us follow it.

Several of these candidates form little families of their own. St Paul’s repeats in three closely related unsigned cards. The oval Prince Street residence appears in four. The ice boats have two principal titles, while the view through Queen Square toward Zion Church changes emphasis according to its caption. That repetition makes the search more useful than a gathering of vaguely similar borders.

The oval residence offers a particularly pleasing connection with the people who used these cards. 0450 went from Clayton Campbell to the same Miss Lois Morrison who received his signed Battery Point view. He described the house as one of Charlottetown’s best residences and promised to try to obtain “painted ones” next time. The two cards belonged to the same exchange. His promise also reminds us that monochrome and colour views were choices a collector could make.

A shared reverse can lead us toward a printer or distributor as readily as toward a photographer. An older image such as “Queen Square, fifty years ago” makes that especially apparent: somebody selected and reproduced an existing scene. A period order sheet, advertisement or printer’s sample could eventually give this working group a firmer boundary.

Four further views to keep alongside the comparison

These have useful similarities, but their layouts are less close to the signed core. They remain a separate group of possibilities while the search continues.

Other pale-margin cards use substantially different backs or explicitly name another producer. Card 2552, for example, says it was printed at the office of The Prince Edward Island Magazine. Generous white space was available to many postcard makers; the combination of details is what makes a proposed connection worth pursuing.

3. Where the photographs travelled

Once a photograph has been recognized, it can be followed through changes that initially disguise it. Colour is added. A caption moves into the sky. A border disappears. An image is cropped into a narrower panel, or put beside a company’s advertisement. The printed name on the card changes while a tree branch, a person’s pose or a line of carriages carries the original scene forward.

The nine signed photographs all have another printed appearance in the collection. For some, the trail reaches several named publishers; for the street-level public buildings view, the companion located so far is the unsigned card in the gallery above. From cathedral view to medicine card

The bishop’s residence and cathedral offer an unusually vivid example of reuse. On 3908, the view sits quietly above Bayer’s name. On 4508, the lower part of the card announces Johnson’s Anodyne Liniment and calls it “The Canadian Family Medicine.” The image has become an advertising vehicle.

The advertising card gives the lower margin a new job. The buildings, trees and road still let us recognize the photograph. A surviving credit on another edition restores a piece of its history that the advertisement leaves unstated.

Three photographs in a Carter album

The golf links, college and Market Building lead into the same book: Charlottetown: The Beautiful City of Prince Edward Island, issued by Carter & Co. Its title page says the photographs were furnished by W. S. Louson. It also advertises duplicate five-by-seven-inch photographs, obtainable through Carter for thirty cents.

The individual cards credit Bayer; the album credits Louson for supplying its photographs. Together, they place these images within a wider trade in photographic prints and printed souvenirs. The precise arrangements between photographer, supplier and publisher remain a subject for the Bayer–Louson investigation.

The album’s printed title page gives no year. Internet Archive catalogues it as 1903, while Harry Holman’s study places Carter’s Louson-supplied souvenir volumes in 1904. The postcard postmarks provide firmer dates for particular surviving objects. We can follow the same picture into both forms while leaving their order of production open.

The unsigned views have travelling lives too

St Paul’s supplies a particularly easy comparison. Its distinctive foreground tree runs through the unsigned pale-margin card, Carter’s colour version and Tuck’s version. The oval Prince Street residence acquires a name on Haszard’s “Fairholm” card. Each added edition supplies information about the image or its commercial life.

The Montague River view makes a more dramatic change. On 0921, the picture is tall and narrow, with a small figure near its lower edge. Taylor’s colour card keeps that arrangement. The Warwick Brothers & Rutter sample card takes a horizontal section through the river and trees. Recognizing the crop brings three rather different-looking objects back to the same photograph.

The Montrose scene likewise passes into Warwick Brothers & Rutter and Taylor editions. A photograph from the upper half of Pastoral Scenery appears on a Prince Edward Island Development and Tourist Association card. The association’s invitation to request further illustrated literature gives the rural view another purpose: attracting interest in the Island.

At the beach, the same seated figures and parasols appear in Haszard’s Cavendish view and Carter’s “What are the Wild Waves Saying?”. The writer of 0913, mailed in October 1905, said the original photograph had been taken at the North Shore about two years earlier and marked a pair of figures with an X. That is a personal recollection preserved on the card, and a useful lead for the photograph’s history.

Other candidates have a shorter trail so far. The ice-boat scene has been located under two titles within this broad family. The “Queen Square, fifty years ago” view has yet to yield another matched edition in this search. Each result belongs to the current collection: a newly acquired card, a catalogue or an illustrated book could extend it.

Two anglers, three place names, and a Louson credit

One of the more loosely related cards gives the search a particularly useful turn. 4254 calls its scene “Fishing at Dunk River.” The fine frame, small caption and flowing reverse make it worth comparing with the pale-margin family. Follow the actual photograph, however, and a named photographer appears on another edition: “Photo by W. S. Louson.”

That credit belongs to Warwick Brothers & Rutter’s “Trout Fishing, Souris”. The anglers hold the same poses, the rods follow the same lines, and the fallen timber lies in the same places across the water. The image also appears in Taylor’s colour edition and an ornamentally shaped Dunk River card.

Then the caption sends the anglers much farther away: 1821 places the same photograph on the Humber River in Toronto. The people and riverbank stay put while the printed geography travels. Establishing the actual location will require evidence beyond these competing titles.

For the present enquiry, the Louson credit is the significant discovery. It supplies an attribution for one unsigned image while keeping the possible printing-family connection in view. A wider group of these cards may have drawn on photographs from more than one source. The surviving credits give us a way to examine that possibility one image at a time.

The photographic postcards still to find

The search through the catalogued fronts has so far produced no confirmed RPPC counterpart for the nine signed photographs. Their printed relatives are plentiful. Their possible lives as photographic postcards remain an inviting next stage.

Bayer did issue photographic postcards of several of these subjects. The college card 1870, for example, shows a snowy setting and a different view of the building. It belongs in the larger Bayer story, while the signed pale-margin college image follows the particular figure, trees and camera position preserved on 4039, 4141 and 2532.

That is what makes this kind of collecting so absorbing. A tiny name in a margin can lead to six unsigned image companions, a wider group of possible subjects, several publishers and a souvenir book. Meanwhile, the messages bring the cards back to the people who chose them: someone looking for an upright view, someone promising a colour card, someone explaining where they went to market twice a week. The photographs travelled through businesses; the postcards travelled between people.

Sources and how the group was assembled

The principal evidence is the collection itself: printed captions and credits, front and reverse designs, postmarks, messages and comparisons of the same photographic details. This November 2026 pass searched the 4,694 catalogued fronts for image relationships, compared back designs, and visually reviewed the resulting matches and pale-margin candidates. Computer matches were used to locate possibilities; the examples discussed here were checked visually.

The signed core contains eleven cards representing nine photographs. Six photographs have close unsigned companions in the spacious monochrome format. Twelve additional subjects are proposed as companions, with four more loosely related views retained separately. These are research groupings; a publisher’s original list may eventually establish their boundaries.

The Carter album is available through Internet Archive. Its title page and scans 24, 34 and 36 support the comparisons here. For the history and dating of Carter’s souvenir publications, see Harry Holman, Our Quiet But Engaging Scenery, The Island Magazine 80 (2016), through Island Archives.

Dates on used cards establish that those copies existed by the time of mailing. The order in which different publishers issued an image, the printer behind the pale-margin cards, and the attribution of the unsigned new subjects remain open research questions.
