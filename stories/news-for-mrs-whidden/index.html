A Little Island News for Mrs. Whidden

There are faces inside every letter of ALBERTON, but the people I’d most like to meet are on the other side of this card. Mrs. Hooper has been buried. Aunt Martha is poorly. Somebody has been offered work and has made up their mind to take it. Mrs. Whidden, over in Shubenacadie, Nova Scotia, was evidently expected to know everybody and everything. We’re the ones arriving 120 years late and trying to catch up.

Here’s how I read the first part, with punctuation supplied:

Dear Mrs. W., Mrs. Hooper was buried Wednesday. Aunt Martha is very poorly. Love to all.

No introduction, and no first name for Mrs. Hooper. Even Wednesday can stand on its own. The writer seems to sign “Lily W.” Immediately below comes “I will accept that work,” with what looks like a second signature. Two people may have shared the card and its one-cent postage. I can’t read that last name with confidence or tell what the work was. I like the economy of the definite article: that work. The important part of this conversation had already taken place.

The name the writer left out

The address gives us more to go on than the salutation: Mrs. R. S. Whidden. Annie Jane Whidden, wife of the Rev. Richard Smith Whidden, is a good match. The North Bedeque Presbyterian church records list him among the ministers who served there, with his ministry ending in 1906. The couple’s family record names Annie Jane. Put the two together and there’s a reason for this particular bit of Island news to travel to Nova Scotia. People who had known the minister’s family would hardly have needed to explain who was ill or which family had lost someone. The address tells us where this piece of mail was going, though not how long Mrs. Whidden had been there. North Bedeque church records · The Whidden family record.

And there is a particularly interesting Mrs. Hooper. In the Presbyterian cemetery at North Bedeque, the family stone carries Alexander A. Hooper’s name on the front. Turn to the side and there is Isabella Cole, his wife, who died on July 31, 1906, aged 83. The 1901 census places her in Lower Bedeque with her son Nelson. July 31 fell on a Tuesday; the next day was Wednesday, August 1. The catalogue reads the cancellation on the postcard as August 5. The community, the surname and the week all fit. The stone records when Isabella died; the card tells us when an unnamed Mrs. Hooper was buried. Until a funeral notice or burial register connects the two, I’d keep Isabella as the best lead. Aunt Martha remains Aunt Martha. Read Isabella’s inscription in David A. Walker’s photograph.

Where did the greeting come from?

One last trap: ALBERTON is a very large word on a rather small postcard. It tells us which souvenir someone chose, but it needn’t tell us where they bought the stamp or handed over the card. On the reverse I can make out the beginning of Charlottetown and the letters “M.C.” on the rim of the postmark. Those letters stand for Mail Car. Mail could be sorted and cancelled aboard the railway. The ink where the rest of the route and the date meet the stamp is much harder to read. There was a Charlottetown–Tignish mail car, but I wouldn’t fill in the missing part of this strike from that fact alone. Compare a Charlottetown–Tignish mail-car postmark.

There’s a second copy of this very design in the collection, and it makes the point beautifully. On 3506 the note is headed Campbellton, October 12, 1907. The postmark is Lot 14, and the destination is Brockton, Massachusetts. Someone even used the white strip below ALBERTON to put in a few more words. On a different Alberton greeting, 4398, Frieda asks after Norman and wants Maude to write her “a good long letter.” The first Mrs. Whidden got considerably less than that. But for one cent she got the news of a burial, word of an ailing aunt, love from home and an answer she’d been waiting for.
