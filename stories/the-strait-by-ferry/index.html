The history behind the picture

The Strait by Ferry

On July 24, 1907, Bert and Charlie set off on their wedding trip. Someone kept a picture of the Northumberland and wrote the occasion on the back. In February 1913 Owen picked out a picture of the Earl Grey to tell Win why he was still in Charlottetown. And on a card showing the ferry Prince Edward Island at Pictou, someone signing as Teddie drew kisses around three edges and asked Daddy to come home. Three ferries, and three quite different reasons for wanting to remember the boat.

Which boat you took depended on where you started, where you wanted to go and, for a good many years, the state of the ice. This album follows fourteen ships, beginning with the winter steamers and ending aboard the original Abegweit. Choose a ship below, or read straight through. Each photograph opens its catalogue card. Where a ship has more cards, unfold its album for additional views and copies with other messages.

One return ticket, two crossings

Stanley

Winter service from 1888

Start near Pictou, with the horse. A horse and sleigh and a line of people stand out on the ice beside the Stanley. The photographer was far enough away to get both forms of transport into the frame. Now look at the broadside view near Georgetown. Ice rises so high against the hull that the funnel and masts are much easier to pick out than the waterline. What did a passenger about to make that trip think? Maria, sending the broadside, ventured that she wouldn’t fancy being aboard.

The Stanley entered the Strait service in 1888. She was 207 feet long, had 1,900 horsepower and bore the name of the same governor general who gave hockey the Stanley Cup. Islanders had already seen the steam icebreaker Northern Light. The Stanley improved the chances of making a winter passage, particularly on the Georgetown–Pictou run. In bad stretches the mail still went across at the capes in boats hauled by hand. Large and small boats belonged to the same winter. Marine Atlantic’s history of the Stanley · The iceboat crossing

Minto

Georgetown and Pictou from 1899

In the Georgetown picture the Minto faces the camera bow-on. There are people out on both sides of her, with room enough for a photographer to stand in front. The caption supplies a place and a season, “Winter, 1905.” An uncoloured salesman’s sample of this same photograph survives as card 3345. You can see what the publisher had to work with before a blue sky and the touches of colour on the coats went in. The Georgetown sample

Introduced in 1899, the Minto carried passengers, freight and mail. Her steel hull and 2,900 horsepower gave the government another way of keeping Georgetown in touch with Pictou. One correspondent at Pictou, writing to his wife in November 1908, had come for coal and expected to go out to a wreck that night. To explain the ship in his picture he simply called her the boat that broke the ice all winter. There’s another Minto in this album dressed with signal flags in Charlottetown Harbour. It’s a pleasure to see her somewhere other than up to her rails in white. Marine Atlantic’s history of the Minto

Earl Grey

The winter passenger service from 1909

Friday, February 28, 1913. Owen had finished his business on the Island. The Earl Grey was due from Nova Scotia and he meant to take her back the next day, then carry on to Toronto. A telegram reached him first. Ice and a heavy snowstorm had stopped her coming over. He reckoned he might be in Charlottetown until Monday, the very day he had hoped to be in Toronto. He underlined the word rotten. The telegraph had crossed the water. Owen still had to wait for the boat.

Charles Duguid designed the Earl Grey for the Island’s winter passengers. Built in 1909, she also served as a vice-regal yacht. In July 1912 the navy temporarily commissioned her for the Duke of Connaught’s cruise around the Gulf of St. Lawrence and the Maritime Provinces. Card 2916 catches a different duty. The small dark figures between the two steamers are people crossing the ice. The sender identifies the Earl Grey as the boat they had travelled on and explains that she had gone to the Minto’s relief. The photograph is marked February 25, 1914. That October a Canadian naval crew took the Earl Grey overseas for transfer to Russia. A ship Owen had waited for at Charlottetown went on to work in the Arctic. Northumberland

Summer passages to Pictou and Pointe-du-Chêne

Put the winter pictures aside for a moment. In open water a traveller could go from Charlottetown to Pictou, or from Summerside to Pointe-du-Chêne near Shediac. The Northumberland worked both routes during her Island career, from 1891 to 1919. A caption placing her at Summerside and another advertising her daily Charlottetown–Pictou trip can both be right. Her dark hull, single funnel and long passenger accommodation are easier to follow through the album than the sky, which changes colour according to the printer. Marine Atlantic’s history of the Northumberland

On the back of the green Pictou view someone wrote down that this was the steamer Bert and Charlie took on their wedding trip. There’s no address and no postmark. The date of their departure was worth saving all by itself. Another sender, Harry, remembered seeing Ivan and Bessie for a few minutes at Pictou. By contrast, the close wharf photograph gives us the crowd, the smoke and the cargo, and scarcely a clue to anybody’s name. Those two short notes give a sense of what a few minutes at the steamer could mean. Harry’s note about the meeting at Pictou

Empress

Summerside and Pointe-du-Chêne from 1906

At Summerside there was a roof over the railway platform and precious little distance between the train and the water. The words printed across one view of the Empress are “All Ashore!” There is a train on the right and the steamer on the left. Look down the length of the wharf in the wider view and you can see why a missed train or a late boat mattered to everyone at the other end.

The Empress entered Island service in 1906. She could accommodate 500 passengers and even carried a post office. For ten years she served the Strait. In 1916 she went to work between Saint John and Digby, which accounts for the Bay of Fundy captions among her cards. One traveller needed no caption at all. Across the back of the Summerside view they wrote that they were crossing on this very steamer and having a fine trip. The two careers of the Empress

Enterprise

From Montague by way of Georgetown and Murray Harbour

Then what is the “New Steamer Empress” doing in Montague? Turn to the view framed by two tree trunks. Harry Holman identified this particular Warwick Brothers & Rutter card, the firm’s no. 6045, as the Three Rivers Steamship Company’s Enterprise. The collection has two copies carrying the misleading name. Beside one of them here is a real photograph lettered S.S. Enterprise at Montague. Follow the projecting upper deck and the single tall funnel from one view to the other. In the broadside you can even make out the railway station behind her. Holman’s identification and history of the company

A traveller in eastern Kings County had a choice quite easily lost in a history told from Charlottetown. In the 1908 season the Enterprise was contracted to make two round trips a week from Montague to Pictou by way of Georgetown and Murray Harbour. One of the company’s officers, George A. Thompson, was the Thompson of Poole & Thompson. The Montague merchant whose warehouse appears behind the naval launches on another postcard had an interest in the steamers that took people and produce out of the river. Lady Sybil

The Pictou–Souris leg of the Magdalen service

At Souris the Island could be a stop on the way to another set of islands. The Lady Sybil lay alongside the wharf at Pictou for this photograph. The federal return for the 1915 navigation season names her on the service to the Magdalen Islands. It reckons fifty miles from Pictou to Souris, then another seventy-six to Étang-du-Nord. The mail went free under the contract. In bad weather there were even instructions for where to put it ashore if the ship couldn’t land it at the intended port. The shipping return, printed page 70

A message on a different postcard, preserved at UPEI and postmarked at Souris East on December 26, 1911, tells of a Sunday arrival from Pictou aboard the Lady Sybil. Tucked into the writer’s Christmas news is just the journey the government would later express in miles. The Souris Christmas postcard

Prince Edward Island

A railway ferry for Borden and Cape Tormentine

On March 23, 1916, somebody in Georgetown wrote to Jack in New York during one of the worst snowstorms of the season. The photograph on the other side showed what the writer called their new icebreaker and car ferry. Georgetown is an important part of that address. The Prince Edward Island had arrived in 1915, before the terminals at Borden and Cape Tormentine were ready. She spent her first season working between Island ports and Pictou. Teddie’s card even gives the new ship a Pictou caption.

“Car ferry” meant something rather grander than room for a motorcar. The Prince Edward Island was 300 feet long and loaded through the stern. On her train deck two tracks held a dozen railway cars. From 1917 the Borden–Cape Tormentine terminals brought the railway right up to her, so a load of freight could cross in the car in which it had arrived. In Louson’s view at Cape Tormentine, take a look at the lifting gear beside the ship. The apparatus on shore was as much a part of this new crossing as the steamer. Louson’s terminal photograph · The ship and her engineers’ records

Ethel sent the photograph of tourists loading at Cape Tormentine and explained that she had taken her own picture of the boat. It had been a dull day and she didn’t know how hers would turn out. Teddie’s card has a different purpose. After the news that Uncle Bill was over at Grandpa’s comes the request, “do be quick & come back daddy.” The published photograph could explain the new railway apparatus to a stranger, or do duty for a child who wanted somebody back on this side of the water.

Hochelaga

A summer passage from Pictou to Charlottetown

Borden had the train ferry. Charlottetown could still offer a four-hour summer outing to Pictou aboard a former private yacht. The card that advertised “Four hours in a floating Palace” even offered accommodation for cars. The Royal Canadian Navy knew the same vessel as Hochelaga, a converted yacht that patrolled from Halifax and Sydney in the First World War. Sold in 1923, she found an occupation for which that long passenger deck was particularly well suited. The naval career of the Hochelaga

Naval historian Fraser McKee spent boyhood summers at his grandmother’s cottage on Pictou Harbour, watched “the Hoch” coming and going, and took the trip himself. The timetable he reproduced had her leave Pictou at nine in the morning, reach Charlottetown at one, leave again at four and bring you back to Pictou by eight. Daily except Sunday. A visitor sailing both ways had three hours in Charlottetown. The advertised one-way fare for a person and the separate charge for bringing a car were each $2.50. Lovat

Pictou, Souris and the Magdalen Islands from 1924

Meanwhile at the eastern end, the Lovat entered the Pictou, Souris and Magdalen Islands service in 1924. The Magdalen Islands’ English-speaking community has preserved an account of the weekly round trip. The vessel took passengers, vehicles and cargo, and after a change of ownership in 1945 she was renamed Magdalen. People there carried on calling her the Lovat. A postcard lettered with the older name would have made perfect sense on either side of that change. Council for Anglophone Magdalen Islanders, maritime history

Charlottetown

The second Borden railway ferry, 1931–41

The next Borden railway ferry had two big funnels and made a handsome postcard from almost any angle. For a change, start inside. Someone in the Charlottetown’s main lounge could sit in an armchair with a rug underfoot and a window close at hand while railway cars crossed on the deck below. The traveller who sent that card to Dundee promised a proper letter. For the time being, “the trip was swell” would do.

Canadian National’s history puts her entry into service on July 26, 1931, with room for sixteen railway cars, forty-one automobiles and eight hundred passengers. She ran for less than ten years. In June 1941, bound for her annual drydocking at Saint John, she was lost off Port Mouton, Nova Scotia. The older Prince Edward Island went back into full-time service. Canadian National’s account of the rail ferries

A good reason to read both sides of a card is sitting in this group. On September 28, 1945, Ada wrote from Cape Tormentine that she might cross that day if Gordon could get away. The picture she chose shows automobiles leaving the Charlottetown. The postcard was still in use more than four years after the ship was lost. It is wonderful evidence of what passengers once saw on the ramp and of what someone could still send from the port. Ada’s message tells us only that she hoped to sail.

Prince Nova

The first Wood Islands–Caribou ferry, 1941

Ten days after the loss of the Charlottetown, a new crossing opened at the other end of the Island. On June 28, 1941, Northumberland Ferries Limited opened the Wood Islands–Caribou automobile service with the first Prince Nova. She had sailed on the Great Lakes under the name Erie Isle and could carry fourteen vehicles. Set her little profile beside that of the railway ferry on the Travel Bureau’s July 2 card. These two very different vessels could sell you the two halves of the same trip. The Northumberland Ferries photograph collection

Turn the brown schedule card over. A motorcar cost two dollars one way, three dollars return. A passenger was forty cents one way, sixty-five cents return. The return half was interchangeable. You could drive onto the Prince Edward Island at Cape Tormentine, spend your visit crossing the Island, and drive home via Wood Islands and Caribou. Four days after the new service opened, the Travel Bureau was already explaining how to use both routes. Follow that round trip on the map. A later Prince Nova turns up elsewhere in the collection. Her own card dates her construction at Pictou to 1964. The later ship of the same name

Charles A. Dunning

A second ship for Wood Islands

The view from above is a good way to meet the Charles A. Dunning. The photographer could show almost the whole length of her vehicle deck with a single exposure. At the wharf in the next picture you can look straight down the approach she used. Before serving these two ports she had sailed as the Sankaty and done wartime duty as a Canadian naval minelayer. After the war she joined the first Prince Nova on the eastern crossing. The Island shipping register’s account of the Dunning

The little 1949 schedule lets us watch the two ships at work. In high summer the Prince Nova left Wood Islands at seven in the morning while the Dunning left Caribou at seven. At nine each sailed back from the other shore. The schedule card draws the crossing as fourteen miles and shows the six miles of road between Caribou and Pictou. The fares side promises a lunch counter and canteen on each boat. It also sets out an exception to the popular round trip. By 1949 the eastern company specifically excluded trucks from the arrangement for using its return ticket at Borden. The fares, map and working timetable

Abegweit

The original Abby at the capes from 1947

On August 8, 1947, a postcard copyrighted by D. W. Allen shows the Abegweit entering the dock at Cape Tormentine. Her name is legible on the bow. The day is printed right on the postcard, six days before she entered regular service on August 14. It’s an unusually satisfying pair of dates. We know the new ferry was there to have her picture taken before the first regular passenger sailing.

The original Abegweit was built at Sorel, Quebec. She was 372 feet long, diesel-electric, with propellers both forward and aft. She could carry 950 passengers, and her train capacity was nineteen railway cars. The Prince Edward Island continued alongside her as a relief and summer ferry. In a family album it is quite possible for two generations to remember different boats making the very same crossing in the very same year. The original Abegweit’s history, kept by her Chicago club

The publishers finally gave us a tour indoors. In the wheelhouse the controls stand close beneath a row of windows. The companionway has a broad stair and seats at its foot. In the restaurant the staff posed for their picture among the set tables, while the observation room was photographed empty, right down to the last upholstered seat. The traveller who addressed the stairway card to Pennsylvania had bought a new tire at Bathurst, driven down to Cape Tormentine and crossed to Borden. After naming the route, the writer added two words to make the choice of postcard perfectly plain. “This boat.”

Canadian National’s 1949 timetable allowed fifty-five minutes for the crossing and still listed a forty-cent passenger fare. Before driving off, passengers travelling in motor vehicles, including the driver, had to get a landing check from the purser and surrender it with the vehicle ticket. There was time to find the restaurant, but there was a little business to see to on board. The pink timetable and its instructions to motorists

One last pair of pictures. In 1938 a father wrote to Walter from the Abegweit Hotel at Borden, astonished to find his automobile going across on the train. His pink card shows the hotel. The ferry that would carry its familiar name was still nine years away from entering service. And the ship in Allen’s photograph has a later address of her own. At the end of her Strait career in 1982 she gave way to a second ferry named Abegweit. The original left for Chicago in 1983 and became Columbia Yacht Club’s clubhouse. A person looking for the boat in these postcards can still find her on the water. Walter’s card from the hotel · The Abby on Lake Michigan
