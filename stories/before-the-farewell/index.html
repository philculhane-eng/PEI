The history behind the picture

Before the Farewell: The Island Prepares for War

A gun fires in Charlottetown harbour. A line of men stands beside the road at Souris while artillerymen attend their guns. Horses wait in a military camp. A long wooden armoury appears in a town view that might otherwise be filed under buildings. The military history in the collection begins well before the photographs of men leaving for war.

These postcards show how service fitted into Island life: drill, equipment, annual camps, public ceremony, recruitment and the arrival of warships. Turn some of them over and the story changes register. A gunner tells his mother about exercise before breakfast. A family writes to a young signals man at Valcartier. Another correspondent reports worry and enlistment at home. Preparation and departure had addresses, timetables and people waiting for letters.

The guns at Fort Edward, and a salute at Souris

The battery at Fort Edward overlooked the harbour entrance. In the official account prepared for the restored Prince Edward Battery, its guns are described as part of Charlottetown’s harbour defence. The postcards bring that purpose down to a visible position: guns above the water, with the town beyond. They also show the guns being used for public salutes. Defence and ceremony shared the same landscape. The Prince Edward Battery’s historical commemoration.

Card 3709 is particularly specific. Its title reads “First Salute to King George V. at Charlottetown, May 9, 1910.” The caption places the salute a year before the coronation celebrations of 1911. The smoke makes an otherwise still military scene suddenly active. The photographer caught the guns doing something.

Souris contributes a wonderfully legible scene of its own. Card 0563 is captioned “No. 9 Battery, C.G.A. Firing Salute Coronation Day, Souris, P.E.I.” and credits Photo Acorn. The uniformed line runs along the unpaved road, the gun crews occupy the grass, and spectators have taken places beside the wooden pavement. The photograph contains both the military formation and the public occasion.

By 1913, when this card was mailed, the salute had already become a postcard subject. The sound and sight of artillery belonged to public occasions in communities beyond Charlottetown.

The buildings where preparation happened

An armoury is less dramatic on a postcard than a firing gun, but it was a necessary part of the same system. Equipment needed storage; men needed somewhere to assemble and drill. Charlottetown’s wooden military buildings appear both on their own and beside West Kent School. With the school beside the armouries, military preparation becomes part of the ordinary townscape.

The militia’s annual reports supply some of the less picturesque necessities behind these views. In 1902, additions and alterations to the Charlottetown drill hall were nearing completion, with heating and flooring still to finish; in 1912, another large extension was reported complete. Those are practical details, but they help explain what the postcards leave out: keeping a local military establishment ready involved buildings, fittings and work that continued between ceremonial appearances. The 1902 militia report · The 1912 report.

Summerside’s earlier drill shed burned in the great fire of 1906. Work on the new armoury began in 1911, the date on its façade; the completed building was formally transferred to the 82nd Regiment on June 22, 1912. Culture Summerside records annual drill there in 1913 and, in early 1916, recruits making trenches, dugouts and loopholes in Armoury Square and practising bayonet work against dummies. The building became a recruiting centre during both world wars. Culture Summerside’s history of the armoury.

That changes the way the quiet architectural card reads. The steps and windows are easy to see; the exercises on the grounds have to be recovered from the record. The PEI Museum and Heritage Foundation also describes the armoury’s home-front role, including Red Cross activity and fundraising. A recruiting building was part of a larger network of civilian work. The armoury’s military and community history.

Horses, tents and heavy guns

The Light Horse card was already in the mail in July 1904. The mounted squadron had become the Prince Edward Island Light Horse in 1903, before expansion into a regiment in 1914. The men and mounts therefore belong to a local military tradition that predates the Great War. Library and Archives Canada’s cavalry guide, p. 55.

The equipment helps us read the work. Some horses carry riders; others draw guns and supplies. Tents suggest a temporary camp, while armouries provide permanent storage and gathering places. Around a gun, the men form a crew whose training brought their separate tasks together.

There are four copies of these heavy-battery postcards in the collection, showing two views. Pugh’s number 42-7 shows the horse teams at Camp Brighton: 0129 and 4416. Number 42-3 shows the gun crews beside the harbour: 2935 and 4418. The second view gives the fuller caption, “Heavy Battery No. 3 Co., 4th Regiment, C.A., Charlottetown, P.E.I.” The Charlottetown cancellation on 2935 is July 5, 1911, above a message dated July 4. The gun-position photograph was therefore in circulation more than three years before the war.

“Heavy artillery” describes the artillery and its service; a battery is a unit of guns and the men who operate them. A brigade could contain several batteries. The Island’s names and numbers changed too. In 1910 the 4th Prince Edward Island Heavy Brigade’s three companies became numbered batteries; in June 1912 the Prince Edward Island Heavy Brigade was reorganized with the 3rd and 4th Heavy Batteries. The “No. 3 Co.” printed on these earlier cards belongs to the older organization. Its number should not be silently changed to “3rd Heavy Battery.” Library and Archives Canada, militia artillery guide, printed p. 71.

A fleet in the harbour, and Canada’s new navy

The postcard of Prince Battenberg’s fleet belongs to the era of visiting Royal Navy squadrons. Contemporary shipping reports transcribed by the Island Register place the visit of Prince Louis of Battenberg’s squadron in September 1905. The ships brought naval power close enough to become a subject for an Island postcard. The added colours give Louson’s photograph a particularly vivid postcard life. The September 1905 shipping reports.

Niobe brought a new connection. Transferred from the Royal Navy in 1910, she arrived at Halifax that October as one of the first ships of Canada’s naval service. Her subsequent career included wartime patrols and service as a depot ship. On the Charlottetown postcard at the beginning of this story, she is dressed with flags, and a cloud of white smoke marks a firing gun. The Navy’s history of Niobe.

Historian Harry Holman identifies this particular image as the royal salute on Coronation Day, June 22, 1911. His account draws on the Guardian and accompanying photographs: the ship’s company and band joined the procession through Charlottetown to Victoria Park, along with local military and civilian organisations. The harbour visit was an event ashore as well as afloat. Holman’s account of Niobe and Coronation Day.

Put the salutes at Fort Edward and Souris beside the camp, armouries and visiting ship, and military service becomes a familiar presence in Island streets and harbours long before August 1914. The postcards preserve the public occasions through which many Islanders encountered it.

August 1914: the familiar uniforms leave home

The photograph of men aboard the Cacouna appears to show the departure on August 5, 1914, at Buntain & Bell’s Wharf. The soldiers are sitting wherever they can on the ship, including high on the deckhouse and along the rail. Two gun barrels are visible at the lower left. The City of Charlottetown Archives has a wider view, dated and identified, of this same departure. The City Archives’ photograph.

Their destination was Nova Scotia. An August 11 Newfoundland newspaper reported 125 soldiers, six officers, forty horses and four large guns taken aboard at Charlottetown. It assigned sixty men to guard the cable at Canso and the remainder to wireless duty at Sydney. This was an immediate coastal-security deployment, the day after Britain’s declaration of war. The contemporary shipping report.

The full story of this departure follows the wharf, the ship and the evidence in greater detail. Here, the image makes the connection with the earlier postcards: the men and guns that had been part of the Island’s peacetime military life were being sent to a wartime assignment.

Louis writes from Sydney Mines

At half past six in the morning, before the men had eaten breakfast, Louis had the whole battery out for drill. That is the small, precise detail he wanted his mother to know. He was the battery’s physical director as well as a gunner, and he was pleased with the photograph he was sending home.

His message on card 4255 is headed “PEI Heavy Artillery Encampment, Sydney Mines, C.B.” and dated September 23, 1914. On the front, eleven uniformed men pose beneath leafy trees in front of a stone building. Rifles, rolled equipment and pale canvas bags crowd the group; part of a bicycle appears at the left. Louis supplies the photographer’s name and the occasion: Mr. Bayer took it on the morning they left for Sydney Mines.

That credit connects the picture with James Alexander Shaw Bayer, the Charlottetown photographer. Louis’s wording suggests a photograph made before the group reached its Cape Breton posting. September 23 is the date of his message, and Sydney Mines is where he was writing. Neither should automatically become the photograph’s date or location.

PEI Heavy Artillery Encampment,
Sydney Mines, C.B.
Sept 23rd / 14

Dear Mother,
Hope you are well. I am fine. I like the place very well.

This is a group taken by Mr Bayer on the morning we left for S.M. It is a pretty good picture of the group. I am physical director for the battery. I have the whole Battery out for a drill at 6:30 am before breakfast. I am a gunner in addition to the P.D.

I think Alex looks pretty good. Give him my love when you write or see him. Tell him I will write soon. Tell our dear Rita I will write soon.

Love to Capt. [name and following words unclear] … family.
Yours lovingly, Louis.
WRITE SOON.

The transcription follows the readable message, with punctuation and paragraph breaks added. “S.M.” is his abbreviation for Sydney Mines; “P.D.” refers back to physical director. The closing runs sideways into the remaining space. The reading is “gunner,” the artillery rank. Louis’s surname, the captain’s name, and the identities of Alex and Rita are still unconfirmed.

Louis was describing a daily responsibility within the battery. He gives us the hour, the men involved and the place of exercise in their routine: the whole battery, before breakfast. He also remained someone’s son, asking his mother to pass on his love and promising letters to Alex and Rita. The capital letters at the end make his own wish plain.

His card fits the Island artillery’s first wartime chapter. The August 5 departure photograph shows men and guns leaving Charlottetown for service in Nova Scotia; by September 23 an Island gunner was writing from Sydney Mines. The two cards connect the departure and the work that followed. A shared connection to the Island artillery does not identify any particular man in either photograph, or establish that Louis travelled on the August 5 sailing.

July 1915: “An Island Unit For the Front”

The Guardian of July 6, 1915 supplies a further chapter on page 9. Across the top is a headline announcing that the Cycle Corps and Heavy Artillery had reached Plymouth aboard the Caledonia. Beneath the large group photograph, the newspaper credits W. G. MacLaughlin with taking the picture of the “Heavies” immediately before embarkation. A small advertisement offers photographs of every section of the Second Heavy Battery and the 25th Nova Scotia Battalion, available from D. H. McDonald, P.O. Box 645, Halifax. Military group photographs were being sold to families as the men went overseas. Read the July 6, 1915 Guardian, page 9.

This is a different photograph and departure from Louis’s Bayer group of 1914. The 2nd Heavy Battery, organized at Halifax in November 1914, drew men from several militia formations, including the Prince Edward Island Heavy Brigade. It sailed in June 1915. Its Island members were serving in a unit recruited across several provinces. Library and Archives Canada, CEF artillery guide, printed p. 4.

The lower half of the same page turns from men already away to men still needed. The appeal, headed “An Island Unit For the Front,” argues that Islanders wanted to serve with their own officers and friends. It seeks 200 men for a heavy artillery battery. Six qualified artillery officers had volunteered for the front, it says, so the invitation could be “come” with them. The notice reports 86 men already volunteered and 17 applicants as officers. Previous military experience was preferred, but men without it were welcome; at least six months’ training was promised before service at the front.

The practical instructions are unusually useful. Pay would begin with medical examination, drill would start within days, and prospective recruits could approach Lieutenant-Colonel A. G. Peake in Charlottetown, Major C. Leigh or Captain W. Cook at the Drill Hall, or Captain W. B. Prowse at the Dalton Office. Around those details runs the pressure of wartime recruiting: an appeal to women to encourage husbands and sons, fear for the Empire and the Island, and shame if the province failed to fill its battery.

The assertion that the Island had no unit it could call its own was an argument for a distinct overseas formation. The pre-war battery cards, the Cacouna departure and Louis’s letter show how much Island artillery service had already taken place. The newspaper itself places an overseas group containing Island recruits above the appeal for an Island unit.

Military records make the next step clearer. Library and Archives Canada records the organization at Charlottetown in July 1915 of No. 2 Heavy Battery Depot, under Major A. G. Peake. This became the Island’s 2nd Canadian Siege Battery. Its similar name can cause confusion with the 2nd Heavy Battery pictured above the appeal. The Island siege unit sailed for Britain on November 27, 1915, and reached France on June 1, 1916. These are successive stages of mobilization, with different formations and departure dates. LAC’s account of the Island siege battery, printed p. 138 · The official lineage and overseas service dates.

News from home

A fox on the front of 2327 conceals a letter full of wartime worry. Its Central Bedeque cancellation is from August 1914; the writer asks whether the war is creating a panic at the recipient’s end and reports enlistment, anxiety and interrupted business at home. The message catches one correspondent trying to explain what those first weeks felt like.

The young man at Valcartier

A small run of cards follows H. Ronald Stewart from an address at the Collegiate in Windsor, Nova Scotia, in April 1914 to the Divisional Signal Company at Valcartier that August. Cards 4648 and 4649 mix ordinary writing with Morse. The family voice remains unmistakable: one message reports Grandma’s suggestion that a pair of glasses should accompany writing so small.

The name and signals unit match Hugh Ronald Stewart, listed in the London Gazette as Private 5753 of the 1st Canadian Divisional Signal Company. He later served in the Royal Canadian Air Force as a group captain. That later career deserves its own story; here, these cards catch him at the beginning, receiving messages from home in a mixture of family language and a signaller’s code. Another departure, and a wish for luck

The departures continued. An undated message on a bird’s-eye view of Summerside says that the 105th Battalion, recruited on the Island, will leave for Valcartier “next Sunday.” The message and cancellation leave the exact Sunday open. The official record supplies the larger sequence: the battalion was authorised in December 1915 and embarked for Britain on July 16, 1916. Its overseas role was to provide reinforcements to the Canadian Corps. The 105th Battalion’s service history.

On the back of the Fort Edward view, 3678, a man writing in February 1916 tells Lizzie that he is training and cannot get home until the following week. The fixed guns on the front and the delayed visit on the back belong to different scales of the same story. One is a familiar view of Island defence; the other is someone’s immediate difficulty in getting home.

The 105th’s decorative card offers a horseshoe and a verse wishing protection from harm. We know what came afterwards in the war, but the card should first be allowed its own moment: someone wanted the person carrying it to come through safely. Before there were inscriptions to read at memorials, there were addresses to keep, letters to answer, and people to wish luck as they went away.
