The history behind the picture

Before the Farewell: The Island Prepares for War

A gun fires in Charlottetown harbour. A line of men stands beside the road at Souris while artillerymen attend their guns. Horses wait in a military camp. A long wooden armoury appears in a town view that might otherwise be filed under buildings. The military history in the collection begins well before the photographs of men leaving for war.

These postcards show how service fitted into Island life: drill, equipment, annual camps, public ceremony, recruitment and the arrival of warships. Turn some of them over and the story changes register. A gunner tells his mother about exercise before breakfast. A family writes to a young signals man at Valcartier. Another correspondent reports worry and enlistment at home. Preparation and departure had addresses, timetables and people waiting for letters.

The guns at Fort Edward, and a salute at Souris

The battery at Fort Edward overlooked the harbour entrance. In the official account prepared for the restored Prince Edward Battery, its guns are described as part of Charlottetown’s harbour defence. The postcards bring that purpose down to a visible position: guns above the water, with the town beyond. They also show the guns being used for public salutes. Defence and ceremony shared the same landscape. The Prince Edward Battery’s historical commemoration.

Card 3709 is particularly specific. Its title reads “First Salute to King George V. at Charlottetown, May 9, 1910.” That is the date supplied by the photograph, and it should not be confused with the coronation celebrations of the following year. The smoke makes an otherwise still military scene suddenly active. The photographer caught the guns doing something.

Souris contributes a wonderfully legible scene of its own. Card 0563 is captioned “No. 9 Battery, C.G.A. Firing Salute Coronation Day, Souris, P.E.I.” and credits Photo Acorn. The uniformed line runs along the unpaved road, the gun crews occupy the grass, and spectators have taken places beside the wooden pavement. The photograph contains both the military formation and the public occasion.

The card was mailed in 1913, which supplies a latest possible date for the image, not the year of the ceremony. For now, its printed description is enough. It shows that the public sound and sight of artillery belonged to communities beyond Charlottetown.

The buildings where preparation happened

An armoury is less dramatic on a postcard than a firing gun, but it was a necessary part of the same system. Equipment needed storage; men needed somewhere to assemble and drill. Charlottetown’s wooden military buildings appear both on their own and beside West Kent School. Seeing the school and armouries together puts military preparation into the ordinary townscape, rather than somewhere outside civilian life.

The militia’s annual reports supply some of the less picturesque necessities behind these views. In 1902, additions and alterations to the Charlottetown drill hall were nearing completion, with heating and flooring still to finish; in 1912, another large extension was reported complete. Those are practical details, but they help explain what the postcards leave out: keeping a local military establishment ready involved buildings, fittings and work that continued between ceremonial appearances. The 1902 militia report · The 1912 report.

Summerside’s earlier drill shed burned in the great fire of 1906. Work on the new armoury began in 1911, the date on its façade; the completed building was formally transferred to the 82nd Regiment on June 22, 1912. Culture Summerside records annual drill there in 1913 and, in early 1916, recruits making trenches, dugouts and loopholes in Armoury Square and practising bayonet work against dummies. The building became a recruiting centre during both world wars. Culture Summerside’s history of the armoury.

That changes the way the quiet architectural card reads. The steps and windows are easy to see; the exercises on the grounds have to be recovered from the record. The PEI Museum and Heritage Foundation also describes the armoury’s home-front role, including Red Cross activity and fundraising. A recruiting building was part of a larger network of civilian work. The armoury’s military and community history.

Horses, tents and heavy guns

The Light Horse card was already in the mail in July 1904. Its name is not an anachronism: the mounted squadron had become the Prince Edward Island Light Horse in 1903, before expansion into a regiment in 1914. The men and mounts therefore belong to a local military tradition that predates the Great War. Library and Archives Canada’s cavalry guide, p. 55.

These images also distinguish different kinds of work. Some horses carry riders; others draw guns and equipment. The tents suggest a temporary camp, while the armoury cards show the permanent buildings that supported the units. A group around a gun has to be understood as a crew, with tasks that a posed photograph cannot fully reveal. The postcard may arrange the men for the camera, but the equipment explains why they had to train together.

The artillery’s formal names changed over time. By June 1912, the unit was the Prince Edward Island Heavy Brigade, Canadian Garrison Artillery. A battery is an artillery organisation; it should not casually become a “heavy battalion” in the telling. That distinction becomes important when these familiar Island gunners begin to appear in wartime records. The official unit lineage.

A fleet in the harbour, and Canada’s new navy

The postcard of Prince Battenberg’s fleet belongs to the era of visiting Royal Navy squadrons. Contemporary shipping reports transcribed by the Island Register place the visit of Prince Louis of Battenberg’s squadron in September 1905. The ships brought naval power close enough to become a subject for an Island postcard. The printed colours belong to the postcard’s treatment of the scene; they do not establish the time of day when Louson took the photograph. The September 1905 shipping reports.

Niobe brought a new connection. Transferred from the Royal Navy in 1910, she arrived at Halifax that October as one of the first ships of Canada’s naval service. Her subsequent career included wartime patrols and service as a depot ship. On the Charlottetown postcard at the beginning of this story, she is dressed with flags, and a cloud of white smoke marks a firing gun. The Navy’s history of Niobe.

Historian Harry Holman identifies this particular image as the royal salute on Coronation Day, June 22, 1911. His account draws on the Guardian and accompanying photographs: the ship’s company and band joined the procession through Charlottetown to Victoria Park, along with local military and civilian organisations. The harbour visit was an event ashore as well as afloat. Holman’s account of Niobe and Coronation Day.

The salutes at Fort Edward and Souris, the military camp, the armouries and the visiting ship can now be seen together. Islanders encountered military service in streets, public celebrations and the harbour long before August 1914. The photographs do not tell us how every spectator felt about it. They do show how visible it was.

August 1914: the familiar uniforms leave home

The photograph of men aboard the Cacouna appears to show the departure on August 5, 1914, at Buntain & Bell’s Wharf. The soldiers are sitting wherever they can on the ship, including high on the deckhouse and along the rail. Two gun barrels are visible at the lower left. The City of Charlottetown Archives has a wider view, dated and identified, of this same departure. The City Archives’ photograph.

Their destination was Nova Scotia. An August 11 Newfoundland newspaper reported 125 soldiers, six officers, forty horses and four large guns taken aboard at Charlottetown. It assigned sixty men to guard the cable at Canso and the remainder to wireless duty at Sydney. This was an immediate coastal-security deployment, the day after Britain’s declaration of war. These men were not boarding a transport for France. The contemporary shipping report.

The full story of this departure follows the wharf, the ship and the evidence in greater detail. Here, the image makes the connection with the earlier postcards: the men and guns that had been part of the Island’s peacetime military life were being sent to a wartime assignment.

“Dear Mother”: the other side of the military postcard

Card 4255 brings us close to a particular group. A group of uniformed men poses beneath trees, with equipment, weapons and pale bags; a bicycle is partly visible at the left. On the reverse, the writer heads his message from the PEI Heavy Artillery encampment at Sydney Mines, Cape Breton, September 23, 1914. He tells his mother that Mr. Bayer took the group photograph on the morning they left for Sydney Mines. That locates the letter, while leaving the exact location and date of the photograph unconfirmed.

He is pleased with the picture. Then he explains his new responsibilities: “I am physical director for the Battery.” He has the men out for drill at 6:30 a.m., before breakfast, and is a gunner as well. The message continues into family news and ends with an emphatic request to write soon. There is no need to invent a speech about duty for him. He has left us the work, the hour of the morning, and the wish for a letter.

Other wartime messages hide behind subjects that would never be found by searching for a uniform. The front of 2327 shows a fox. Its Central Bedeque cancellation is from August 1914; the writer asks whether the war is creating a panic at the recipient’s end and reports enlistment, worry and interrupted business at home. That is one correspondent’s account, not a statistical description of the Island’s economy. It is valuable precisely as a voice trying to describe what the first weeks felt like.

The young man at Valcartier

A small run of cards follows H. Ronald Stewart from an address at the Collegiate in Windsor, Nova Scotia, in April 1914 to the Divisional Signal Company at Valcartier that August. Cards 4648 and 4649 mix ordinary writing with Morse. The family voice remains unmistakable: one message reports Grandma’s suggestion that a pair of glasses should accompany writing so small.

The name and signals unit match Hugh Ronald Stewart, listed in the London Gazette as Private 5753 of the 1st Canadian Divisional Signal Company. He later served in the Royal Canadian Air Force as a group captain. That later career deserves its own story; here, these cards catch him at the beginning, receiving messages from home in a mixture of family language and a signaller’s code. Another departure, and a wish for luck

The departures continued. An undated message on a bird’s-eye view of Summerside says that the 105th Battalion, recruited on the Island, will leave for Valcartier “next Sunday.” Without a dated message or cancellation, the exact Sunday remains open. The official record supplies the larger sequence: the battalion was authorised in December 1915 and embarked for Britain on July 16, 1916. Its overseas role was to provide reinforcements to the Canadian Corps. The 105th Battalion’s service history.

On the back of the Fort Edward view, 3678, a man writing in February 1916 tells Lizzie that he is training and cannot get home until the following week. The fixed guns on the front and the delayed visit on the back belong to different scales of the same story. One is a familiar view of Island defence; the other is someone’s immediate difficulty in getting home.

The 105th’s decorative card offers a horseshoe and a verse wishing protection from harm. We know what came afterwards in the war, but the card should first be allowed its own moment: someone wanted the person carrying it to come through safely. Before there were inscriptions to read at memorials, there were addresses to keep, letters to answer, and people to wish luck as they went away.
