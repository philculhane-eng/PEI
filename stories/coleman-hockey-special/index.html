The history behind the picture

Fifty Yards from Disaster: The Coleman Hockey Special

They were going to a hockey game. On the evening of February 25, 1914, a special train carried hockey enthusiasts from Kensington towards Alberton, passing through Summerside on its way west. About a hundred people were aboard. At Coleman, their evening became a story that would make the next morning’s front page.

Five real photo postcards preserve the aftermath. A passenger coach leans at a dreadful angle. A locomotive stands beside it, its wheels down in the snow. Men gather around the wreckage, and in one photograph a man sits high on the roof of the tilted coach. Then there is the snowplough: a great wooden and metal machine with its front smashed open. It takes a moment to reconcile those pictures with the most welcome sentence in the newspaper account: “There was nobody hurt.”

A siding, a switch, and a train behind them

The hockey special was travelling under Conductor John Hughes. It had passed through Summerside about fifteen minutes before the regular No. 1 freight left for Tignish. At Coleman the special took the siding to allow an eastbound freight to pass. There were trains moving in both directions, and another westbound train coming along behind the excursion.

The trouble began as the special backed out of the siding. The fuller account says the switch had been left in the wrong position after the eastbound freight passed. The coaches came off the rails at the points. The engine stayed on, went out through the other end of the siding, and came back along the main line to help recover them.

The crew managed to get coaches back onto the track, but one remained lying across the main line. The passengers found room in the other coaches. Behind them, somewhere down the railway, the following freight was approaching with a snowplough ahead of its engine.

Hughes knew that second train was coming. He walked back to the water tank at West Devon, roughly a mile and a half away, to warn it. The newspaper describes him waving a red light. It suggests that steam obscured the signal from engineer E. Cameron, while the sound of the engine swallowed the warning shouted from beside the track.

The freight reached the obstruction and struck the coach. Cameron applied the emergency brake, but the train was moving downhill on slippery rails. It went about another fifteen feet before the snowplough jumped the track and the engine followed it off.

The occupied coaches were about fifty yards farther ahead. The newspaper’s account dwells on that distance: the freight had derailed before reaching the cars into which the hockey supporters had crowded. The smashed equipment in these photographs marks the place where a much worse evening came to an end. The fuller account, preserved in the Guardian’s Saturday edition.

The snowplough tells its part

This is the photograph that makes the newspaper’s inventory of damage tangible. The great face that should have driven snow away from the railway has been opened up, exposing the structure behind it. Snow lies against the broken machine. The men standing above it give us its scale.

The follow-up report said the engine and one coach had suffered comparatively little damage. Another coach was badly damaged at one end and had a broken truck—the wheeled assembly beneath the carriage. The snowplough had lost its face. Across the five cards, those separate pieces of railway news become objects we can examine: coach body, wheels, tender, engine, plough.

A wrecking train and crew were sent from Charlottetown. A train that had reached Wellington was sent back to help collect the stranded passengers. The hockey excursion had become a railway recovery operation, with damaged equipment to move and a main line to reopen.

By about five o’clock on the afternoon of February 26 the track was clear. The first train from the west, again under Hughes, reached Summerside around nine that evening. The damaged engine, plough and cars were still at Coleman when the follow-up notice was written; the wrecking train was expected to take the engine and plough to Charlottetown the next morning. “Collision Damage,” Guardian, February 27.

A hard winter on the railway

The front page that announced the Coleman wreck carried another railway story alongside it: “Snow Fighting on Railway.” The paper described the most difficult conditions since the winter of 1905. During the previous week’s storms, trains had been stalled east of O’Leary and at Alberton. In some cuttings west of Summerside, drifts were reported to be five to twenty feet deep. Men could walk on snow packed high enough to reach the telegraph wires.

That account puts the snowplough back into the daily work for which it was built. Railway crews had been trying to keep passengers, freight and mail moving across the Island through repeated storms. A hockey special belonged to that same railway world: the line carried people to an evening’s entertainment as well as carrying the necessities of Island life. At Coleman, a problem at a switch brought an excursion train and a working freight into the same stretch of track. The two railway reports on the February 26 front page.

Five postcards, one close escape

All five cards bear the imprint “B. H. Turner, O’Leary, P.E.I.” Each also carries a handwritten date, February 26, 1914, and the words “Prince Edward Island, Canada.” That date fits the day after the collision, when the wreck was being cleared. O’Leary gives us Turner’s business address; the newspapers place the accident at Coleman.

One photograph also survives in a Kensington heritage exhibit preserved by Library and Archives Canada. Its reproduction matches card 1829, including the man on the roof, the tender beside the coach and the wreckage beneath it. The exhibit credits Lowell Huestis as the source. The postcard scan lets us see considerably more of the detail. The Kensington exhibit’s copy.

What makes the group so absorbing is the way each view adds something. One shows the wreck along the side of the carriage; another looks into its broken end. Walk around through the next photograph and the engine comes into view. Then the damaged plough explains the force involved. Together they preserve the aftermath of an evening that began with a journey to a hockey game.

The passengers’ part of the story survives in the newspaper: about a hundred excursionists, moved out of danger, with a freight train stopped short of the cars sheltering them. After all the broken machinery, the line worth remembering is the one the Guardian printed the next morning: everybody had come through safely.

A note on the date and sources

The February 26 front-page report places the accident on the previous evening; the fuller account reprinted on February 28 specifies Wednesday, at about 8:30 p.m. Together these establish February 25, 1914. The brief February 27 cleanup notice says Tuesday, and the Kensington exhibit’s reproduction is captioned January 25. The contemporary accounts support the February 25 date used here. The handwritten February 26 annotations are consistent with photographs of the following day’s aftermath. The newspaper reports also differ over the number of coaches initially derailed; the sequence above follows the fuller account. The five card images are 1648, 1829, 3955, 3956 and 4037.
