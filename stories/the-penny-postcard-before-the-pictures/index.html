The history behind the picture

The Penny Postcard: Before the Pictures

On July 27, 1880, someone at the Bank of Prince Edward Island filled in a small printed acknowledgement. A letter had arrived, with its enclosures, and the bank was sending word that it had been received. The form carried the name of J. R. Brecken, cashier. On the other side, Queen Victoria looked out from an elaborate blue frame, beside the words Canada Post Card.

There was no harbour view, summer hotel or street scene. The card itself was the convenience: a place to write, an address on the other side, and the postage already paid. This little bank receipt belongs to the postcard’s first working life, before pictures became the reason so many people bought and kept them.

A penny bought the card and the journey

Canada introduced its first official postal card on June 1, 1871. The government sold it for one cent, including both the card and its delivery within the Dominion. No envelope was needed, and no separate stamp had to be bought and stuck on. The little printed stamp was proof that the postage had been prepaid. Collectors call this postal stationery. George B. Arfken, “Early, Unauthorized Post Cards” · Clifton A. Howes, Canada: Its Postage Stamps and Postal Stationery, chapter XXIII

The idea had spread quickly. Austria introduced official correspondence cards in October 1869; Britain followed in 1870. A few lines could arrange a visit, acknowledge a payment or ask a price. The address occupied one side and the message the other. Anyone handling the mail could read it, so a card was particularly useful for the ordinary things people had no need to seal inside an envelope. University of Alberta Library, “The Postcard – A Brief History”

The 1870s: a blue frame around everyday business

The earliest dated postal card in this collection takes us to another Charlottetown bank. On March 31, 1877, the Union Bank of P.E. Island acknowledged a letter of the 22nd. Its cashier, George MacLeod, had a printed form ready for just this purpose. A clerk supplied the dates, addressed the card to the manager of the Bank of British North America in Brantford, and sent it on its way.

The scrollwork gives these small pieces of business mail a surprisingly handsome appearance. They belong to the engraved, blue-bordered family introduced in 1871. Both the 1877 Union Bank card and the 1880 Brecken card carry the later printer’s imprint naming Montreal alone. Howes records that change from “Montreal & Ottawa” during 1876. It is a small clue tucked against the bottom border. Howes, chapter XXIII

The banks were only part of the traffic. In March 1878, James R. Reid used a card to announce his application for discharge from insolvency. That May, another card went from Montague Bridge to Montreal asking about a shipment. These early survivors already contain much of the later postcard’s working vocabulary: received, please send, when will it come? Reid’s notice · The Montague Bridge enquiry

The 1880s and 1890s: the border gives way to a banner

In 1882 the Post Office introduced a simpler, typographed design. The surrounding frame disappeared. Across the top floated a ribbon bearing Canada Post Card, with Victoria’s portrait in an oval to the right. This banner family continued through changes in the stamp ornaments, ink and card size. Howes, chapter XXIII

On a July 1889 card, Miller Brothers of Charlottetown wrote to Boston about band parts. Their business heading offered pianos, organs and sewing machines. The blue ribbon and oval stamp left plenty of room for the address. Two years later, a Tignish correspondent used the same broad design family to ask a very short commercial question: “Can you quote us on 1000 bags salt? Please reply.” The salt enquiry, March 1891

The Black Diamond example, Card 4522, belongs beside these commercial messages. Turn it over and the steamship Bonavista is preparing to sail for Gulf ports. A postcard could summon freight to a Montreal wharf as readily as it could request a thousand bags of salt from an Island office. The agents’ sailing circulars have their own story in “Now Receiving Freight.”

There are personal discoveries among the business forms, too. In December 1894, W. S. Louson wrote from Montreal on H. A. Nelson & Sons stationery, offering to help his correspondent before Christmas and reminding him about some promised stamps. Years before the Island views associated with Louson filled postcard albums, he was already using the modest prepaid card—and thinking about collecting. Louson’s note

Pictures find their place

From January 1, 1895, privately produced cards could enter Canada’s domestic mail at the one-cent postcard rate with an adhesive stamp attached. That opened the way for printers, businesses and publishers to supply their own cards. “Private Post Card” referred to who produced the card; the message still travelled in the open. University of Alberta Library

The collection’s Independent Order of Foresters card, mailed in October 1898, makes the distinction visible. Its address side carries a drawing of the Foresters’ Toronto building, a prominent advertisement for the society and an ordinary perforated stamp. Picture and business promotion were sharing the postcard before the familiar Island holiday view became commonplace.

Among the earliest dated Island view cards here are two sent in 1901. Effie chose a photograph of Dalvay, captioned “Dalvey,” and posted it from Charlottetown on August 23. Her message runs around the picture’s margins, finishing with the pleasure of sending a pretty view. In December, another writer sent Victoria Row to Texas, with Christmas wishes and news of good sleighing and skating. Both cards keep the address side to itself.

By 1904, Canada had adopted the divided back, giving message and address their own spaces on the same side. The whole opposite face could then belong to the image. These earlier cards show why that was such a useful change: senders had been fitting their words wherever the picture left room. University of Alberta Library

The picture-postcard years still needed plain cards

Prepaid stationery kept its place on the desk. By the turn of the century the banner had given way to plain lettering and a rectangular green stamp impression. On July 18, 1901, Goff Brothers wrote a receipt for a cheque of $3.05 on just such a card. Its purpose was much the same as the Union Bank’s in 1877. Card 1642

A May 1901 message from Rusticoville shows how comfortably the same stationery fitted family life. Mrs. B. H. wanted to know which train a visitor would arrive on: the horses were busy, and no one wanted to wait needlessly. “Drop a card Monday,” she asked. There was also a request for two spools of orange silk. A penny could settle the railway pickup and the shopping in one journey. The Rusticoville card

Victoria’s successors appeared on the printed postage while the familiar uses continued. A July 1914 card from Bloomfield has George V’s portrait and a bilingual heading, Post Card – Carte Postale. The writer wanted the invoices behind an account for $28.20. In July 1915, a visitor attending the Sons of Temperance convention in Charlottetown sent a George V card to Iowa, explicitly offering it for the recipient’s collection.

The red two-cent card also records a wartime change. From April 15, 1915, a one-cent war tax raised the postcard charge to two cents for mail within Canada and to the United States. The extra penny is built into this card’s printed value. Royal Philatelic Society of Canada, Admiral-era postal rates

A long working life

Turn forward to March 1936 and the plain card is carrying a report on the London fox-pelt auctions. The Canadian National Silver Fox Breeders’ Association sent its market news from Summerside to Ambrose Lewis at Johnstone River. Dense type fills the message side; the address side still has the familiar heading and prepaid postage. Card 2331

In April 1951, Sandra Ann Sutherland of Kent Street received a card telling her when to appear at the Empire Theatre for the P.E.I. Musical Festival. Her session was Friday, May 4, at 2:15 p.m. She was to present the card to the platform secretary; it would admit her to the session. A one-cent adhesive stamp had been added beside the card’s printed one-cent stamp.

Seventy-four years separate the Union Bank’s receipt and Sandra Ann’s festival appointment. In between are salt orders, sailing notices, family arrangements, invoices and market reports. The postcard acquired pictures, colours, jokes and holiday greetings along the way. These plain cards kept doing the work that had made the idea useful in the first place.
