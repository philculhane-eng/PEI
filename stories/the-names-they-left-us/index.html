The history behind the picture

The Names They Left Us: Remembering the Island’s Fallen

In one photograph, two women stand beside the new monument in Summerside, their faces turned towards its inscription. Above them a bronze soldier strides forward with a rifle. At their feet lie wreaths. The photographer has given us the whole memorial, but it is the people beside it who make the picture difficult to pass over. We cannot know whose names they were looking for. We can see that there were people there to read them.

The Island’s memorial postcards preserve more than the appearance of statues. They show remembrance becoming part of a town: a procession past shop windows, cars parked beside a square, children sitting under the trees, a hospital among the birches. Some were made while the loss was still recent. Their first viewers did not need a historian to explain what had happened to the people whose names were being cut into stone.

Summerside, Dominion Day, 1922

On July 1, 1922, the Prince County Soldiers’ Monument was unveiled in Summerside. The name matters: this was a memorial to the county’s fallen, with a place in the town where people could gather around it. The unveiling date is recorded in the collection’s series of twelve photographs and in the town’s account of the monument’s centenary. The 2022 account of its restoration and unveiling anniversary.

The bronze figure was the work of Emanuel Hahn. His soldier advances with his equipment and rifle, held in a movement that can never finish. Years later the square and monument acquired further layers of remembrance: the park was formally named Memorial Square in 1938, and a separate stone commemorating the Second World War and Korean War dead was unveiled in 1956. The photographs here belong to the earlier memorial, before those additional losses could have been known. The monument’s history.

Read’s photographs let us move between the public ceremony and the streets around it. There are mounted men, marching men, a band, and spectators who have found places along the pavement. The cars are almost as conspicuous as the horses. Shopfronts remain legible behind the procession. This is a town making room for remembrance within its everyday surroundings.

The series includes several views from a high position over the same street. Compare the shop signs, striped awning, telegraph poles and parked cars, and the different portions of the procession fall into place. Card 0902, catalogued separately, turns out to repeat the mounted view in 3276: even the positions of the spectators and the horses’ legs agree. It gives us a better print of the moment, rather than an additional moment.

At the square, the camera comes down among the gathering. In one view, people face towards the photographer with the monument behind them. In another, they stand in a loose line beneath the trees. Elsewhere, the white pedestal is visible across a comparatively open lawn. These are different views of the occasion, not a sequence whose exact order we can reconstruct. What survives is the range: marching, watching, gathering, and standing close enough to read.

The ground itself had a military history. A militia drill shed once stood here; it burned in the great Summerside fire of 1906. The town bought the property in 1908 and named it Dominion Square in 1911. Guns brought back after the First World War were placed there before the memorial. A place once used to prepare men for service had become a place to remember those who did not return. The history of Memorial Square.

There is a temptation, looking at a photograph like 3273, to supply the women with a particular loss: a son, a brother, a husband. The image does not give us that. Leaving their identities open does not make their presence less moving. Their attention is directed towards the names, and ours can follow it.

Two memorials in Charlottetown, two generations of loss

Charlottetown’s earlier Soldiers Monument was unveiled on July 6, 1903, in memory of Islanders Roland Taylor and Alfred Riggs, killed at Paardeberg during the South African War in February 1900. Hamilton MacCarthy’s bronze shows a soldier with fixed bayonet defending a disabled gun. The original plan for a more elaborate two-figure memorial proved too expensive; public fundraising eventually produced the single figure seen on these cards. The South African War memorial.

Card 0439 has an unusually personal addition. Its sender writes of seeing pictures of “these two young men, Taylor and Riggs.” The names on the memorial had led the visitor to portraits. That is a small but important movement: from a public monument to two faces. The writer’s further comment about the number of Islanders lost should not be treated as a complete casualty account; the useful evidence here is the encounter the writer describes.

The memorial’s language belongs to the imperial world in which it was raised. That setting is part of its history, alongside the deaths it commemorated. A person can read the wording critically and still recognise what it meant for families to have two names, two dates, and a place to stand.

Twenty-two years after that unveiling, a second major memorial was dedicated in Charlottetown. George William Hill’s group of three soldiers was unveiled on July 16, 1925. Its inscription remembers the Island’s Great War dead and also honours everyone who served. The woman who unveiled it was Matilda McLean, mother of the nursing sister Rena McLean. That choice brought a bereaved family directly into the public ceremony. The 1925 Soldiers’ Monument.

In 1671, the final line is simply 1914–1918. Later inscriptions would add the Second World War and Korea. That makes the postcard a record of an earlier stage in the monument’s life, when the “Great War” was the war its makers had just endured. Hill’s figures stand amid the familiar buildings of the town; the surrounding postcards show how the memorial became part of a place people crossed on ordinary days.

A boulder at O’Leary, a grotto at Tignish

The collection takes remembrance beyond the two largest centres. At O’Leary, card 2464 shows a substantial boulder bearing a plaque and wreath. It was mailed in March 1941. The photograph therefore preserves that form of the memorial during the Second World War, although the picture itself may be earlier. Veterans Affairs records that the original plaque was later retained within a rebuilt monument. A card like this lets us see the older setting of an inscription that outlasted it. O’Leary’s memorial.

Tignish chose a different form: a stone grotto, with a cross above and a religious figure sheltered inside. Built in 1947 by Edmond Laurent Gallant and Louis Harper, the memorial used land donated by the Sisters of Notre Dame. Its construction was a community effort, including stones hauled from Pleasant View. An archival photograph records the dedication on October 5, 1947. Card 3685, mailed the following August, shows the memorial near the beginning of its life. Tignish’s memorial and dedication photograph.

Placed beside the striding soldiers of Summerside and Charlottetown, these smaller memorials enlarge our view of what remembrance could look like. A sculpted figure, a named stone, a place for prayer: communities found different ways to give loss a visible and lasting home. The postcards preserve those choices without requiring every town to express its grief in the same way.

A nurse’s name, and care for the living

Rena McLean’s name also appeared on a building. She was a Canadian Army Medical Corps nursing sister who died when the hospital ship Llandovery Castle was sunk on June 27, 1918. All fourteen nursing sisters aboard were lost. In Charlottetown, a convalescent hospital opened that August and was named for her on the first anniversary of the sinking. The building stood east of Government House. Rena McLean and the hospital’s history.

The photograph is gentle: trees, grass, windows, a building set back from the camera. The archive describes convalescent care here and technical training in the basement. The hospital closed about 1920. For its brief working life, remembrance of a nurse was joined to practical help for returning service personnel. The Provincial Archives’ hospital record.

It is a fitting place to leave these cards. The monuments ask us to remember the dead; the hospital reminds us that the consequences of service continued among the living. And the people in Read’s photographs bring us back to the moment when a public inscription was still new, and someone could step out of a crowd to find a name.
