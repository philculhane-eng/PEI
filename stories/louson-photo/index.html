The history behind the picture

Looking for “Louson Photo”

On the Charlottetown wharf, a man stands with his back to us, watching the steamer Halifax. Down by his feet, three words in white read “Photo by Louson.” Look at the fishing boat on the Dunk River and the photographer has signed quite differently. Below the picture, on a pale strip of paper, are the words “Louson Photo” and the number 10.

“Photo by Louson”

Four views in the collection carry those three words. A fifth, the card with the Byron verse, gives his initials as well. The views are in alphabetical order by caption. Select one to see both sides.

The lighthouse card gives its entire address as “P. E. Island.” Two copies of that same view went to Miss Kattie J. Harris in Pictou. One reports the safe arrival of a trunk. On the other, Annie promises that Jennie will write tomorrow. Both were posted in Charlottetown in 1913, nineteen days apart. It makes you wonder how many family messages travelled on copies of that one photograph. The October copy and the November copy.

Pastoral Scene survives here in two prints. Follow the great leaning trunk down to the cow drinking in the water and you can match them branch for branch. Both prints carry No. 4. The gallery shows the lighter copy, which gives a little more detail in the shade. See the darker print, postcard 4664.

Looking through Louson’s camera

William Steele Louson came from Montreal to Charlottetown in 1900. He earned his living as a wholesale sales agent, work that took him around the Island. His photographs found readers in the Prince Edward Island Magazine and the Montreal Standard, and publishers put his views on picture postcards. Some of his pictures survive chiefly in that form. The scarcer signed real-photo cards in my collection offer a way to look at his work a few inches at a time. Harry Holman’s Louson exhibition at the Confederation Centre

In the Dunk River picture, two figures stand on the bank and a third is down at the boat. The eye has to find its way between the white birches to see them. No. 19 takes us out onto the ice. The Earl Grey fills the width of the card, smoke above the funnel and small dark figures beside her bow. Turn to No. 30 and men are out on the ice again, this time beside a boat they can haul themselves. Two ways of making a crossing fit into the same little run of photographs.

No. 37 gives us a canoe heading towards the camera and two banks that seem to close in behind it. There is a second A Quiet Stream in the PEI Museum Postcards Collection. The museum credits it to “Louson Photo,” just as the little strip beneath this print does. The museum’s card.

On the back of No. 12, a family wrote from Prince Edward Island on August 8 about seed potatoes and silver-fox farms. The stamp went through Quebec the next afternoon, August 9, 1927. Louson had died in 1921. Six years later a visitor could still choose his picture of two figures reflected in a Victoria Park pool and send it home.

One possible addition is A Never Ending Panorama of Rustic Beauty. The picture has the familiar foreground tree and a pale caption beneath it. The title is legible; a signature or number remains elusive. It is a promising lead to keep beside the signed views. Postcard 4561.
