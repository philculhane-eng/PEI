The history behind the picture

Spotlight on Souris

Souris is a town a postcard can explain from a distance. Harbour below, houses beyond, a church high on the slope: the eye can travel from the boats to the skyline without leaving the picture. Carter’s broad view gives the water most of the foreground, with wharves stretching across it and the buildings gathered behind. Even the railway fits into this compact arrangement.

The repeated choice of that viewpoint says something about Souris’s appeal. It had a legible shape and a handsome setting. But the closer cards are just as rewarding. They bring us into a shop-lined street, up to the church and convent, and out onto the breakwater with someone carrying an umbrella.

A town to walk through

McCoy’s Main Street view looks west along a road whose ruts are almost as conspicuous as its buildings. Wooden sidewalks keep pedestrians above the rough surface; shopfronts stand close to the street. This copy was already in the mail in August 1905. The photograph makes a useful companion to the closer view of the Sea View Hotel and Mabon’s Drug Store. Here people stand in the doorways, a bicycle leans at the front, and the signs tell us what can be found inside.

A later, undated RPPC looks along Main Street past a particularly substantial neighbour. At the left of card 3979 stands the Post Office and Customs House, built in 1905. Its Island sandstone came from nearby St. Catherine’s; the paler trim is Wallace freestone from Nova Scotia. The rough masonry gives this corner a weight and texture all its own. The building’s heritage record identifies both materials and its original postal and customs uses.

Above the roofs

St. Mary’s supplied photographers with a different landmark. William Critchlow Harris’s larger church was completed in 1902, with walls built from sandstone quarried at Chepstow. The postcard showing church and convent together makes their position on the hillside wonderfully clear. The open ground between them allows the two buildings to command the picture. A closer photograph of the convent, mailed in 1914, replaces that distant outline with steps, windows, dormers and a welcoming front entrance. St. Mary’s architectural history helps place the church in this early twentieth-century landscape.

Acorn’s Souris

Morley S. Acorn’s name gives this town album a particularly welcome photographic voice. His multi-view card, sent in April 1913, assembles streets, church, woods and water into a little exhibition. The arrangement lets us see his range at once. Buildings matter, but so do boats, shorelines and the light falling on them.

On the long breakwater, Acorn makes the curve of the walkway lead towards a solitary figure holding an umbrella. The printed claim that this was the longest breakwater in Eastern Canada is part of the card’s sales pitch. What the photograph gives us is more immediate: a narrow path, an exposed edge and a large stretch of sea. It is an arresting choice for a souvenir, especially beside the conventional town panorama.

His photograph of the Cox Hotel motorboat Louise C. takes us off the shore altogether. People sit beneath its canopy as it passes in front of a sailing vessel. The writer has added “My old boat & Souris Harbor.” A commercial view has become a personal possession: someone knew this launch from experience. It gives the hotel trade a delightful extra dimension. A stay in Souris could include looking back at the town from the water.

Across these cards, Souris offers the camera both a clear town portrait and a succession of small invitations. Walk the main street. Climb the hill. Stand on the breakwater. Take a boat out. The photographs work especially well together because the harbour keeps reappearing from another angle, tying the grand view to the everyday pleasures of the place.
