The history behind the picture

From Board Ice to Board Ice

Fred Kelly of North Bedeque wore low shoes to cross Northumberland Strait in winter. He had bought passage in a boat and expected to sit in it. By the time he reached New Brunswick he had been in water up to his knees. Years later, when Helen Creighton recorded him, he remembered the distance and the time quite precisely. Cape Traverse to Cape Tormentine was nine miles. He had done it once in three hours and once in nine. Fred Kelly remembers the crossing, September 1956

The postcards make good company for Fred. Some say “Ice Boats,” some say “Crossing at the Capes,” and one simply calls it “The Old Way.” I found eleven different views among forty-one cards. Here they are, one copy of each. Where a publisher or a photographer gives us a set, I’ve kept it together. The other pairs let us look at the same piece of work from different positions. Open any picture to see the whole card and turn it over.

Warwick’s two views of the crossing

“Striking Board Ice” is an odd-sounding caption until you know what a passenger’s ticket promised. The board ice was the ice fast to the shore. Out beyond its edge the ice could move under you. On a two-dollar ticket preserved in copy at the Island’s Public Archives, the passage offered was “from board ice to board ice.” That little phrase tells you where the boatmen’s part of the journey began and ended. The Archives reads this as a fare for someone expected to help haul. A passenger who wanted to stay aboard paid more. The Cape Traverse–Cape Tormentine passenger ticket

The Toronto firm of Warwick Brothers & Rutter put numbers 2669 and 2675 on two photographs that turn up over and over in the collection. Look at the line of people against the sky in the first one, then at the foreground of the second. Even if you could see a perfectly straight course to New Brunswick, how would you get a loaded boat across those upended slabs? On the right of the second picture two figures in long coats stand a little apart. I’d dearly like to hear their account of the day.

The pictures with the sails up

The first two photographs have a name attached to them, though you could turn these cards over all afternoon without finding it. Harry Holman traced them to the April 1903 Prince Edward Island Magazine. Its frontispiece calls the first scene “A Hard Pull” and credits W. Erdley Hyndman. A few pages on, there is the second, down to the little figure at the far right, under the caption “Rough Road to Travel.” The magazine puts both photographs in print by April 1903. It also preserves a third Hyndman view, “An Easy Stretch,” with the boats strung out across much more level going. The original illustrated issue · Harry Holman’s identification

There’s a second way these cards fit together. Haszard & Moore sold “Ice Boat Service from P.E.I. to Mainland” in Charlottetown. Taylor’s Bookstore sold “Crossing to Prince Edward Island in Winter.” Turn those two over and the red printing matches almost line for line, down to the Belgian printing credit in the stamp box. On Taylor’s card a man is down on one knee beneath the sail. The matching backs tell us something quite definite about the making of the cards. The photographer of the Taylor view remains an open question.

One copy of the Haszard & Moore picture went through the Charlottetown post office on December 7, 1906. The writer signed it “Bird” and addressed it to Mother in Hamilton. “Leaving here tomorrow to escape being frozen in.” That one line tells Mother why a departure date mattered. Whether Bird got away the next day is another question. Bird’s message home

Two portraits at the capes

Now the men have time to face the camera. In the McCoy view the two bows point towards us, and the people standing down on the ice give a fair measure of the boats’ size. The wider view was sold by William Kennedy, a Summerside druggist. There are sails at the left and bare hulls at the right. One fellow has found a seat out in front of the group. This copy went through the post at Carleton in August 1907. Someone picked out a winter scene to send in the middle of an Island summer.

The captions give Cape Tormentine as the New Brunswick end. Nearby Jourimain Island was a landing place in its own right. The Allen family took iceboat crews into their home between trips, and the house came to be known as the Allen Hotel. The Cape Jourimain Nature Centre traces the crossing much further back. Mi’kmaq travellers used the point to cross to Epekwitk long before mailbags went that way. The crossing and the Allen Hotel at Cape Jourimain

When there was room to row

The dark strip across Carter’s card is worth enlarging. It resolves into hulls and people, one party following another along the ice. The oval view catches the change that Fred Kelly described. There is open water enough for the boats to float, the crews are at the oars, and one person still stands on the ice at the right. These are two different photographs. The oval picture itself sometimes turns up cropped much closer and captioned “The Old Way.” The gap of water and that person at its edge give it away.

The boat had runners underneath. On the trip Kelly described, eight people hauled and one woman stayed aboard. When they met ice strong enough to bear the boat, the haulers slipped the straps over their shoulders, lifted it from the water and pulled. At the next patch of water everybody could climb in and row again. The strap had a second use. If the ice gave way beneath a hauler, the line still connected that person to a boat that could float. Even after reaching Cape Tormentine, Kelly still had the ride to Sackville by horse and sleigh. Imagine packing for all three parts of that journey. He particularly remembered getting the shoes wrong.

Two real photographs at shoulder height

Here at last you can see how those straps sit on a person. In the broadside photograph, follow the pale diagonal across the nearest man’s dark coat. There are people down inside the boat while the men beside it lean into their work. In the other photograph the line stretches away from us. You can pick out the boots of the man nearest the camera and the hull right beside him. That print carries a signature and a number, “Louson Photo 30.” The broadside print has its own caption, “Crossing the Ice,” and gives us no photographer’s name. What else they had aboard

The mail is easy to miss in a postcard. It was the reason for this service in the first place. Parks Canada dates the start of the Cape Traverse–Cape Tormentine mail crossing to December 19, 1827. In the 1890s the Cape Traverse telegraph operator, T. C. Muncey, kept a journal. Along with the weather he entered departures, arrivals, passengers and the number of bags. A family waiting for a letter on the Island and another waiting in New Brunswick depended on the work he recorded from shore. The Capes mail service · Muncey’s journal, 1894–96

One winter’s figures give those little hulls an astonishing carrying record. When the steamer Stanley stopped making her daily trips on February 8, 1895, the iceboats took up the service. By April 12 they had carried 3,497 bags of mail. The federal return counted nine passengers hauled in the boats and seventy-seven “strap passengers.” It even counted seventy-six pounds of express goods. In two months the boatmen had delivered all that over the kind of surface we’ve just been looking at. Department of Marine and Fisheries, “Ice Boat Mail Service,” scan 85

The night the boats became shelter

One passenger left a much longer account than would fit on a postcard. On January 27, 1885, Philip Farrell set out for Cape Tormentine in a party of twenty-two, fifteen crew and seven passengers divided among three boats. In the poem he later wrote, I’m struck by what the men were looking for as the weather closed in. They knew a house on the other shore. Again and again a drift piled on the ice looked enough like it to raise their hopes.

Once they knew they were lost, they arranged the boats to give them shelter. The water in their kegs had frozen. Daylight came and they still couldn’t see land. By the following afternoon, when a shore finally showed, it was over towards Crapaud, back on Prince Edward Island. Even then there was a strip of marsh between the exhausted party and firm ground. Neighbours went out for the people who could go no farther. Farrell made room in his poem for Mrs. A. McPhail, who cared for them afterwards. His recollection comes into this collection by a curious route. He was the father of the man who married the likely sitter in our signed Alice Maude Kinch portrait. The tunnel card gets the ice right

J. A. S. Bayer’s tunnel card has a joke you need a light to see. A train is printed on the address side. Hold the picture up to a lamp and the train runs under the Strait between the two capes. The little verse around the picture gives the prospective passenger three good reasons for going underground. One is wind, one is frost, and the one in between is lolly.

Lolly is that soft mixture of ice and floating snow that can look like a surface from a distance. It won’t carry a person the way board ice will. It also fills the very water a crew would like to row through. The white of the paper does service for all these things in our postcards. To somebody with a strap over one shoulder, the differences mattered considerably. The writer of this particular copy was having trouble with the roads on land as well. The plan for Monday morning, if they stayed bad, was snowshoes. Parks Canada on Island ice words · Both sides of Bayer’s tunnel card

When the icebreakers needed help

The iceboats and the big steamers worked in the same winters. On the eastern route a steamer could take people between Georgetown and Pictou, Nova Scotia, while the little boats worked between the two capes farther west. After the Northern Light came two steel-hulled icebreakers, the Stanley and the Minto. The Stanley postcard gives the government ship a rather good unofficial title. Fred, writing to his father, called her the “great ice smasher.” Look just ahead of her bow in the Summerside picture. People and horses are standing on what she was built to get through. The Coast Guard’s history of the Island icebreakers

The Minto card says “Icebound” right on the front. The great heaps banked up along her hull explain why a ticket to Pictou could involve a considerable wait. There’s a revealing photograph in the same 1903 magazine that printed Hyndman’s iceboats. W. S. Louson photographed Captain Finlayson of the Minto and Captain Brown of the Stanley beside the diver who repaired the Minto’s propeller. Between the two captains in their caps stands the diver in his helmet. Louson’s photograph of the captains and the diver

Then there is the photograph marked February 25, 1914. The Earl Grey and the Minto sit some distance apart, and the small dark marks between them are people. The writer had travelled on the Earl Grey and explains that the photograph shows her coming to the Minto’s relief. Even aboard the newer ship a crossing could end where it began. In February 1911 a traveller reported to Carrie that they had left Georgetown at seven, gone about ten miles and turned back in heavy ice. By the time the card was written Carrie’s correspondent was still in Georgetown. The note to Carrie, February 21, 1911

A railway carriage across the Strait

At Cape Tormentine, Louson photographed the ferry Prince Edward Island beside the railway loading gear. She was three hundred feet long, with two tracks on the train deck and room for a dozen railway cars. They went in through her stern. She reached the Island in 1915 and served Charlottetown, Georgetown and Pictou while the terminals were being finished. In 1917 she took up the Borden–Cape Tormentine run. That was the year the old Cape iceboat service came to an end. The ferry’s history and surviving engineers’ records

The person who sent the Louson view had crossed with Mme. Grondin on an automobile trip with friends. What the writer wanted the recipient to understand was that whole railway cars could go aboard, just as they did on the ferries from France to England. Then came a little instruction to look again at the picture. “See the ice.”

I’d give the last word to Hugh, who sent the upright photograph of the ladder. There’s a passenger partway up a ladder against the side of the ship, with people waiting at the rail above. Hugh supplied the part outside the frame. The previous winter the car ferry had been unable to reach her wharf, so the passengers walked out over the ice and climbed aboard on ladders. The Island had a ship that could carry a train. Fred Kelly would still have had good reason to pack a second pair of shoes.
