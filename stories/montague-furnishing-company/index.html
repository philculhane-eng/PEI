The history behind the picture

The Montague Furnishing Company’s Casket Cards

Look up at the roofs on this picture of the Montague Furnishing Company. The artist has written “Dry Kiln No. 2” along one of them. Just beyond it are stacks of lumber. A warehouse stands to the right, an office to the left, and the company name runs across the front of the long building in the middle. Turn from that picture to the company’s advertising postcards and you can see what some of that wood became.

Four caskets in the collection

Each card names Montague and gives the casket a model number. Three show a separate view of the end, where a buyer could compare the handle and fittings. Open the fronts side by side and the differences that mattered to the maker begin to show.

No. 28 has plain, dark panels with stepped edges. On No. 29 the lining has a diamond pattern that continues down the flap draped over the side. No. 33 rewards a second look at the little end view. The curling decoration runs right up over the rounded lid. No. 144 fills the photograph by itself, its pale lining spilling over the edge. The photographs let an undertaker examine those details and specify the model in a letter.

Turn the cards over and two more firms enter the story. The back of No. 29 credits the L. L. Cook Co. of Milwaukee along the dividing line. On No. 144 the Canadian Post Card Co., Ltd., Toronto, printed its name down the left edge. Montague is the maker’s address on both fronts. Read the Milwaukee credit and the Toronto credit.

“Ship two more suites same as last”

Another card in the collection takes us inside the furniture business on a particular working day. On July 19, 1900, D. L. McKinnon wrote from Montague Bridge to a furniture company in Nova Scotia. “Ship two more suites same as last,” he asked, adding instructions about payment and a discount. The printed side offers parlour, dining and bedroom suites, window blinds, wallpaper and carpets. A line in heavy capitals also promises “Undertaking in all its branches.” One everyday order brings both sides of the business together.

The historic register’s account of the MacKinnon family traces the business to about 1880, when Donald L. MacKinnon had two employees making household furniture and coffins south of the bridge. After a fire destroyed the factory in 1911, the firm moved into a former Owen Connolly warehouse on the north side. Following the Halifax Explosion in 1917, the register records that the Montague plant worked around the clock to supply caskets. Canadian Register of Historic Places, the MacKinnon family home.

By 1930 the Montague firm was the only Prince Edward Island establishment listed in the federal survey of coffin and casket manufacturers. The national report counts lumber, linings, coverings and hardware among the industry’s purchases. Those categories are almost a guide to reading the postcards. Look at the woodwork on No. 28, the lining on No. 29 and the metal fittings picked out against the dark sides. Dominion Bureau of Statistics, 1929–30 report.

There is another place to continue the search, in Montague itself. The Garden of the Gulf Museum holds a series of casket advertising postcards in the papers of Athol M. MacKinnon, who ran the family firm with his sister Olga and brother Eric. Its finding aid describes the series as a group. If another numbered card turns up, the photograph and the number together will tell us where it belongs. Athol M. MacKinnon collection, Fonds64.
