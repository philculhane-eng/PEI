The history behind the picture

PECO: An Island Album in Many Editions

Turn a pile of PECO postcards face down and a second Island album begins to appear. There are shields and maple leaves, flowing French lettering and sturdy English capitals. Turn them over again and the changing backs have gathered roads, rivers, hotels and ferry crossings into families.

Our first PECO chapter followed the late bilingual back of card 2314. This chapter works back through the earlier cards, then brings the neighbouring Toronto designs into view. Together the two albums begin a much fuller picture of PECO’s Island work.

From a printer’s name to an Island journey

PECO stood for the Photogelatine Engraving Company. Rowley Shillito Hooper founded his printing business in 1917 and incorporated it in Ottawa in 1920. Ken L. Elder’s history traces early monochrome cards, a colour line introduced in 1930, and a move of the main operation to Toronto in 1947. The company closed in 1954. Earlier photographs continued to appear in later editions, giving some views a remarkably long life. Elder’s history for the Toronto Postcard Club

Those dates provide the outline; the postcards give it texture. A shield in the stamp box belongs to a very different-looking object from a glossy colour view with a diagonal divider. Yet the hotel, the river or the ship may already be familiar. An Island scene could travel through several of the printer’s designs.

The albums below follow visible back designs. Their names describe what you can see on the cards. Within the broad Ottawa-to-Toronto story, overlapping use and reprinting leave room for a more detailed chronology as dated examples accumulate.

The little shield

A small shield saying “Made in Canada” marks this family. Its fronts favour quiet monochrome tones, with lettering that often feels close to the photograph. Beach Grove, the Dunk River and the capital’s streets make a particularly inviting opening album.

The broad Ottawa colour album

This is the great colour journey: streets and inns, red soil and green fields, boats, beaches and the places associated with Anne of Green Gables. Monochrome companions share the design. Identical photographs can carry different numbers—Beechwood Gardens appears as 83 in colour and 201 in monochrome—so the linked copies are part of the pleasure.

The Island that keeps returning

Beach Grove is a lovely place to begin following a photograph through the editions. The putting scene appears with the little shield on 2125 and with the flowing French heading on 0173. The pose and the putting green hold steady while the printer’s arrangement changes on the back. The hotel’s driveway, shoreline and distant view from the beach build a small holiday around it.

The same pleasure awaits at Green Gables and Dalvay. Visitors could choose the house, the path, the woods, a golf-course view or a glimpse across the water. When these cards sit together, a famous building becomes a place to move around in. The cards preserve approaches, leisure spaces and neighbouring scenery as well as the attraction itself.

PECO also had an eye for the working Island. The gallery holds a potato harvest, unloading coal at Summerside, a fox farm seen from above and a fisherman sorting his catch at Borden. Some fronts give their photographic suppliers a credit: “R.C.A.F. Photo” appears on the Summerside aerial and experimental fox farm, while several later views credit the National Film Board. Each printed credit offers a route into the history behind an individual image.

A number needs the company of its picture. In the Ottawa album, no. 20 appears on two Fort Edward views, and no. 31 belongs to Green Gables as well as the Alberton fox-industry monument. No. 67 takes us to an inlet near Tignish and to a landscape whose location is still being investigated. Those surprises explain why the gallery follows the images as carefully as the numbers.

Then there are the souvenir greetings. A pennant can say Kensington or Souris while the little scene beside it remains unlocated. These cards show how a local greeting could be assembled from a publisher’s stock of attractive pictures. They have their own charm, and their captions reward a little curiosity.

The late bilingual Toronto album continues the journey with another 98 entries. Together these chapters gather 452 catalogued cards. There will be gaps in the surviving record, and surely more discoveries to make. For now, spreading the backs into families has given us a wonderfully broad Island to wander through.

About the albums

The groups follow the visible heading, divider, company imprint and stamp-box design. Small ink-colour and spacing changes remain together. A sample reverse illustrates every group. Fronts were compared visually; repeated photographs and modest crop or colour variants share an entry, with each copy still accessible. The totals describe this collection’s present holdings.

Display titles are shortened for reading and alphabetising; the linked records preserve the catalogue descriptions. Cards from Pictou Landing, Digby, Cape Tormentine and Muskoka retain their actual locations. Generic greeting scenes and the two unresolved lighthouse captions are labelled individually. A printer’s city, postal date and photograph’s date are separate clues.

Company history follows Ken L. Elder’s study linked above. Back designs, image comparisons, credits and numbers are taken from the cards themselves. The 1924 and 1935 examples can be examined through records 0402 and 2213.
