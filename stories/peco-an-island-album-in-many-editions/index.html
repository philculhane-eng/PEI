The history behind the picture

PECO: The Island Collection

PECO stood for the Photogelatine Engraving Company, a Canadian postcard printer based first in Ottawa and later in Toronto. Its Island cards carried pictures of familiar places far beyond our shores: Charlottetown’s streets, country churches, fishing harbours, summer hotels and the ferries that brought visitors here. Together they offer a leisurely tour of Prince Edward Island in the decades before colour photography became commonplace on postcards.

The pictures invite a closer look, and the backs tell a little of their publishing history. The albums bring together cards with matching backs and separate their numbered runs. Numbered cards follow their printed sequence; unnumbered groups follow their subjects alphabetically. Within them are changing views, familiar photographs printed again, and small details of Island life waiting to be rediscovered.

452 catalogued cards · 321 gallery entries · 18 albums
Open an album below to see its identifying back and three-column gallery. Repeated copies are linked beneath a representative front; a photograph can appear again when its back belongs to another edition.

Ken L. Elder’s history traces a printing business founded in 1917, incorporated in Ottawa in 1920, and producing a colour line from 1930. Its main operation moved to Toronto in 1947; the company closed in 1954. Older photographs continued through later editions, giving familiar views long lives in the postcard rack. Following a picture through the editions

Beach Grove makes a lovely starting point. The putting scene appears with the little shield on 2125 and with the flowing French heading on 0173. The pose and green hold steady as the printer’s arrangement changes on the back. The driveway, shoreline and distant view build a small holiday around the hotel.

At Green Gables and Dalvay, the house becomes part of a wider place: paths, woods, golf-course views, water and visitors. PECO also found a working Island in the potato harvest, a fisherman’s catch, coal being unloaded at Summerside and a fox farm photographed from above. Some cards name their photographic suppliers, including the R.C.A.F. and the National Film Board.

The numbers have their own surprises. Beechwood Gardens appears as 83 in colour and 201 in monochrome. In the ordinary sequence, no. 31 belongs to Green Gables as well as the Alberton fox-industry monument. That is why the albums preserve different photographs under a shared number and give each copy a route back to its catalogue record.

About the albums and sources

The back designs bring these cards into families through their headings, dividers, imprints and stamp boxes. Within each family, printed numbers guide the order. Groups without numbers are alphabetical. The broad Ottawa family has separate colour and monochrome albums.

Repeated copies of a photograph share an entry when their back design, number and colour treatment agree. Their catalogue records are linked beneath the picture. Different views under a shared number have separate entries, as do colour and monochrome editions in the broad Ottawa family.

Generic greetings and unresolved lighthouse locations are identified beside the pictures. Cards from Pictou Landing, Digby, Cape Tormentine and Muskoka retain their locations, offering a glimpse of PECO’s wider Canadian trade.

Company history follows Ken L. Elder’s study linked above. The cards supply the back designs, printed numbers and picture comparisons. A contemporary 1949 printing-trades directory, page M-29, places the company at 3551 Danforth Avenue, Toronto.
