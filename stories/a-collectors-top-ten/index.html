PEI’s Ten Greatest Postcards

If I could lay just ten Island postcards on a table, which ones would I want you to turn over? One needs to be held to the light. One has a name pencilled below a portrait the publisher left unnamed. Another has a photograph timed to the minute. I can lay nine of them on the table today. I’m keeping the tenth place for a card I’ve only ever seen once. Each title opens its story.

  1. The Tunnel · 1865. Hold it up to the light and a train on the back appears to pass under the Northumberland Strait. The sender had a practical reason for wanting that tunnel. The note describes bad roads and a possible journey on snowshoes. On another copy Nellie tells her mother where they might live when the tunnel is built.
  2. The New Railway Station in Montague · 4603. I saw a version of the first-train card when I started collecting and waited ten years for one to come up for sale. The engine shares the waterfront with schooners. A timetable lets us follow the six-mile branch, and two Georgetown prints show the station at the other end of the opening excursion.
  3. The Million-Dollar Fox Train · 3716. Someone took the trouble to print names up from the bottom of the photograph to the men beside engine No. 20. On the other side, James Paton put his name to it too, and underlined his job. Two copies of Bayer’s wider view show what the engine was pulling. A baggage master remembered guarding the live foxes. “Million-Dollar” is the publisher’s billing.
  4. Frank Snake in Two Summerside Postcards · 4183. Two publishers sold the same portrait of a man with a pipe and a gun over his shoulder. Each gave it a different title. His name is pencilled under the portrait on 0053, and the Nova Scotia Museum identifies the same photograph as Frank Snake of Lennox Island. A third copy went to Massachusetts with the sender’s verdict on the visit.
  5. A Christmas Card from One Chief to Another · 3279. Chief Matthew Francis of Pictou Landing is in the photograph and signed the greeting. He addressed it to a chief in Truro. Martha turns up again in a smaller family portrait. The man addressed as John on the reverse and the man called Joe on the front remain a question.
  6. Troops Embarking at Charlottetown · 4665. Island artillerymen have found seats wherever they can aboard the Cacouna. Two gun barrels show in the foreground. The City Archives identifies a wider view of the August 5, 1914, departure. Annie’s family postcard tells us what was already being said on the Island two days before it.
  7. The Night St. Dunstan’s Burned · 4012. Walter G. Stiff photographed the fire at 2:30 in the morning. His 2:55 caption records the fall of the south tower’s top. The timed cards tell the story of the spires, and an address on the photograph leads from Charlottetown to a photographer’s family on Cape Cod.
  8. Largest Ever Caught on P.E. Island · 0102. Fourteen pounds in one hand, twelve in the other. Those are the weights Pugh printed. The fisherman gets the whole front and the lobsters get all the billing. A July 1911 postmark dates a second copy.
  9. The Card That Started My Collection · 0126. An antiques dealer I’d known for years sent me a picture of four strangers in a carriage. Thirty-three minutes later I could tell him the name of the bareheaded man on the right. A newspaper portrait and sixteen numbered prints helped to place the carriage in Charlottetown’s Maritime Odd Fellows gathering of 1908. This is the card that got me collecting.
  10. My Heart’s Desire. I was the underbidder by sixty-five cents. I’ve never seen another copy. I’m saving the space, and keeping the name to myself.
