The history behind the picture

PEI’s Greatest Postcards – #2 – The New Railway Station in Montague

About ten years ago, when I started collecting Island postcards, I saw a version of this one. I love trains, and here was the first train at Montague, drawn up beside the platform with steam blowing across the waterfront. I wanted a copy. For ten years I couldn’t find one for sale. In 2026 one finally turned up. Here it is, number two in my series of favourite PEI postcards.

P. D. Ayer & Co. of Moncton put the words “First Train” down in the right-hand corner. My eye goes to the little group beside the coach. They have barely room to stand between it and the edge of the platform. Look past the engine, though, and there are masts. Even in the postcard announcing that the railway had come to Montague, the boats got into the picture.

Six miles that mattered

By 1901, people in Montague Bridge were holding public meetings about the inconvenience of having a railway to Georgetown and no rail connection of their own. One local man’s working life gives that complaint some substance. John J. MacDonald, who ran the Village Hotel, used to carry the mail from Montague to Cardigan. The gap that looks so small on a railway map was somebody’s regular journey.

Tenders for the Montague line went out in October 1904. A year later crews were laying the last rails and working on the station interior. The provincial heritage account says the grand opening was set for Dominion Day in 1906. People turned out to greet the locomotive, and passengers rode to Georgetown and back. Ayer printed “First Train” without a day or a year. I would keep the official opening and the moment of the photograph separate. My copy has no dated message. The PEI Museum collection has an Ayer copy of this same title sent from Montague to Charlottetown on October 2, 1908. Its writer was asking about a previous postcard and sending news of her mother. It is a secure date by which customers could buy the first-train view. The museum’s posted copy · The Station, Canadian Register of Historic Places

Pugh Manufacturing of Toronto made the building the attraction on card 4623. They framed it in a red flag, but the best bit of lettering is in the photograph itself. MONTAGUE runs across the slope of the station roof, large enough to read from across the tracks. Under the deep eaves a row of people faces the camera. Whoever sent this card to Medford, Massachusetts, left the recipient a question. “What do you think of this place?”

In the January 1907 timetable the outbound express ran daily except Sunday. The railway timetable gives the 7:05 a.m. departure from Montague nineteen minutes to reach Montague Junction. Robertson, Brudenell and Roseneath lay along those six miles. At all three it stopped on signal or to set down a passenger. The first stop out was Robertson at 7:10. From there the printed times were 7:14 at Brudenell, 7:21 at Roseneath and 7:24 at the junction. One could travel the whole 6.2 miles before a caller who had just missed the train in Montague had sat down to breakfast. At the waterfront end of the branch a turntable let the crew turn the locomotive for the journey back. January 1907 railway timetable

The station at the other end of the excursion

What did people see when the excursion train reached Georgetown? There are two copies of William Cumming’s Georgetown station photograph here, and the difference between them is worth the detour. Compare Montague’s long low roof with the high many-sided tower at Georgetown. Both pictures have people standing at the platform edge, and both buildings had the town name on the roof. The Georgetown station would have been unmistakable even if the lettering had been too small to read.

The published Georgetown card, 2133, credits Cumming and names A. Maclean & Co. in the narrow space beside the picture. Warwick Bros. & Rutter’s number 2512 appears there too. The same people in the same positions are on 3320, the collection’s monochrome salesman’s sample. Its plain reverse has the number in pencil. On one side of this pair we get a clear view of the building. On the other we get a glimpse of how a local railway station became a numbered product a merchant could order. The men posing beside Georgetown station are unnamed. The photograph gives the excursion a destination we can see. Cumming’s Georgetown Station · The sample for number 2512

There is even a second salesman’s sample from Georgetown which shows where the tracks could take you in winter. On 3345 a passenger car enters at the left. Straight ahead, almost bow-on, is the Minto. People stand between the railway car and the steamer. Others have gone right out onto the ice beside the ship, where you can pick out the horses. The sample says simply “Georgetown, P.E.I.” and gives the number 4543. On the coloured card sold to the public the printer supplied both the name of the steamer and “Winter, 1905.” That was the winter before the official opening at Montague. The new branch was connecting the town to a railway that already brought trains down to the winter boats. The Georgetown sample, 3345 · The named, coloured view, 3356

Farmers loading vessels

At the far left of card 4598 is the same station. You could easily miss it among the horses, wagon wheels and schooner rigging. The woman who sent the card knew exactly where she wanted her correspondent to look. “This is a picture of what takes place here every fall. Farmers loading vessels,” Agatha wrote to Miss Gladys Dodge in Alton, New Hampshire. She went on to mention the cold. Her card reached Alton in December 1906, the very year the branch opened. The train was new enough to merit a souvenir. For Agatha, this was something the farmers did every autumn. Read Agatha’s message

The title on card 3502 is simply “Port at Montague, P.E.I.” Follow the shore behind the schooner masts and you’ll find a line of boxcars. The sky has a wash of pink. The cars are so dark and close together that, at postcard size, they almost pass for a fence. Between Agatha’s card and this one you can see how little ground separated a farmer’s wagon, a vessel’s hold and a railway siding. This copy went through the post at Elmira in August 1927. That dates the camper’s pencilled message on the back and leaves the age of the waterfront view open.

In the real photo postcard of the S.S. Enterprise, the station is tucked behind the steamer. A little open boat with three people aboard has come alongside, and an empty white boat takes up much of the foreground. At that distance it’s hard to imagine a long journey. Yet in the 1908 navigation season the Enterprise was contracted for two round trips a week from Montague to Pictou by way of Georgetown and Murray Harbour. The government return even gives her capacity in barrels. She could carry 1,200 of them. The steam railway and the steamship each had a timetable, and here they met within sight of one another. Federal steamship contract and vessel return, pp. 58–59

The contract itself is dated July 17, 1908. Alongside the two weekly Pictou trips it required a third circuit by Georgetown and Souris to ports on the Nova Scotia side, including Port Hood when the weather allowed. The agreement provided for carrying mail and a subsidy of $6,000 at an annual rate. The sailing season was to last as long as the ice permitted. Set those terms alongside the January railway timetable and the difference is striking. The railway could promise a January departure at five minutes past seven. On the steamship route the end of the working year depended on what happened to the water.

It’s easy to look through an album like this one and feel that everything happened on a single busy afternoon. Agatha’s visit to the post office belongs to 1906. The contract belongs to 1908. The camper with the boxcar view was writing in 1927. Another visitor, writing to Massachusetts in 1947, sent a photograph of two former naval motor launches at Montague. Those later boats make a good follow-up for anyone curious about the wharf in another generation. The Fairmiles and Poole & Thompson’s warehouse

In 1910 the station figured in a visit by Prime Minister Wilfrid Laurier. The town bought the building in 1984 and turned it into a welcome centre.

I keep coming back to that little question on the flag card. What do I think of the place? For someone who loves trains, a spot on that platform would have suited me very well. You could have watched an engine turn and kept an eye on the boats at the same time. It took me ten years, but I finally have the view.
