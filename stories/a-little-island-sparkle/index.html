A Little Island Sparkle

I adore sparkly colours. Give me a postcard with purple flowers, a generous helping of gold, and an Island town’s name written in something that catches the light, and I am very happy indeed.

These little cards are a feast: violets and roses, curling ribbons, pink borders, forget-me-nots, and greetings that wander across the picture in wonderfully individual lettering. “Just a few lines” announces one Alberton card, and then proceeds to make those lines as pretty as possible.

Fourteen cards were already gathered under “Sparkles” in the catalogue. A closer look through the greeting cards added two more: the violet Charlottetown card, 1021, and the pink Cardigan card, 4156. Here are all sixteen, arranged by the place named on the front. Cape Tormentine and Pictou join the Island towns from across the Strait.

A town’s name, with a flourish

There is a lovely little transformation at work in these cards. A ready-made floral design leaves room for a place name; the added letters turn it into a greeting from Alberton, Cardigan, Charlottetown, or Tyne Valley. The writing has its own character. Some names are neat and compact, some stretch across the whole card, and Freetown’s congratulations seem determined to reach from corner to corner.

At full size, the Cardigan lettering on 4156 shows tiny uneven flecks catching the scan’s light. On 1021, much of the decoration has worn away, leaving scattered bright and dark traces along the name. Those little survivals are part of the appeal. A scan gives us the colours and the shapes; turning the original card in the light would give us another part of the pleasure.

These cards bring together several designs and publishers. Both the Alberton “Just a few lines” and Charlottetown “Just a greeting” examples carry the small number 532 on their fronts. The two ornate Cardigan floral cards name E. R. Stiles & Co. of Moncton on their backs. The similarities invite a little browsing, but for today the bouquets are quite enough to enjoy.

Even the steamship gets some gold

The Campana is the surprise guest. Look along the rigging and around the ship and waterfront: little gold-coloured touches have been added to the view. A working harbour has acquired its own party clothes. The card was posted at Pictou in January 1905, and its sender reported being there to have photographs taken.

That is one of the pleasures of turning these cards over. Behind the bright decoration is the same everyday correspondence found throughout the collection. Sadie’s St. Peters greeting reports that the baby is ten months old and has one tooth. Ethel’s Tyne Valley card thanks her cousin Nellie for a picture, calls it lovely, and hopes that she will still come home for a visit.

For all the flourish on the front, the messages are often very small and very human. A tooth. A photograph. A hoped-for visit. Love to everybody. The sparkle gives those ordinary kindnesses a particularly cheerful envelope.

And sometimes that is all a postcard needs to do: carry a little brightness from one person to another.

A small note on the sparkle

This gallery combines the fourteen cards catalogued as “Sparkles” with two additional examples, 1021 and 4156, identified from their scans. Ninety-seven other greeting and souvenir candidates were reviewed. The scans show applied, reflective decoration; its exact material and the person who added it remain open questions. “Sparkles” is the collection’s descriptive grouping.
