The history behind the picture

The View from the Minister’s Window

The story begins with a few words written in ink: “View from Study window.” Beneath them is a name, Edwin Smith.

There is something wonderfully intimate about that caption. It gives no village, province or landmark. Whoever received the photograph was presumably expected to understand—or could simply enjoy seeing what Smith saw from his window. Beyond the fences and little trees in the foreground, the river holds the reflections of the buildings opposite. A wooden bridge crosses at the right, with schooner masts rising beyond it. The view brings a waterfront community almost into the room.

A second photographic postcard bears another handwritten caption, “Shipping Season,” and the shorter signature “E. Smith.” Here the vessels demand attention. Schooners lie close beside the bridge, their masts and rigging crowded together. Small boats sit beneath them, while the bridge carries the landward business of the village past the shipping at its edge. Smith has given us two aspects of the same place: the broad, reflective prospect and the concentration of activity within it.

Neither copy carries a stamp, address or postal cancellation. Turn them over and they match in another small particular. Each has the same “Private Post Card” heading and one-cent stamp box. Faint pencil marks on the backs include the consecutive numbers PE 591 and PE 592. The numbers are a later reader’s clue to the pair. The photographs give us no name or date for that numbering. The writing is ink applied to the finished photographs, preserving a personal connection that a printed caption cannot quite supply.

Then two other cards bring the scene into focus.

The photograph labelled “Shipping Season” also appears as “Shipping Time, Cardigan Bridge, P. E. Island,” published by P. D. Ayer & Co., printers in Moncton. The correspondence is unmistakable: the same vessels, the same rigging, the same buildings. The commercial version includes more of the scene at the edges, suggesting access to a less tightly cropped version of the photograph.

The study-window picture has its commercial counterpart too. Ayer issued it as “A Calm Day, Cardigan Bridge, P. E. Island.” With that title, an unnamed personal outlook becomes an identified Island view. Smith’s private caption tells us something the publisher’s title cannot: the particular window from which Smith said the picture was seen. The publisher, in return, tells us where his window looked.

The fifth card supplies the missing title before the name.

Below the photograph of St. Andrew’s Church, Cardigan, a printed credit reads: “Photo by Rev. E. Smith.” This card, too, came from P. D. Ayer & Co. Taken together, the signatures, matching photographs, location and explicit credit point convincingly to the Reverend Edwin Smith as the photographer behind this little group.

The tower on the church card is worth a second look. The heritage register names the architect as George Baker of Summerside and records that the church opened on January 10, 1889. Presbyterians at Cardigan had shared a minister first with Georgetown and then with Dundas. By the time the new church opened, Cardigan had become a separate charge. Smith’s photograph shows the pointed spire over the entrance beside the gable. The register records that the spire was taken down in the 1940s after it deteriorated. Someone looking for the church today with this postcard in hand has a good historical reason to find a different outline against the sky. The provincial heritage record for St. Andrew’s

What the printer saw, and what the senders saw

A good way to see what happened between the photographic print and the printed souvenir is to follow the bridge across the two “Shipping” cards. At its far end the little house, wharf and masts line up. At the near end, the commercial card includes a larger piece of wall and roof on the left and a crossarm with its insulators on the right. Ayer’s lettering sits in the margin below the image. On the photographic card the handwriting shares the dark foreground with the ships. It’s possible to compare the two pictures this closely without knowing who delivered the original to the printer or what arrangement Smith had with the firm.

Now turn over the published cards. Both bear an elaborate “Canadian Souvenir Post Card” heading and divide the back between correspondence and address. On the shipping view, Marjorie Shaw of Cardigan wrote her compliments and addressed the card to a captain in Liverpool, Nova Scotia. She didn’t name one of the vessels on the front or say why she had chosen it for him. There is a nice fitness to a shipping picture for a captain. It would take another document to put him aboard any particular schooner. Marjorie Shaw’s inscription

The buyer of the calm view made much fuller use of that left-hand space. “I thought you were going to call me up,” the message begins, and later the writer expects to be there the next afternoon, Saturday. The address takes it to Woodville on the Island. A Cardigan Bridge cancellation gives May 15, with a year too uncertain to print. Those few lines make a difference to the way I think of a postcard printer in Moncton reproducing an Island minister’s photograph. A copy could go out of province to a ship’s captain. Another could make the much shorter journey to arrange a visit with someone who was close enough to call. The Woodville message

Cardigan’s waterfront served people whose working lives crossed from one trade into another. The Cardigan Area Heritage Centre records that John A. Macdonald, who arrived in the village in 1893, kept a store there in the early twentieth century and had interests in shipping and shipbuilding. It also remembers Captain Jack Macdonald, who went to sea at sixteen and held master’s papers for both sail and steam. I have no name for the master of any of the ships in Smith’s picture. These local accounts do tell us what kinds of work lay behind the crowded masts outside that window. Cardigan Area Heritage Centre’s community histories

The minister came back

Smith appears in an unexpected place in the history of L. M. Montgomery. Her biographer Mary Henley Rubio identifies him as a friend and a former classmate of her husband, the Reverend Ewen Macdonald. Smith had served during the war and continued in the ministry. In the Montgomery biography he comes into view through an acquaintance in Ontario. In a letter written by a Cardigan woman, he is still the minister people remember having in their own pulpit. Mary Henley Rubio’s biography of Montgomery

Emily McLaren began her letter to Will Meiklejohn at seven on a Sunday morning. She wished he were there for the Charlottetown Exhibition, told him about going into town by automobile, and thanked him for a birthday card, a book and a photograph. By the time she took up her letter again it was two in the afternoon and she had been to church. Their old minister had returned for a visit of two weeks, she wrote, and his friends were proud to hear him preach again. She supplied his new title herself, “Lieut. Edwin Smith.” McLaren also said she hadn’t cared for the sermon that day. Her letter gives Smith the welcome. It leaves the preacher of that particular sermon unnamed. Emily McLaren’s letter from Cardigan

I like that the link to a named minister still depends on reading the little words beneath a photograph, just as the story began. Lay the unmailed prints down on their own and you have a name and a view from someone’s study. Add the two Ayer views and there is Cardigan Bridge. Add the church card and the printer has supplied the title before the name. Then Marjorie, the writer to Woodville and Emily McLaren remind us that people here had ships to look at, calls to make and a former minister they were glad to see again. To a postcard buyer the river was “A Calm Day, Cardigan Bridge.” To Edwin Smith, it was the view from his study window.
