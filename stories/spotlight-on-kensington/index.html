The history behind the picture

Spotlight on Kensington

Two men stand outside Kensington station beneath a roof that seems large enough to shelter half the platform. Behind them the rounded stones catch the light, the window openings are neatly framed, and a small Canadian Express sign hangs on the wall. This is a real-photo postcard with an ordinary moment at its centre. It is also a portrait of a building that photographers clearly loved.

Kensington’s album is smaller than Summerside’s or Montague’s, and the station makes repeated appearances. It deserved them. Its shape was instantly recognisable: a steep gable, a sweeping roof, rough stone walls and the town’s name set where an arriving passenger could see it. Different viewpoints turn the same building from a picturesque landmark into a place where people waited, met and conducted business.

The town at the crossing

Before the railway, Kensington’s position at converging roads had already given it the name Five Lanes’ End. The railway strengthened that advantage. The town’s own historical account describes businesses moving from nearby Margate as Kensington became a station and shipping centre. Roads brought the district into town; trains connected that district to a wider market.

The stone station was built in 1904–05, designed by Charles Chappell and constructed by M. F. Schurman. Parks Canada describes the passenger building and its sheltered platforms, with freight handled separately to the north. Those broad eaves were useful as well as handsome. Parks Canada’s history of the station gives the architecture its practical setting.

The colour picture rewards a second glance at the left edge. Behind the new stone building is a glimpse of the older wooden station’s steep roof and dormer. It is a small detail with a large effect: the postcard catches change happening in one place. The railway postcard tour follows that pairing and the station’s companions around the Island.

Beyond the platform

The “Main Business Street” card follows a long line of gabled buildings into the distance. Trees occupy one side, stores the other, and the broad road provides room for the traffic the shops depended on. The photographer has chosen depth over a single imposing facade. Each successive gable adds another business to the impression of a growing commercial centre.

The schoolhouse was sold in the same local publishing group. Its prominent central gable and arched entrance make a pleasingly formal front, but the people at the doorway keep it approachable. These two cards give a useful measure of what a town wanted a correspondent to see: its place of business and its place of learning. A handsome station was only the beginning of the introduction.

At the bank and the drugstore

Two undated RPPCs bring individual businesses close enough to inspect. The Bank of Nova Scotia occupies a compact brick building with its name running across the front. The lettering is a useful clue: Kensington’s local history records the Bank of New Brunswick’s opening in 1906 and its amalgamation with the Bank of Nova Scotia in 1913. The name on the building therefore belongs to the later chapter. Kensington’s business history follows that progression.

The Nyal Drug Store has a very different presence. Its projecting sign announces it to people approaching the corner, while the windows make room for displays. Wheel marks curve through the roadway beside it. These photographs preserve the scale of everyday errands: a few steps up to a shop, a turn at the corner, a doorway familiar enough to pass without thinking. The plain photographic format makes the small details especially rewarding.

Just after the snowplough

Victoria Street supplies a final change of season. The card tells us exactly what interested its maker: “Just after the Snow Plow.” Ridges of snow run along the road between houses and bare trees. The view is beautifully orderly, with the cleared route leading straight through the middle of the photograph. Someone thought this freshly passable street worth turning into a postcard.

That is an apt last image for Kensington. The postcards keep returning to connections: roads meeting, railway tracks beside the station, customers reaching shops, children entering school. The station gives the town its most distinctive silhouette. The other photographs show the community gathered around it, keeping its doors open and its routes in use.
