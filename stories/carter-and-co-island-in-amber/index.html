One sky for a whole Island

Start with the sails in Charlottetown Harbour. Someone has left them white against the green water and laid a broad band of apricot just where sea and sky meet. Now look at O’Leary. A strip of road runs past a row of red roofs, and above them is much the same sky. At Georgetown the houses are across the water; at Kildare a single dark figure stands under a jutting cliff. Spread these cards on a table and it looks as if the whole Island had agreed to sit for its portrait on the same evening.

It’s the colour that gives that impression. Nothing on the cards tells us the photographs were taken at sunset, let alone on the same day. There’s pleasure in noticing where the colour goes: the yellow canoe below the Hatchery Bridge, the little box under the fox at Rosebank, the salmon-coloured sacks on the Montague waterfront. In the last of those pictures you can follow the sacks back from the carts to the masts at the landing. For what a load of Island potatoes on its way to sea actually amounted to, there’s another Montague postcard: the crew of the Anna. The loading view doesn’t name a ship.

What Carter put on the back

Turn over the Montague card. Along the short edge, in letters small enough to overlook, Carter & Co. Ltd. describe themselves as booksellers and stationers in Charlottetown. On the next line is “Made in U.S.A.” There are two places in that little imprint: the firm offering the Island views was in Charlottetown; the postcards themselves were made across the border. It doesn’t tell us who operated the press or whose camera supplied the photographs.

The red lettering alone can lead you astray. Harry Holman’s illustrated account of Carter’s earlier Red Letter cards shows block capitals and a reverse headed Souvenir Mailing Card. Lay one beside these and the difference is plain. Here the caption sits in the pale top margin, begins with capitals and continues in small letters. On the back, the big outlined words are simply Post Card. “An Island in Amber” is a name for the pictures gathered here; no such series title appears on them.

Hunter River is the interesting exception. The red type sits exactly where you expect it and there’s that deep orange horizon again. The reverse even has the outlined heading and the dotted rule beneath it. But the printing on that side is reddish brown and there’s no Carter imprint down the edge. Its Kensington postmark is dated August 14, 1929. It earns a place in the gallery on the evidence of the design, and a question mark on the publisher.

What the senders saw

On the Bonshaw card, the sender tells Bud about staying at a little hotel in Charlottetown, then turns to the scene on the front: “This is one of the fishing places for trout.” The Charlottetown postmark gives us August 27, 1925. Beside it is a little advertisement impressed by the post office for the Exhibition postal booth, September 22 to 25. The sender, the postcard publisher and the post office each found a different use for this one small piece of paper.

At Kildare the printer gave us the colour of the sand. Margaret, who sent the other copy of Kildare Capes in 1928, had been out digging clams. What she wanted Hazel to know was that the sand was “white as milk.” And on the other Georgetown waterfront, Mildred’s news is simply: “Go in swimming every day.” The figures on the beach in the Cape Turner picture are too small to tell us much about themselves. The people who turned these cards over had a little more to say.

Rosebank is where the captions finally give out on us. Four different pictures carry the same words. One shows the buildings and pens; in another, a figure in a long coat stands among the foxes. A third puts one fox on top of a box. And on the fourth, two foxes share a shelf scarcely wide enough to accommodate them both. You could own three copies of the person with the foxes, as this collection does, and still never have seen those two on their perch.

Is there a twenty-fourth?

My hunch is that Carter issued twenty-four views in these colours. Counting Hunter River, I have twenty-three. If my guess is right, there’s one more out there. If you have a view I’ve missed, I’d love to see both sides. Get in touch.

Which copies are in the collection?

The 27 cards that started the search contain 21 different pictures. Card 4558 adds the fourth Rosebank picture. Hunter River adds one probable view: 29 physical cards altogether, illustrated here by 23 fronts.

Five pictures have duplicate copies. The first number on each line is the front chosen for the gallery; the other numbers open the remaining copies, including their messages.
