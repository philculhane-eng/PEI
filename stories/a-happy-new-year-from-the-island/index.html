A Happy New Year from the Island

At Belmont, near the end of December 1909, a planned family visit had run into Island weather. First came a Friday storm so fierce that the writer said they could scarcely show their noses outside. They got ready again on Monday, and a worse storm arrived. The roads were blocked. The visit would have to wait.

So a postcard went instead, carrying the whole exasperating story to a cousin on Brackley Point Road, followed by a wish for a very happy New Year. On the front, a marvellous little scene of a clock, a ladder, a horn, and sacks of money promised a rather more accommodating turn of the calendar.

New Year cards have a character all their own. They make room for luck and prosperity, for plans to see one another, and for a fresh start after the weather has had its say.

When the visit has to wait

The Belmont writer manages to sound disappointed and amused at the same time. After explaining the second storm, the message asks, “pretty good don’t you think?” Anyone who has arranged a winter visit on the Island may recognise the tone.

The card was addressed to Miss Ethel B. Stuart. A couple of years later, another New Year greeting in the collection went to Miss Ethel Stuart on Brackley Point Road: the green-dressed figure shown above, posted at Stanhope in December 1911. The matching name and road suggest that two of these cards preserve part of the same person’s holiday mail.

A photograph of snow-bound O’Leary gives us a view to set beside the Belmont letter. Its caption dates the snow scene to 1905. The sender used it later, thanking the recipient for postcards and a lovely present, and adding New Year wishes. The image has people making their way between banks of snow almost as conspicuous as the buildings. The message has gratitude and friendship. Together, they make a very Island combination.

A little prosperity, and love from all

The January 1 card travelled farther. David wrote from Sturgeon Mills to Lizzie and Bob in Redcliff, Alberta. Everyone was well, he hoped the same for them, and he wished them a prosperous New Year, signing off with love from all. The Montague postmark is dated 20 December 1913: the New Year greeting was already on its way before Christmas.

“Prosperous” appears on other cards too. Mary sends a happy and prosperous New Year on a view of Summer Street, Summerside. There is a generous simplicity to the wish. The sender hopes that life will go well for the people at the other end of the address.

Sometimes the year turns amid everyday errands. A Hunter River card combines thanks for a present, a postponed visit, a hope that a dress has worked out well, and a Happy New Year. It is easy to enjoy the orderly town view on the front; turn it over, and someone’s busy week comes into focus.

The weather, as always

Winter had several moods. On 22 December 1906, a visitor writing on a Charlottetown harbour card reported arriving tired but delighted. The weather was very mild, and it was raining. Christmas would be spent in the country; a New Year wish followed. That small weather report sits happily beside the snow-bound journeys elsewhere in the collection. Every winter had its own days.

Another card needs just three words. On the back of an Acorn photographic view of the lighthouse at Souris, Julie writes “Happy New Year.” The card was posted at Souris West on 31 December 1919. Its quiet water and luminous sky make a lovely way to leave the old year.

Across these cards, the hopes are wonderfully familiar: good health, better weather for travelling, a visit soon, a little prosperity, and people remembering one another. There is still plenty of use in wishes like those.

About these cards

This story draws on the cards’ messages, pictures, and postal markings. The snowy cottage and other decorative New Year scenes are greeting-card illustrations; the Island connection comes from their senders, recipients, and postmarks. The O’Leary photograph and the Belmont message record different winters.
