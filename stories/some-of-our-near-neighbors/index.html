The history behind the picture

“Some of Our Near Neighbors” — Mi’kmaw Lives on Island Postcards

For Christmas in 1907, someone on Prince Edward Island sent a postcard to William Crane in Boston. Three people are outside a shelter near Richmond. One stands with arms folded, another sits low beside the doorway, and a wooden chair stands empty at the right. The caption calls it a “scene.” On the back the sender calls it “a snap of some of our near neighbors.” Look at the lines under near and neighbors. Of all the words in that Christmas greeting, those were the two the writer chose to underline.

You can find this photograph twice more in the collection. The chair and its curved arms give it away at once. The Red Cross Drug Store in Summerside sold those copies under the title “One of the Natives, Richmond, near Summerside, P.E.I.” There are three people in the picture, and the title offers us one. I’d have missed the best description of them altogether if I’d stopped scanning at the front. I keep the original captions so that readers can see what Islanders bought and sent. For the people themselves, I use their names wherever a record gives one.

Sally Mitchell, in the picture all along

One Summerside souvenir has a brightly coloured Island crest on the left and a portrait on the right. The woman wears a patterned shawl and a tall peaked cap. There is room beneath the crest for a Latin motto and “From the Garden of the Gulf.” Under her own likeness the printer gives her a family role and a place, “A Grandmother Micmac, P. E. Island.” There are two copies in the collection. Both backs have space for an address. Both are blank.

It turned up in another copy of the photograph. The Nova Scotia Museum’s Mi’kmaq Portraits Collection indexes the sitter as Sally Mitchell. The record places the portrait at Rocky Point about 1895 and gives the P.E.I. Public Archives reference 2320-9/6. You can compare the patterned edge of her cap, the folds of the shawl and the position of her hand at the bottom of the frame. “Grandmother” may well have been a word somebody dear to Sally used for her. It means something different coming from a printer who left out her name. Sally Mitchell’s portrait record.

The matching card of Frank Snake has a piece of handwriting beneath the frame. Someone wrote his name below the printed words “One of the Micmacs.” The museum also indexes the same photograph under Frank Snake and places it on Lennox Island about 1895. Summerside in the publishing history and Lennox Island in the portrait record tell us two different things about the journey of this picture. Frank Snake’s portrait record · The story of the Summerside editions.

Another portrait asks a collector to live with a gap. On 3783 a woman in a peaked cap stands beside a doorway at Rocky Point. Look down to her left and there is a small child close against her skirt, one bare foot visible. The wording above them uses a slur for the woman and an old postcard spelling of “papoose” for the child. Those words told a buyer how to categorize two people without telling the buyer who either of them was. Their names, and what they called one another, are still open questions. The woman and child at Rocky Point.

What the hands are doing

On postcard 3900, look just outside the doorway at Rocky Point. A row of baskets sits at people’s feet. Some are deep, with arched handles. One is much wider than it is tall. In the distance, through the trees, you can see the water. Up close you can see how the basket makers varied the shapes and the height of the handles. There is even one tipped up on its side, so that its mouth faces the camera.

Card 0108 lets us see a basket considerably earlier in its life. The woman second from the left holds splints that fan out in front of her. The museum has the same photograph from an A. W. Mitchell negative, dated about 1894. Its cataloguer points out four strips woven across four others. They make the base. Bend the ends upwards and they become the uprights for the sides. There are finished baskets within arm’s reach, but it’s those few crossed strips that let us see a decision being made. The museum puts a question mark beside Lennox Island as the location. The card itself says only P.E. Island. The museum’s description of the basket in progress.

Mi’kmaw writer Tiffany Sark tells this history through the people who did the work. In her essay Mi’kmaq Baskets: Our Living Legends she draws on Matilda Lewis’s recollections. As a girl, Matilda shaved the strips and fastened handles. Her family sold baskets in Tyne Valley and Summerside at Easter and Christmas. Egg baskets, potato baskets and clothes hampers each answered a different need. Someone digging Island potatoes depended on somebody else having chosen the wood, prepared it and made a handle that would hold the weight. Matilda’s account gives us the child’s part in that work in her own family. It’s the sort of thing I’d never learn by counting the children in a photograph. Tiffany Sark’s essay in First Hand.

Two other cards show how easily that work could disappear in print. A patterned quilt hangs at the right of both photographs. In one edition the publisher calls the people basket makers and gives the place as Rocky Point. In the other the caption says simply that this is a camp in Charlottetown. The quilt, the round basket and the five people stay in the same positions. A few words along the lower edge are enough to move them across the harbour. Rocky Point is part of Abegweit First Nation today, one of the Mi’kmaw communities of Epekwitk, the Island we also call Prince Edward Island. The view from the writing side

The back of 0107 is a harder read. The writer thanks a correspondent in California for sending cards and asks for more. Then comes a curious pair of observations. The writer says that few Island Mi’kmaq now live in wigwams and calls the picture “typical” in the very next sentence. After drawing attention to a woman smoking, the writer invites the recipient to laugh at the group. A person could receive that card thousands of miles away and come to feel they knew something about a woman whose name they had never heard. The sender who wrote to Boston underlined near neighbors. The two messages give us some idea of the range of attitudes already present when these cards were new. Read the California-bound card.

Sometimes the picture gives no hint of the people who will turn up in the message. On a view of Keppoch Shore mailed in July 1912, a visitor tells Martha about cherries in the yard, a trip the day before to see the camps and plans to go to the circus. We have the visitor’s itinerary. What that afternoon was like on the other side of the visit is missing from the letter. In 1926 Edith used a view of Trout River to tell a friend in Massachusetts that she planned to go down the bay to Lennox Island the next day. Today the Nation’s own Cultural Centre welcomes visitors to see work by local artists and to hear the stories the community chooses to share. Keppoch Shore, 3719 · Trout River, 2685 · Lennox Island First Nation on welcoming visitors.

A lumbering card addressed to Cheshire, England, carries a rather different sort of news. Writing from Charlottetown on January 13, the sender reports too little snow for tobogganing or snowshoeing and passes along a prediction of plenty next month. The forecast is attributed to Indigenous people on the Island, collectively, in three words. We can see that someone thought their opinion worth passing across the Atlantic. We have neither the speakers’ names nor their actual words. The winter letter on 2785.

At Miminegash it is the waterfront caption that deserves a second look. Under a row of fishing boats the printer gives Mi’kmaw life the word “formerly” and the fishing village the word “now.” Yet the very name under the boats carries the language forward. L’nuey, the Island’s Mi’kmaw rights organization, traces Miminegash to Elminikej, “let us carry something animate on our shoulders.” I like having that translation beside the postcard. The next time I read a Miminegash postmark, those are words I can hear in it. Miminegash Run, 0848 · L’nuey on the name Elminikej.

“From your Friend”

At Pictou in July 1923, John Muir photographed a group that included Chief John Sark from Prince Edward Island and Chief Matthew Francis from Pictou Landing. The postcard printer gathered the women and children into four words, “their wives and children.” The Nova Scotia Museum’s record gives several of them their names, including Agnes Francis, Louisa Julian, Elizabeth Paul and Matthew’s daughter Martha. There is another card here, 4653, in which Martha sits beside her family and former Chief Peter Wilmot. You can follow a girl from one photograph to the next once somebody has given you her name. The museum’s group portrait · The smaller Pictou portrait.

On this particular copy Matthew did something no museum identification could do for us. He turned the picture over and wrote a Christmas greeting to Chief John Julian in Truro. A label covers much of the message. Along the bottom, in the space it missed, you can still read “From your Friend Chief M Francis.” The name printed “Frances” on the picture side survives as Francis in his own hand. The man at the left of the photograph got to choose who would see it next, and what he wanted to say. The story of Matthew’s Christmas card.

There is a present-day Richmond connection I like to put beside that pencilled word neighbors. Nora Richard, a Mi’kmaw basket weaver and member of Lennox Island First Nation, grew up in Richmond. As well as making baskets, she repairs older ones worn from use. She is teaching her son to weave. Looking again at the row of handles outside the doorway at Rocky Point, I wish I could turn one of those baskets over as readily as I can turn over a postcard, and find its maker’s name. Nora Richard and her baskets.
