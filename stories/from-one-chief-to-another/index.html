The history behind the picture

PEI’s Greatest Postcards – #5 – A Christmas Card from One Chief to Another

Chief Matthew Francis of Pictou Landing signed this Christmas greeting and addressed it to another chief in Truro. That alone would have earned it a place in my collection. Turn it over. There is Matthew himself, standing at the far left of a group that includes Chief John Sark of Prince Edward Island. Someone has put a remarkably large label over the message. I can read enough around its edges to know what Matthew was doing with a photograph that a stranger might have bought as a souvenir.

The label shows a ship and advertises Pictou County’s Old Home Week, July 15 to 21, 1923. The event marked 150 years since the arrival of Scottish settlers aboard the Hector. The Nova Scotia Museum places the group portrait in Pictou that week and credits John Muir, then working for W. H. Munro Studios. The photographer, the studio and the celebration are three details the face of this card wouldn’t have given me. The date on the label belongs to the summer gathering. The Christmas message had its own journey. Nova Scotia Museum’s portrait record

Ten people and four printed descriptions

The publisher put the words “Group of 4 Chiefs” in a panel below the photograph. The rest of the party gets “their wives and children.” Even one of the four men is identified only as “Medicine Man.” The museum’s copy lets us restore his name, Jeremiah Bartlett Alexis, known as Jerry Lonecloud. The same record identifies Mrs. John Sark, Agnes Francis, Louisa Julian and Elizabeth Paul among the standing figures. The boy in front has acquired several identifications over the years. The museum preserves those disagreements in its entry.

The girl is easier to follow. The museum names her Martha Francis. Now look at another card in the collection, 4653. There is Martha again, this time in a smaller group with her father, a woman the printed caption calls his wife, and former chief Peter Wilmot. On this copy the printer spells the family name “Frances.” On our Christmas card Matthew supplied the spelling himself.

The McCulloch Heritage Centre has a copy of this second picture. Its description names the woman Mary Jane Francis. The museum’s record for the large group names Agnes as Matthew’s wife. Both are worth recording against the photograph where the name occurs. A shared surname and two printed captions can carry a family historian a long way. They can also carry one past the point where the records agree. For Martha and her father, the two identifications do agree. The McCulloch Centre’s smaller group portrait

Chief John Sark in two other photographs

The Nova Scotia Museum has a second portrait from the Hector celebration, made of John Sark himself. Its old caption calls attention to four medals. The record even preserves a reference to the Standard of Montreal. Someone looking through the Pictou group for the first time might go straight to the imposing standing figures at the back. Another person looking at the same gathering thought the seated Island chief, and what he was wearing, deserved a picture of his own. I would want much better evidence before assigning an occasion or a giver to any one of his medals. The museum’s separate portrait of John Sark

An Island photograph of about 1895 puts him in quite a different setting. The PEI Public Archives holds the print as 2602-14. A row of children stands outside a white wooden building on Lennox Island, and the museum identifies Sark by name in his beaded coat. The cataloguer thought the building might be a school. The children make it worth bringing that older view to mind when we look at Martha and the boy in the Pictou photograph. One image places Sark at home among Islanders. The other places him at a public gathering with families from several Mi’kmaw communities.

There is a last photographic wrinkle. The glass negative the Nova Scotia Museum describes for the big group was itself a copy, made by Gauvin & Gentzel in Halifax on December 18, 1928. The museum records that glass as broken. The sitting was in Pictou in 1923. A collector could easily mistake one date for the other if all that survived were the label on a negative sleeve. Matthew’s postcard preserves something even a perfect museum copy of the front could never give us, the use he personally made of the picture.

The address to Truro

At the left edge I can make out the beginning of the salutation. Most of the name there lies under the label. The name John is clear in the address. Farther down are Christmas and New Year wishes and the words “From your Friend Chief M Francis.” The address supplies a title, a surname, Truro and Colchester County. Back on the front, the tall standing man second from the right is captioned “Joe Julia.” The museum identifies him as Joe Julian of Truro. It is tempting to give him the card in his hand, so to speak. Place, title and surname point in that direction. The address gives us a reason to hesitate over the first name. I can identify the sender in the photograph. The recipient’s identity within the group remains an open question.

Martha’s later life takes us across the space between the two families by another route. The museum identifies her as Mrs. Sandy Julian. In the family notice for Alexander “Sandy” Julien, his former wife is named as Martha Francis, and his parents as Chief Joseph Julien and Annie Louise GooGoo. Notice the different spellings even in records we can place side by side. A printer’s “Julia,” the museum’s “Julian” and a family’s “Julien” would make three separate trails in an alphabetical index. The notice gives us a marriage to follow without requiring us to decide that John and Joe were the same man. The Julien family’s notice for Sandy

The Island connection that first brought this card to my attention is John Sark. The reason I put it at number five is Matthew’s signature. The photographer arranged a group during a public celebration. Matthew picked up one of those pictures, turned it over, and wrote to a friend. The ink on the back lets us read the portrait as one of the people in it chose to use it.
