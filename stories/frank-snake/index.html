The history behind the picture

PEI’s Greatest Postcards – #4 – Frank Snake in Two Summerside Postcards

Put the two portraits side by side. Follow the pipe stem, the fingers around the long gun and the little folds at the top of the hat. Whoever printed these postcards chose the same photograph. One printer gave the man a whole vertical card. The other fitted him inside a little gold-coloured frame and left half the space for an Island emblem. Between the two publishers they gave him two titles. It took someone with a pencil to give him a name.

On 4183 the words just above his head read “The First P. E. Islander, Summerside.” With that title at the top and the portrait underneath, a purchaser could take it for a statement about the man. Turn it over and there is rather better evidence about the people selling his likeness. A line up the left margin identifies the Red Cross Drug Store of Summerside and gives the card a number, A 371. The store’s name and the series number were worth space in the smallest print on the card. His name was left out.

Harry Holman’s account of Summerside’s drugstore postcards identifies the McLellans with the Red Cross business and reproduces this issue. There was a local trade in views like these. A dealer could have cards made bearing the shop’s name, a customer could buy one to mail, and the person to whom it travelled could be a continent away. This particular copy went from Richmond, PEI, to Los Angeles. The writer dated it March 12, 1908, and used most of the few lines available to send news that everyone was well and the weather was fine. Harry Holman on Summerside’s drugstore postcards

Where the name comes from

On 0053 the publisher put a crown and a shield beside him, above the motto Parva sub ingenti. Below the shield one can read “From the Garden of the Gulf.” Below the portrait, in considerably smaller type, is “One of the Micmacs, P. E. Island.” Then under the frame, just before the bottom edge cuts off the writing, there it is in pencil, Frank Snake. The catalogue credits this version to Summerside Journal Job Print. The Pittsfield, Massachusetts, receiving mark is dated November 1905. That gives the portrait a firm latest date, earlier than the journey of the drugstore copy to California. It gives us no date for the pencil.

There are different kinds of identification in this collecting business. Alice Maude Kinch signed her own portrait. Chief Matthew Francis wrote a greeting on a photograph in which he appears. In Frank’s case a hand distinct from the printed caption gives us his name. I don’t know who held the pencil or what that person’s connection to him was. The handwriting is worth preserving precisely because the printed versions are so little help. Alice’s signed portrait · Matthew Francis’s Christmas card

The same portrait also survives in the P.E.I. Public Archives. The Nova Scotia Museum’s Mi’kmaq Portraits Collection indexes the sitter as Frank Snake, places the photograph on Lennox Island and dates it to about 1895. The folds in the hat, the pipe and the grip on the gun all match 0053. Another portrait in this Summerside series now has a name as well, Sally Mitchell. The other cards on the drug counter

Another copy of the larger portrait turns up as 0050. It carries the same publisher and A 371 number. This time someone sent the picture to Massachusetts with six words about the visit, “It is an odd place here.” If we had only this copy and 4183, we would know what two senders thought worth telling people away and still have no personal name for the man whose face they mailed. The second Red Cross copy

Look a few numbers back in the publisher’s series and the company he kept on the rack becomes clearer. A 368 shows men on a Summerside sidewalk and a horse and carriage in Central Street. The sender of that card, Mattie, wrote to Kansas about the rain. A 370 shows a family group beside a shelter near Richmond. Its printed title once again singles out a person without giving a name. The catalogue holds that image both in monochrome and with added colour. The man on A 370 has his own history to recover. A neighbouring stock number tells us which business sold the two pictures. A name in the Island census

There is one Island record I’d put beside the pencil inscription. The Tku Atlasma’lit Digital Archive holds a page of the 1881 census of Prince County. Across the top the enumerator named Township 18, Princetown and Royalty and Fish Island. On the very first line I read “Snake Francis,” surname first. Follow the line across the page. His age was recorded as fifty, his birthplace as Prince Edward Island and his occupation as fisherman. Four more people in the household appear beneath him. Farther down the same sheet the enumerator entered a family named Sark. The handwritten census page, 1881

Could Francis the fisherman be the Frank somebody named in pencil? The card was already in the mail in 1905. Its postmark gives us no age for the sitter, and the census gives us no likeness. I’d want a family identification, a photographer’s record or a second named portrait before joining those two people. Meanwhile that return does something the shop’s caption never did. It records Island people named Snake going about their lives twenty-four years before the postcard addressed to Pittsfield arrived in the mail.
