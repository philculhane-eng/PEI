The history behind the picture

Yours truly, Alice Maude Kinch

Sooooo many real photo postcards of people had no attribution - THEY knew who they were, and the recipient knew who they were...but poor old posterity...Sigh!

So, thank you, Alice Maude Kinch, for signing your card.

“Yours truly, Alice Maude Kinch.”

Those few words, written in ink across the bottom of this portrait, sent me looking for an Island story. Alice left us her full name, and more than a century later it gives us somewhere to begin.

Look at where she put the signature. The photograph fades out below the lace on her blouse, leaving a pale space the width of the card. “Yours truly” takes one line. Her name takes nearly all of the next. Somebody who bought the portrait a century later could lose the envelope, the address and every scrap of family correspondence and still have those three names. That is more of an introduction than many a portrait in this collection gives us.

Alice, Maude and an Island family

Our best match is the Alice Maude Kinch whom a Kinch family history places at Cascumpec, in western Prince Edward Island. It gives her birth as March 22, 1874, and names her parents as John Kinch and Rachel McElduff. Among the children it lists are older siblings Albert, John and Louisa, and two younger brothers, Percy and Michael. A little landscape in the collection helps place the family. The caption on card 4123 reads “Cascumpec Light, Alberton Harbour, P.E.I.” Two names that turn up separately in accounts of Alice’s family sit together on a single line under the lighthouse. The Kinch family history

One of the more persuasive details comes from the other side of the marriage. Tom O’Connor, writing about the Farrell family, remembered the Island-born dentist who practised in Malden, Massachusetts. His wife, O’Connor says, was Maude Kinch, daughter of John Kinch of Alberton. Follow her family and you find Philip Farrell. Follow his and you find Maude and her father back on the Island. The name on my card fits both accounts. The Kinch genealogy names O’Connor among those researching the family, so the two accounts may draw on some of the same information. What I’d still like to see is a second family photograph identified in another hand. Tom O’Connor’s notes on the Farrells

Boston was already part of the family geography. The Kinch history places the marriage of Alice’s older brother John David there in 1901, to Sarah J. Proctor of Port Hawkesbury. It records his death ten years later in Maine. Another brother, Albert, married at Tignish and remained in the Alberton area. That gives a little substance to the question I asked when I first saw the portrait. Sitting for it on the Island and sitting for it in Massachusetts were both possibilities in a family with ties on either side of the border.

A family record which cites the Massachusetts marriage collection places Alice’s marriage to Philip Leo Farrell in Boston in 1909. That is an attractive clue to the date of a portrait signed Kinch. My working estimate has been 1904–09, combining the proposed date range of the photographic stock with the marriage. The upper date depends on an assumption about when Alice would have used that signature. A woman could use her birth name after marriage, an older photograph could be printed on newer stock, and a finished postcard could wait to be signed. The portrait as it stands gives us a plausible period and an undated signature. The photographer and the place of the sitting are still open. The Farrell marriage entry in the family profile

Three mail boats on the ice

There were two Philip Farrells. The dentist in Massachusetts was the son. His father worked as a messenger at the House of Commons. The elder Philip also wrote a poem about a winter crossing of the Northumberland Strait, and in this instance the poet was one of the people on the ice. He dates the departure himself, January 27, 1885. He counts three boats, fifteen crew and seven passengers. They were making for Cape Tormentine.

In Farrell’s account the party kept looking ahead for the Cape and picking out shapes in the drifted snow that might have been a building. By the time they admitted they were lost, they had another use for the boats. They arranged them on the ice as shelter for the night. The water kegs froze. When land finally came into view, it was on the Crapaud side, back on the Island they had set out to leave. Some of the party made it through the snow to shelter and neighbours went out for the others. The family account and the poem survive together. Philip Farrell’s “The Ice Boat Crew”

There’s an Island postcard I’d put beside that passage. On 2301 the men and boats make a long dark line against the surface of the Strait. Look at the figures leaning in at the sides. The card illustrates the Cape service and was mailed in 1915, thirty years after Farrell’s ordeal. Its photograph gives the reader a look at the means of travel he described. For that particular night in 1885, the witness we have is Philip. The Cape iceboat service, 2301

O’Connor traced the elder Philip through two Island newspapers once more in 1906, this time reporting his marriage to Mary Dooner in Ottawa. The account he gives of the occasion puts Philip in charge of the House of Commons press gallery. Sixty-two colleagues and friends signed an address in his honour. The man who had nearly lost his life trying to get across the Strait had spent years helping newspaper men do their work in Ottawa. After the wedding the couple’s announced itinerary took them through Boston on their way to Tignish.

There is a last turn in the family account. O’Connor describes the elder Philip later living in an apartment in the Malden building where his son had his dental practice. He names a third Philip, Alice’s son, who also became a dentist. Three generations and the same name are enough to muddle a particularly diligent postcard collector. Here the distinction gives Alice’s portrait a household one can begin to picture. Her husband was the Malden dentist, her father-in-law wrote the iceboat poem, and her son followed his father into dentistry.

What I like most is still that she signed both Alice and Maude. In the Farrell account she is Maude, the dentist’s wife. In the Kinch account she has a place among John and Rachel’s children. Here she has a high lace collar and a dark ribbon at her throat, a face to go with the records, and an introduction in her own handwriting. Thank you, Alice Maude Kinch, for signing your card.
