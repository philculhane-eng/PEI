Christmas, with Love from the Island

Amy had the right idea. Writing on Christmas Eve, she sent a picture of Alberton High School with a perfectly sensible explanation:

“I am sending you this card as I have no Xmas one but this will do just as well.”

It certainly would. A school, a harbour, a street where someone lived: add a few loving words, and an ordinary Island postcard could join the Christmas mail. Alongside those improvised greetings are cards dressed for the occasion, with holly, red berries, bells, and enough good wishes to carry us well into January.

Here is a little Christmas gathering from the collection. Click any picture to turn the card over and meet the people who sent it.

Four Island views, dressed in holly

This lovely Valentine & Sons family gives familiar Island subjects a Christmas border. There are two views of Queen Square, the New Zion Presbyterian Church, and a pair of black foxes. The photographs sit inside cream frames, surrounded by holly and bright red greetings. Even the foxes have been invited to Christmas.

On the fox card, Aunt Annie writes from Covehead on 13 December 1913 to Master Ryckman Alexander in Lachute, Quebec: “Just to remind you of the Christmas season. With much love from Aunt Annie.” The printed verse remembers old friendships; the handwriting makes the greeting belong to these two people.

The Zion church card travelled to a grandfather in Maitland, Nova Scotia, during the first Christmas of the First World War. Its printed verse wishes the recipient peace and plenty. On the back, Graham wishes Grandpa a happy Christmas and regrets being unable to come home and help eat Christmas dinner. That detail reaches across the years rather easily. The dinner was part of the invitation, and so was being together.

There are four different designs here and a fifth card with its own small joke. Card 4480 repeats the Queen Square “Christmas Joys” picture, but Bessie used it on 22 August 1919. Auntie had decided to stay until Tuesday morning; if the family wanted another visit, they could come down. Holly and Christmas wishes accompanied a thoroughly summery piece of family business.

Christmas with an Island address

Some greetings made their local connection part of the design. Father Burke’s card carries his name above a church and its neighbouring house. O’Leary appears beneath a smiling Father Christmas on a little ribbon-tied greeting booklet. A harbour view becomes a keepsake from J. A. Murphy, while another card sends Christmas wishes from Charlottetown “in the Land of the Maple.”

Each gives the recipient something of the sender’s place as well as the season. A familiar building, a town’s name, a view of the water: these were small things that could travel with a greeting and be kept.

Bells, berries, and news from home

The Island connection often waits on the back. A card covered in holly and bells went from Linkletter to Massachusetts on Christmas Eve 1907. Rosa tells Lu that everyone is well and that the family is coming for Christmas. She is looking anxiously for a letter from Mother. Then come love to all, Christmas wishes, and a promise to write soon.

Those little bundles of news are part of the pleasure. The printed card supplies the festive flourish; a few lines of handwriting tell us who is visiting, whose letter is overdue, and who is being thought of across the water.

“This will do just as well”

And then there are the cards that became Christmas cards through the words their senders added. A Victoria Row view, mailed in December 1901, brings Christmas and New Year wishes and news of good sleighing and skating. On a Summer Street card, a sender tells an uncle, “This is a picture of the street that we live on.” The Christmas greeting goes on the other side.

An ice-boat view carries an especially cheerful hope that Santa will be very good to a member of the household. Its boats and ice belong to the Island’s winter story; its message belongs to the much smaller world of family affection.

Amy finishes her school postcard by promising a letter and asking her friend to write soon. That is a fine Christmas wish in its own right: keep in touch. Under all the holly and gilding, across all those miles and years, the cards still carry it.

About these cards

The photographs, printed greetings, handwriting, and postal markings are the sources for this story. The holly-bordered group contains four different designs represented by five cards in the collection; 4480 repeats 2691. Dates refer to the messages or postal use.
