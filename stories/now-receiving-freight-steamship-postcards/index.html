The history behind the picture

“Now Receiving Freight”: Black Diamond and the Gulf Steamers

The Bonavista was due to sail on Thursday, October 5. Freight was being received daily at the Black Diamond Shed on Montreal’s Island Wharf. Anyone with goods to send was to contact Kingman, Brown & Co., at 14 Place Royale. The office even supplied its Bell telephone number: 75.

All of that fitted onto one prepaid postcard. The ship’s name and sailing date were stamped into a printed form; North Sydney had been struck out of the list of ports. Charlottetown and St. John’s remained. This was information to act on, with a sailing close at hand. Today, the same card lets us stand for a moment at the shore end of the Island’s supply line.

The penny card that filled a ship

A shipping agent could keep a supply of printed cards, enter the next vessel and date, and address them to prospective shippers. Canada’s government postal cards already carried their postage. The agent’s business printing went on the message side, while the other side took the address and cancellation. The history of those prepaid cards begins in 1871; the collection’s shipping notices are using them by 1877.

Turn over the Bonavista notice and the destination is Montreal: W. W. Ogilvie, 38 Foundling Street, “City.” Several of the other notices went to Lyman, Sons & Co., also addressed simply “City.” A Polino card went to the Canada Paper Company. These cards were recruiting cargo where the voyage began. The Island appears in their destination lists, waiting at the other end of the water.

Black diamonds meant coal

The Black Diamond Line took its name from the cargo that helped underpin its business. An 1888 account of Montreal’s commerce identifies Kingman, Brown & Co. as its managing agents and describes steamers bringing coal from the Lower Provinces to St. Lawrence ports, chiefly Montreal. Its house flag showed a black diamond on a red ground. The same account names the Bonavista, Coban and Cacouna, serving Charlottetown, Sydney and St. John’s. The Commerce of Montreal and Its Manufactures, 1888

The company had been formed in 1881, and the Bonavista joined its fleet in 1884. Coal, general cargo and passengers all belonged to the business. That combination helped keep a useful circuit going: goods and travellers down the river and through the Gulf, coal among the cargoes coming back. David Piercey, “The Montreal Steamers and the Newfoundland Mails, 1885–1897”

A later Black Diamond passenger brochure preserved by Memorial University advertises weekly sailings linking Montreal, Charlottetown, Sydney and St. John’s. It names Peake Brothers & Company as the Charlottetown agents. Berths, dining arrangements and rented deck chairs occupy its pages. The short freight circular and the passenger brochure were two ways of selling space aboard the same service. Black Diamond Steamship Line brochure

Before Black Diamond: the Hadji and the Miramichi

The earliest shipping notices here take us back to July and August 1877. J. A. Dupont, at 303 Commissioners Street in Montreal, was advertising the Hadji for Halifax, calling at Charlottetown, Pictou and Port Hawkesbury. The July card announced departure at daylight on Saturday the 7th; the August card gave midnight on Thursday the 16th.

Both promised through bills of lading to Arichat, Lunenburg, Shelburne, Liverpool and Yarmouth: cargo could be booked onward beyond the ports on the steamer’s own list. Both directed freight to Island Wharf. And both were addressed to W. H. Smith in the city. The two survivors show a shipping office returning to the same potential customer with a fresh sailing.

In May 1880 the Quebec and Gulf Ports Steamship Company sent a similar notice for the Miramichi. Summerside, Charlottetown and Pictou were printed on the form. Father Point, Metis, Gaspé and Percé were added by hand. David Shaw was the agent, and the announced departure was Saturday, May 22. A few handwritten additions could adapt a stock form to the journey being offered.

Henry Dobell’s notices: the Tiber, Polino and Thames

By the 1890s, Henry Dobell & Co.’s name appears repeatedly in the collection. His circulars called for freight at the sheds on Windmill Point. The ships include the Tiber and Polino, with the Thames sharing one particularly useful card. The Polino belonged to the Ross Line’s trade; Piercey’s postal-history study traces its Montreal agency through Robert Reford to Henry Dobell. These cards bring several shipping concerns into the story alongside Black Diamond. Piercey, “The Montreal Steamers and the Newfoundland Mails”

The card mailed on May 15, 1895, divides its message into two offers. The Tiber would receive cargo on the 23rd for Halifax and Charlottetown. The Thames would receive cargo on the 24th for St. John’s and North and South Sydney. One postcard allowed a merchant to choose between two loading dates and two groups of destinations.

The July notices show the summer trade in motion. A Tiber circular announced a Wednesday, July 10 sailing for Halifax, Charlottetown and St. John’s. A Polino circular offered Thursday, July 18 for St. John’s, North and South Sydney, Charlottetown and Summerside. In August, another Polino card advertised the 29th. The vessels and dates changed while the instruction to bring freight to the sheds remained.

These notices were working forms, and the pen could change them. On a November 1894 Polino card, St. John’s is crossed out in red and Halifax written above it. Charlottetown and Summerside were added with a rubber stamp; North and South Sydney were entered by hand. The ship’s name was stamped into its blank. Every correction helped turn a reusable form into the next sailing’s instructions.

“Last steamer”

The words stand on a line of their own on Card 2840: LAST STEAMER. The Polino was advertised for Monday, November 11, serving Summerside, Charlottetown, North and South Sydney, and St. John’s. Freight was being received now.

For a merchant planning winter stock, those two words mattered. The card offered a final opportunity on this service before the season closed. An ordinary request for space aboard a steamer had become a deadline for getting goods out of Montreal and into the Island’s shops and storerooms.

Not every notice needed a printer. In July 1899, Dobell’s office used pencil on a plain prepaid card to tell W. W. Ogilvie & Co. that the Tiber would sail on Friday morning, July 28, for North and South Sydney, Summerside, Charlottetown and Halifax. The familiar circuit could be set down in a handful of lines. At the Island end: catch Monday’s boat

One more card brings the cargo ashore in a particularly human way. In July 1900, D. L. McKinnon of Montague Bridge wrote to the furniture firm at Bass River, Nova Scotia. His goods had missed the previous Monday’s boat. He wanted them on the next one if possible—and the supplier could add two dozen of the cheapest chairs and two cheap cobble rockers.

Here is the customer’s side of the sailing notice: a shop waiting for furniture, a missed departure, another week to wait. The agent in Montreal, the freight shed, the vessel and the Island merchant were connected by arrangements like these. The steamers moved the goods. The little cards helped get them aboard.

More sailing notices in the collection

Two further examples complete this group of twelve sailing notices: the Tiber’s July 1894 circular for St. John’s, Harbour Grace and Halifax, and the Polino’s August 1895 circular for Newfoundland, Cape Breton and the Island.
