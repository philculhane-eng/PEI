The history behind the picture

PEI’s Greatest Postcards – #1 – The Tunnel

The first thing I’d like you to do with this postcard is the one thing I can’t quite arrange on a website. Pick it up, turn the picture towards you and hold it to a strong light. On the left is Cape Traverse, PEI. On the right is Cape Tormentine, New Brunswick. The little steamer is still on the water between them. Down below, where a moment ago there was just paper, a train runs under the Strait.

It took two sides of one piece of card to build that railway. The engine and cars are printed across the address side so that when light passes through the paper they line up with the tunnel portals on the picture side. You can try the next best thing here by opening both scans. Notice where the track on the Island shore turns to enter the dark opening. Someone went to considerable trouble to make an imaginary journey work at postcard size.

Why anyone wanted a tunnel

When Prince Edward Island joined Canada in 1873, the terms included a federal undertaking to maintain efficient steam service for mail and passengers between Island and mainland, summer and winter. One can read that promise in Ottawa and find it perfectly straightforward. An Islander trying to cross in February had to reckon with the ice. A steamer could be stopped by it. An iceboat crew might have to row on one part of the passage and put their own backs into hauling the boat over another.

Card 2301 gives those words some weight. A line of boats and people stretches almost across the photograph. There’s scarcely anything in it to tell us how big a boat is until we see a man beside one. The caption names the same two capes Bayer printed on his tunnel. This copy was in the mail in 1915. Somebody could still send a picture of that laborious winter crossing years after Nellie and our other correspondents had been sending a train underneath it.

The tunnel had a determined advocate in Senator George William Howlan. In 1886 he formed a company with a remarkable proposition. It would build the tunnel in return for ownership of the Prince Edward Island Railway and an annual subsidy. Five years later Howlan gave up his Senate seat to contest a Commons election in Prince County. He lost and was reappointed to the Senate. His name went on to appear on the Island’s list of lieutenant governors. His train under the Strait went on to appear on a postcard. Boyde Beck’s biography of Howlan

The Public Archives has another small survivor of Howlan’s campaign, a tunnel stamp dated approximately 1890–91. The archival description mentions a tunnel ticket and a button as well. Years before Bayer gave people a train to hold to the light, advocates had found ways to put the idea into somebody’s pocket or onto a piece of correspondence. Bayer’s credit identifies the publisher of my postcard. It gives me no grounds for making Howlan his customer or the campaign his employer. The Island’s surviving tunnel stamp

What happened to the mail in 1905

I turned up a reckoning for the very boats that were supposed to keep the mail moving. In a submission printed in the Canadian parliamentary papers, the Island legislature urged Ottawa to consider a tunnel. It also asked for a more powerful icebreaking steamer for immediate use. Attached to the argument was a table of the trips the Minto and the Stanley had missed. Against January 24 to March 2, 1905, the clerk put thirty-eight days in a single line. Neither boat had made a trip in that stretch.

The next page counts what mattered to a person waiting for a letter. The little boats crossing between the capes could take only part of what was sent to Tormentine, leaving an average of a hundred bags behind each day. Books, seeds and other heavy mail went by way of Pictou. Here is the federal promise of a year-round mail service translated into objects a reader might actually have been waiting for. The Island’s submission and the two returns, pp. 17–20

Two correspondents gave the Minto a much better introduction than I could. On 0883 the writer covered the sky above the vessel with news for Mr. Simpson in London. She explained that the icebreaking steamers made daily trips. The Charlottetown cancellation is October 5, 1905. Three years later, a man writing from Pictou to his wife in Ontario told her he was there for coal. He picked a picture of the Minto and explained that this was the boat that broke the ice all winter. The correspondents were explaining the service to people away. The parliamentary return was accounting for what happened when the boats couldn’t carry it out. I would keep all three accounts. The card to London · The Pictou letter to Mrs. Taylor

What people wrote on it

On my favourite copy the writer starts up in the narrow margin above the steamer. “Wouldn’t the tunnel be the thing now?” Clifford says the roads are too bad to take the writer up. If they stay that way, the plan for Monday morning is to go out on snowshoes. That is about as good an answer as I can imagine to the question of why someone would buy this particular postcard. We know more about the road that week than we do about the writer’s name.

When I first wrote about 1865, I read its date stamp as 1909. There is a better way to date the design than asking everyone to agree on a faint strike. Another copy, 4069, bears a Charlottetown cancellation of August 5, 1907. Yet another, 4526, went through Souris East that September. The two postmarks put that train in two different Island towns in 1907.

And then there’s Nellie. On October 23, 1908, she wrote to her mother, “I think we will live here in C’town when this tunnel is built.” The card went through the Charlottetown post office the next day on its way to Saint John. I like the word when. Nellie gives us a little glimpse of how the scheme sounded in one family. She goes straight on to tell her mother that both of them are well. Nellie’s card, 3906

The instructions are a poem

At the Capes in the winter you stand on the shore

New Brunswick behind, P.E. Island before,

And you long to get over, but how can you cross?

You are stopped by the wind, and the lolly and frost.

But this is to tell you a way has been planned

By which you can pass to the opposite strand.

So don’t be discouraged, despondent or blue –

Hold this to the light and you’ll see your way through.

The little word that stopped me was “lolly.” Parks Canada, drawing on T. K. Pratt’s Dictionary of Prince Edward Island English, gives it as an Island word for soft ice or snow floating on the water. A nineteenth-century glossary even singled out the PEI iceboat crossing as a place where the word was familiar. Put it back between wind and frost and there is an obstacle very particular to this stretch of water. Island words for ice How are you supposed to get to the other side today? At the bottom of the second column comes the invitation to hold the card up. Even somebody who had no intention of reading an argument for a tunnel had a reason to turn this piece of advertising over.

A different sort of fixed crossing finally opened in 1997, when traffic began using the Confederation Bridge. I still prefer Bayer’s railway for the first spot in a postcard collection. It takes the Island’s winter mail, a senator’s proposal and the sort of conversation Nellie had with her mother and fits the lot inside one envelope-sized joke. It even lets me run the train.
