The history behind the picture

Looking West: The E.J.L. Postcards of Southeastern P.E.I.

The lettering on the Murray Harbour card first caught my eye. Five people pose in a rowboat underneath the words “LOOKING WEST.” Below them the photographer gives us a second view, this time of schooners at the wharf. The little sails at the entrance to Belle River looked familiar too. Turn the two postcards over and a few tiny letters in the bottom left corner put them side by side in more ways than one.

Five consecutive E.J.L. designs

These run from 5237 to 5241. Four are represented here by E.J.L. cards, including three copies of Pinette Bridge. For 5240 the collection has the same pair of photographs in another edition. Select that tile to see the numbered museum copy.

The figures in the corner

On the back of Looking West, just at the end of Lauretta’s message to Gertrude, the printer has squeezed in “E. J. L. 5237.” The Belle River copy gives us the initials, but the portion of the imprint where the number belongs is blank. Another copy survives in Island Archives. Same sails, same line of people on the wharf, same buildings reflected in the water. On its back the complete imprint reads “E. J. L. 5238.” A pupil used that copy in August 1916 to let a teacher in Bonshaw know how the vegetables and flowers in the school garden were coming along. The Belle River card in Island Archives.

The two brown-toned cards give way to coloured views. On the fishing-fleet card the water is green and the roofs on the far bank are red. Look above those roofs and you can see two pencilled crosses. The writer supplied the key in a note to Grandma, explaining that they mark Mr. Prowse’s house and store. A harbour full of fishing boats was the picture for sale. For this family, the important landmarks were across the water. Read the message on the back of 5239.

The museum copy of 5240 closes what would otherwise be a gap. Its front puts Murray Harbour’s waterfront above its railway station. The engine faces the photographer with people posed on and beside it. Compare the gable of the station, the white fence and the people on the locomotive with the photograph reproduced on page 15 of the railway journal The Times. It is the same exposure, reproduced there more fully and credited to Elliot Lumsden, circa 1905, courtesy of the PEI archives. The collection’s colour version carries Carter & Co.’s number 3025, and a sepia copy has a J. E. Evans back. That little train made its way onto at least three different issues. The credited station photograph, the Carter card and the Evans card.

Who was E.J.L.?

The collection’s 2021 Stedman Brothers checklist expands E.J.L. as Elliot J. Lumsden. There is evidence for the identification beyond the initials. Besides the station photograph, Harry Holman reproduces the Pinette Bridge card itself and credits its photograph to Elliot J. Lumsden. The Public Archives titled its exhibition The Photography of J. Elliot Lumsden, reversing the order of the names. It records that he was born in Murray Harbour in 1869 and made his living in part from fox ranching and cranberry farming. He died in 1936. He had good reason to know this stretch of shoreline. Holman’s Pinette history and the Public Archives exhibition.

The checklist groups the E.J.L. numbers with Stedman Brothers of Brantford. The cards themselves identify their place of manufacture as Germany. Pinette is especially good for reading the small print because all three copies have clean backs. The stamp box allows one cent for Canada and the United States and two cents for foreign destinations. The numbers and initials establish the run. The evidence for who took a particular photograph is strongest for the train and for Pinette. Read the Pinette imprint. The other collection copies are 2671 and 0911.

Eight more views with the family resemblance

For someone sorting fronts on a table, these eight eastern Island views deserve a second look. They have the dark or red capital-letter captions, sepia photographs and working waterfronts or rural scenes that drew my eye to 4637. Their backs tell several different publishing stories. I’ve kept them together here for comparison and left their authorship open.

There’s a good example in the view across McLures Mill Ponds. The sender of postcard 4535 had picnicked on those very grounds the previous day. Another printing of the same scene has “U.P.S. Series No. 1684 Montreal” down the back. Likewise, there are two copies of Looking North, Murray River, while Looking North from Dunns Hill is a different photograph altogether. The U.P.S. mill-pond card and the other copy of Looking North.

One last connection turns up in the address rather than the printing. The Marconi station at Cape Bear went to Mr. J. H. Brehaut at 8 Codman Park, Roxbury, Massachusetts. The Murray Harbour card that started the search went to Mrs. J. H. Brehaut at that very address. Two cards that look as if they belong on the same album page once went to the same front door. The Cape Bear address and the address on Looking West.
