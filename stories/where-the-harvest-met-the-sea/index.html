The history behind the picture

Where the Harvest Met the Sea

The railway runs straight into this picture. Follow the rails past the switches, the poles and the people on the wharf, and they deliver you to two steamships. Turn the postcard over and four handwritten words tell you what brought everything together: “Loading Potatoes at Georgetown.”

It is a remarkably good picture of how an Island crop reached the sea. The camera looks out along the wharf from above track level. The ship on the left towers over the small boat at the water’s edge. Across the sheds, the second vessel faces almost directly towards us. Between them hangs a white cloud of steam, softening the hard edges of the buildings and hulls.

Walk out along the rails

Start at the bottom of the photograph. Two tracks cross the timber deck, joined by a crossover that lets a railway vehicle move from one to the other. The switch stands, with their small targets, are part of the machinery of the place. Freight could be moved into position beside the sheds and ships. The railway reached right into the port’s working space.

Then look at the wharf itself. The plank walkway, the exposed timbers down its side and the posts along the water show how little ground separates all this activity from the harbour. A small boat lies beside the left edge, almost swallowed by the scale of the steamer above it. Two figures stand out along the tracks. They make the distance to the ships suddenly measurable.

Look closer at 0986

A dark railway car stands near the far end. The sheds have broad openings facing the tracks, with sloping boards or ramps below them. You can follow a load’s route through the scene: along the railway, across the working wharf, and aboard a ship. The photograph gathers those separate stages into one view.

The crop behind the ships

Potatoes made a demanding cargo. They were bulky, heavy and vulnerable to frost. Getting them off the farm was only the first part of the job. They had to reach the port, be loaded and stowed, and arrive in saleable condition at the other end.

Harry Holman’s account of the potato boats follows the growth of the trade from small schooner cargoes to the larger steamship shipments of the interwar years. He describes the importance of the seed-potato business and its southern markets. He also brings the longshoremen into the picture: loading required large gangs, and careful stowage kept the vessel balanced and the bags secure. Georgetown was one of the Island ports taking part in that trade. Harry Holman, “Spuds, Steamers, and Stevedores”.

The crowds and sacks are much easier to see in a companion card from nearby Montague. Its printed title is “Loading Potatoes at Montague Bridge.” Carts crowd the foreground, sacks rise above their sides, and people work among the boats. Put it beside 0986 and the short Georgetown inscription takes on weight. Those few words stand for a great deal of carrying.

A night’s work at Georgetown

Georgetown’s working day survives unusually clearly in the record of another potato shipment: the Terne’s voyage to Cuba in January 1929. The account appears in a later court judgment. Railway charcoal stoves warmed the holds before loading, and wood and straw helped protect the potatoes from the ship’s cold steel.

On the evening of January 19, two trains brought the remaining cargo. Seventy-six stevedores worked from about seven until two in the morning, loading directly from the railway cars. The ship carried 18,400 bags, each weighing ninety pounds.

The Terne sailed the following morning and became trapped in the ice. Part of the cargo froze. In that one voyage, the tracks, labour and weather all meet: a harvest could reach the wharf and still have a difficult journey ahead. The Georgetown loading account, Dyal Produce Corp. v. Munson S. S. Line (1932), especially paragraphs 32–42.

Four words worth keeping

The front has room for the sweep of the rails and the great dark bows of the ships. The back supplies the crop. Together they preserve a meeting point between the Island’s farms and the people who bought what those farms grew.

Return to the little boat, then to the person walking beside the track. This busy port was made to work at a human scale, one movement and one load at a time. That is what makes 0986 worth lingering over.
