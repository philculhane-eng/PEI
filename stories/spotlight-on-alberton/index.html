The history behind the picture

Spotlight on Alberton

From above, Alberton is a gathering of steep roofs, church spires, small yards and generous stretches of open ground. The postcard mailed in July 1913 gives us that whole arrangement before we start picking out individual buildings. It is a pleasingly spacious town, still close to the country around it.

That was a timely postcard to send: Alberton had been incorporated as a town in May 1913. Its shops and services already drew people from a wider district, and the postcards extend the name Alberton well beyond the shopping street. Harbour views, Kildare’s cliffs and Montrose’s river scenery all joined the album. The town’s official plan’s historical account supplies the incorporation date and places Northport beside it.

A station with substance

The depot is an excellent first stop. Its low, spreading roof sits on walls of rounded stone, with ALBERTON lettered on the slope facing the tracks. People wait along the platform, and the rails lead on past them towards a freight car. The photographer has given the station enough surrounding space to show it as a working part of the town.

Built in 1904–05 to Charles B. Chappell’s design, Alberton’s station was one of the Island’s two distinctive boulder stations. Kensington had the other. The heritage record describes the Alberton building as granite construction. The broad hipped roof and strongly textured walls made an everyday railway stop into a building worth putting on a souvenir.

The buildings people wanted to show

The high school was another favourite. Several publishers issued it, and the collection holds different printings and treatments of the view. Atkinson’s 1905 edition wraps the building in flags and red maple leaves. Look through that bright frame and there is a carefully balanced facade, with a central entrance and people at the door. A school could stand for the town’s confidence as readily as a bank or hotel.

The Albion Terrace Hotel’s RPPC moves closer. Its height fills the card, with leaves framing the upper corners and the veranda partly sheltered by a tree. The photograph invites a slower look at the place where a visitor might stay. Alongside the station and school, it gives the town three different sorts of welcome: arrival, community and accommodation.

Foxes with names

Alberton’s postcards also found an attraction that did not need a street address in large letters across a roof. A silver fox could command the whole picture. In one card the animal is simply associated with the Silver Fox Ranching Company. In another, it has a name: General Harry, owned by Henry Lewis of Alberton.

These are commercial portraits. The animal is shown clearly, the enclosure is part of the setting, and the caption tells a prospective admirer whom to associate with it. On a separate card, Henry Lewis appears with James C. Tuplin under the heading “Two Pioneer Fox Ranchers.” The trade produced an album of its own: animals, owners and premises, all bearing the names of Island communities.

The fox cards add a distinctive note to Alberton’s town portrait. The familiar school and station show public life; these photographs show a specialised local enterprise reaching for customers and recognition farther away. There is more of that wider story in the fox-train article.

A town album with a wide horizon

The harbour cards make a quieter invitation. Atkinson set a small sailing boat within another elaborate souvenir border. “Start for the Fishing Grounds” brings a boat and its crew closer, inside a rustic frame. Both copies were travelling through the mail in 1906. Water and work were part of the Alberton image from the beginning of the postcard boom.

Warwick Brothers & Rutter took the reader farther out. Their Kildare Capes postcard gives us layered cliffs and a long, low horizon. “Beautiful Montrose” follows a tree-lined road over water. The captions attach these outings to Alberton; they help explain the larger countryside a visitor might associate with a stay in town.

Two RPPCs carry the town a little farther into the century. Sent together in July 1924, they show Main Street and Upper Church Street. The writer promises, “Will send a card every day.” Shops, houses, a carriage and a motorcar become the sights of someone’s visit. After the formal station and the advertised foxes, this is an appealing last view of Alberton: a place interesting enough to keep choosing another picture.
